## 1. Definition

**Cervical cancer** is a malignant tumour arising from the epithelium of the cervix, almost always in the **transformation zone**, caused by **persistent infection with high-risk human papillomavirus (HPV)**. It develops slowly (10–20 years) through **pre-cancerous stages (CIN/SIL)** that can be detected and treated cheaply. This makes it **the most preventable cancer**: **vaccinate girls, screen women and treat pre-cancer the same day**.

Yet most Ugandan women still present with **advanced disease** (offensive bloody discharge, pain, fistulae and renal failure) when cure is unlikely. Your job as a clinician is therefore threefold:

1. **Never miss an opportunity to screen** (every woman aged 25/30–49, and every woman living with HIV from 25).
2. **Recognise and stage invasive cancer** quickly, get a biopsy, and refer.
3. **Relieve suffering**: bleeding, pain, odour, fistula and renal failure through good **palliative care**.

> [!REDFLAG]
> **Post-coital bleeding, post-menopausal bleeding, or offensive blood-stained discharge = cervical cancer until proven otherwise.** Look at the cervix and biopsy anything suspicious. A screening test is not enough for a symptomatic woman.

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **HPV** | Double-stranded DNA virus; **high-risk types** (16, 18, 31, 33, 35, 45, 52, 58 …) cause cancer; low-risk types (6, 11) cause genital warts |
| **CIN** (cervical intraepithelial neoplasia) | Histological pre-cancer: **CIN 1** (lower third of epithelium; mostly a transient HPV infection), **CIN 2** (lower two-thirds), **CIN 3** (full thickness, includes carcinoma in situ) |
| **SIL** (squamous intraepithelial lesion, Bethesda/LAST terminology) | **LSIL** = CIN 1 (and HPV effect); **HSIL** = CIN 2 and CIN 3 |
| **AIS** | Adenocarcinoma in situ: glandular pre-cancer, often in the canal, hard to see on VIA |
| **Micro-invasive (stage IA)** | Invasion seen only microscopically, depth <5 mm |
| **Screen-and-treat** | Treatment decided on the basis of a positive screening test alone (no colposcopy or histology) |
| **Screen, triage and treat** | Positive primary test (e.g. HPV) is followed by a second (triage) test (e.g. VIA, colposcopy, cytology) before treatment |
| **Ablation** | Destroys the TZ: **cryotherapy** (freezing) or **thermal ablation** (heat) |
| **Excision** | Removes the TZ for histology: **LEEP/LLETZ**, cold-knife cone |

## 2. Epidemiology

- **Burden**: **cervical cancer is the commonest cancer and the leading cause of cancer death among women in Uganda**. GLOBOCAN 2022: age-standardised incidence about **53.8** and mortality about **40.6 per 100,000** women, more than 10 times the WHO elimination threshold (<4 per 100,000); one of the highest rates in the world. Over 4,000 Ugandan women die of it every year. It accounts for about a third of cancers in Ugandan women.
- **Late presentation**: about **80% of women reaching the Uganda Cancer Institute (UCI)** have advanced disease (stage IIB or worse). Only a small fraction of those prescribed chemoradiation complete it on time (one UCI study found **only 12% compliance** with the full treatment course), because of distance, cost, radiotherapy queues and machine downtime.
- **Screening coverage** remains very low overall (single-digit percentages in several analyses), concentrated at higher-level facilities and HIV clinics, against a national target of about 80% of women aged 30–49.
- **HIV**: Uganda has high HIV prevalence among women; HIV reduces clearance of HPV, increases persistence, multiple-type infection and faster progression. Women living with HIV have about **6 times** the risk. About a quarter of women with cervical cancer at UCI are known to be HIV-positive.
- **Services**: radiotherapy is concentrated at **UCI in Kampala** (with expansion to regional cancer centres such as Gulu and Mbarara for chemotherapy and, increasingly, radiotherapy). Surgery for early disease is done by gynaecological oncologists at Mulago, UCI and some large referral/private-not-for-profit hospitals (Mengo, Nsambya, Lubaga, Mbarara, Gulu).
- **Palliative care**: Uganda is a world pioneer: **oral morphine** is manufactured locally and distributed free through public facilities, and trained nurses and clinical officers can prescribe it.

## 3. Aetiology and Risk Factors

**Cause**: persistent infection with high-risk HPV is **necessary**. **HPV 16 and 18 cause about 70%** of cervical cancers. Think "**HPV exposure + failure to clear it**".

| Risk factor | Why |
|---|---|
| Early sexual debut, multiple partners, partner with many partners | More HPV exposure while the TZ is immature |
| **HIV / immunosuppression** | Failure to clear HPV; faster progression |
| **Smoking** | Carcinogens concentrated in cervical mucus; local immunosuppression |
| High parity, early first pregnancy | Prolonged ectropion/metaplasia during pregnancy; hormonal and trauma effects |
| Long-term COC use (>5 years) | Modest increase; reverses after stopping |
| **Never screened** | The single biggest risk factor for invasive cancer |
| Other STIs (chlamydia, HSV-2) | Co-factors of inflammation |
| Low socio-economic status | Less screening, later presentation |

## 4. Pathophysiology

**How HPV causes cancer**

1. **Infection**: HPV enters **basal cells** of the transformation zone through micro-abrasions during sex. Most infections (about 90%) are **cleared within 1–2 years** by cell-mediated immunity.
2. **Persistence**: in a minority (more with HIV, smoking), high-risk HPV persists.
3. **Integration**: viral DNA integrates into the host genome, typically disrupting the viral **E2** gene. E2 normally represses E6 and E7, so its loss causes **over-expression of E6 and E7**.
4. **Oncoproteins**:
   - **E6** binds the cellular ubiquitin ligase E6AP and targets **p53 for degradation** → no apoptosis, no DNA-damage checkpoint → mutations accumulate. E6 also activates telomerase (immortality).
   - **E7** binds and inactivates **retinoblastoma protein (pRb)** → releases **E2F** → uncontrolled entry into S-phase (proliferation). A surrogate marker of this is **p16 over-expression** used in histology.
5. **Progression**: CIN 1 → CIN 2 → CIN 3 → invasion through the basement membrane (usually **10–20 years**: the window for screening). In women with HIV, progression can be faster.
6. **Local spread**: into the **parametria** (lateral) → **ureters** (hydronephrosis → renal failure), pelvic side wall (sciatic pain, leg oedema); into the **vagina** (down), the **bladder** (anterior) and **rectum** (posterior) → fistulae; into the **uterine body** (up).
7. **Lymphatic spread** (orderly): parametrial → obturator/internal and external iliac → common iliac → **para-aortic** → supraclavicular (Virchow) nodes.
8. **Haematogenous spread** (late): lungs, liver, bone.

> [!PEARL]
> **Why screening works**: the long, detectable pre-invasive phase + a visible, accessible organ + cheap, effective ablation. **Why vaccination works**: the vaccine produces neutralising antibodies that block infection **before** exposure, so it must be given **before sexual debut** (hence 9–14-year-old girls; Uganda targets 10-year-olds).

**Why HIV matters**: HIV depletes CD4 cells and impairs cell-mediated immunity, so HPV persists, multiple types co-exist, lesions are larger, recur more after treatment, and progress faster. **That is why WHO screens women living with HIV earlier (25), more often (every 3–5 years), uses screen–triage–treat, and re-tests at 12 months after treatment.**

**Feature links**

- Fragile, neovascular tumour surface → post-coital, inter-menstrual, contact bleeding.
- Tumour erosion of vessels → heavy, sometimes torrential haemorrhage → anaemia.
- Tumour necrosis and anaerobic infection → offensive, watery, blood-stained discharge.
- Parametrial and side-wall spread → back pain, sciatic pain, leg oedema.
- Ureteric compression → hydronephrosis → oliguria, uraemia (vomiting, hiccups, itching, confusion, acidotic breathing).
- Bladder or rectal invasion (or radiation necrosis) → haematuria, rectal bleeding, tenesmus, VVF/RVF.
- Canal occluded by tumour → pyometra, fever.
- Pelvic node and vein involvement + hypercoagulability → lymphoedema, DVT/PE.
- Para-aortic → supraclavicular spread → left supraclavicular node.
- Haematogenous spread → cough (lung), jaundice/hepatomegaly (liver), bone pain.
- Hypoxic (anaemic) tumour → radioresistance → Hb kept ≥10 g/dL.

## 5. Classification / Staging

**Histological types**: **squamous cell carcinoma (about 75–80%)**, **adenocarcinoma (about 15–20%**, increasing; HPV 18-associated; can hide in the canal), adenosquamous, and rare neuroendocrine (small cell) tumours.

**FIGO 2018 staging (with 2019 corrigendum)**

| Stage | Description |
|---|---|
| **I** | Confined to the cervix (extension to the uterine body disregarded) |
| IA | Invasive carcinoma diagnosed only by microscopy, maximum depth of invasion **<5 mm** |
| IA1 | Stromal invasion **<3 mm** deep |
| IA2 | Stromal invasion **≥3 mm and <5 mm** |
| IB | Invasion ≥5 mm (larger than IA), confined to the cervix |
| IB1 | Tumour **<2 cm** in greatest dimension |
| IB2 | Tumour **≥2 cm and <4 cm** |
| IB3 | Tumour **≥4 cm** |
| **II** | Beyond the uterus, but not to the lower third of the vagina or the pelvic wall |
| IIA | Upper two-thirds of vagina, **no parametrial** involvement (IIA1 <4 cm; IIA2 ≥4 cm) |
| IIB | **Parametrial** involvement, not reaching the pelvic wall |
| **III** | Lower third of vagina and/or pelvic wall and/or hydronephrosis/non-functioning kidney and/or pelvic/para-aortic nodes |
| IIIA | **Lower third of the vagina**, no pelvic wall extension |
| IIIB | **Pelvic side wall** and/or **hydronephrosis or non-functioning kidney** (unless known to be from another cause) |
| IIIC1 | **Pelvic** lymph node metastasis (r = imaging, p = pathology) |
| IIIC2 | **Para-aortic** lymph node metastasis |
| **IV** | Beyond the true pelvis or involving bladder/rectal mucosa |
| IVA | Spread to **mucosa of bladder or rectum** (biopsy-proven; **bullous oedema alone is not enough**) |
| IVB | **Distant** organs (lung, liver, bone, supraclavicular/inguinal nodes, peritoneum) |

> [!EXAM]
> Changes in FIGO 2018: IB split into three by size (2 cm and 4 cm cut-offs); the **horizontal-spread criterion was removed** from stage IA; **lymph node involvement now upstages to IIIC** (imaging or pathology may be used, and pathology takes priority); micrometastases count as IIIC (2019 corrigendum), isolated tumour cells do not change the stage.

> [!PEARL]
> When in doubt between two stages, **FIGO says assign the lower stage**. And remember the two traps: **hydronephrosis = IIIB regardless of tumour size**, and **bullous oedema of the bladder is not IVA**.

**Performance status (ECOG)**: 0–2 can usually tolerate chemoradiation; 3–4 → often palliative intent.

## 6. Clinical Features

### History

| Ask | Why: the answer that changes the plan |
|---|---|
| **Abnormal bleeding**: post-coital, inter-menstrual, post-menopausal, contact bleeding; how long? | The classic early symptoms of invasive cancer; long duration + many symptoms usually means a bigger stage |
| **Vaginal discharge**: offensive, watery, blood-stained | Necrotic tumour: advanced disease; you will need metronidazole and a gentle examination |
| **Pain**: pelvic, low back, sciatic radiating down the leg | Parametrial/pelvic wall or nerve involvement → suspect **IIIB** |
| **Leg swelling** | Lymphatic/venous obstruction by pelvic wall disease; DVT |
| **Urine or faeces per vagina**, haematuria, rectal bleeding, tenesmus | **Vesicovaginal or rectovaginal fistula** or bladder/rectal invasion (stage **IVA** or post-radiation) → **cystoscopy/proctoscopy** |
| **Reduced urine output, vomiting, hiccups, itching, confusion** | **Uraemia** from bilateral ureteric obstruction (IIIB), the commonest cause of death → urgent creatinine and renal ultrasound; cisplatin may be impossible |
| **Weight loss, anorexia, cough, bone pain, jaundice, abdominal swelling** | Distant spread (**IVB**) → CXR, abdominal ultrasound; palliative intent |
| **Ability to self-care (ECOG)** | ECOG 0–2 can usually tolerate chemoradiation; ECOG 3–4 → often palliative intent |
| **Screening history** (VIA/HPV/Pap, treatment received) | Never screened is the major risk factor; previous ablation/LEEP matters; a previous benign biopsy does not exclude cancer now |
| **HIV status, ART regimen, adherence, last viral load and CD4** | Risk, prognosis, tolerance of chemoradiation, drug interactions, need to optimise ART before treatment |
| Parity, age at first intercourse, number of partners, smoking | Risk factors |
| **Pregnant? Desire for future fertility?** | Pregnancy changes everything; fertility wish matters in stage IA–IB1 (cone, radical trachelectomy) |
| **Comorbidity** (diabetes, hypertension, heart disease, TB) | Fitness for radical surgery or cisplatin |
| **Social: distance to Kampala, money, caregiver, what she knows and wants** | Whether she can complete **5–7 weeks** of daily chemoradiation within **56 days** at UCI; early link to palliative care and social support |

### Examination

Follow the order of Hutchison's Clinical Methods (24th edition). In a heavily bleeding or uraemic woman, resuscitate first (section 9) and do the pelvic examination in theatre.

#### Introduction, consent and preparation

- Introduce yourself, confirm identity, explain, gain consent; **chaperone** for the pelvic examination; be gentle (friable tumour).
- **Hand hygiene**.
- **Position and exposure**: supine for the abdomen; lithotomy or dorsal with good light for the pelvis; keep her covered.

#### General inspection and vital signs

- **Pallor** (anaemia from bleeding), cachexia, jaundice, **performance status (ECOG)**, signs of **uraemia** (acidotic breathing, uraemic fetor, drowsiness).
- **Vitals**: pulse, BP, shock index if bleeding, temperature, RR.

#### Hands, face and neck

- Palmar and conjunctival pallor; **left supraclavicular (Virchow) lymph nodes** (IVB).

#### Chest

- Effusion, crackles (lung metastases).

#### Abdomen and groins

- Hepatomegaly, ascites, **ballotable/palpable kidneys** (hydronephrosis), pelvic mass (pyometra, bulky tumour), **inguinal nodes** (IVB).
- **Leg oedema** (unilateral: pelvic wall or DVT).

#### Pelvic examination

1. **Speculum**: describe the lesion: **exophytic** (cauliflower), **ulcerative** (crater with raised everted edges) or **endophytic** (hard barrel-shaped cervix, often adenocarcinoma); estimate **size in cm**; **vaginal extension**: none / upper two-thirds / **lower third**; contact bleeding, necrosis, offensive discharge.
2. **Biopsy** now if a lesion is visible: punch biopsy **from the edge** (viable tumour), not the necrotic centre; pack or apply Monsel's for haemostasis.
3. **Bimanual**: size and hardness of the cervix, mobility of the uterus, vaginal fornix involvement.
4. **Rectovaginal examination (index finger in the vagina, middle finger in the rectum)**: essential for staging. Feel each **parametrium** between the cervix and the pelvic side wall:
   - **Soft and free** → no parametrial involvement (stage I or IIA).
   - **Thickened, nodular, but a gap remains** between the thickening and the side wall → **IIB**.
   - **Thickened out to the side wall**, no free space ("frozen pelvis") → **IIIB**.
   - Check the **rectal mucosa** for tethering or ulceration (IVA needs biopsy).

#### Completing the examination

Thank her, cover her, hand hygiene, summarise.

**Document**: tumour size, vaginal extent, each parametrium (right and left separately), rectal mucosa, nodes, kidneys; e.g. "4 cm exophytic growth, upper vagina involved anteriorly, right parametrium thickened not reaching side wall, left free, rectal mucosa free".

> [!EXAM]
> **Clinical FIGO staging exam, as done in Uganda**: FIGO staging is still largely **clinical** in low-resource settings. **EUA** (examination under anaesthesia, ideally a gynae-oncologist and a radiation oncologist together) with **speculum, bimanual and rectovaginal examination**, **biopsy**, **cystoscopy** (and **proctoscopy/sigmoidoscopy**) if bladder or rectal involvement is suspected, plus assessment of the ureters by **renal ultrasound or IVU** (hydronephrosis or a non-functioning kidney = **IIIB**) and a **chest X-ray**. FIGO 2018 allows CT/MRI/PET and pathology to modify the stage where available, but you must always be able to stage with your hands.

### Red flags

> [!REDFLAG]
> - **Heavy vaginal bleeding** from the tumour → resuscitate, pack, tranexamic acid, urgent haemostatic radiotherapy.
> - **Oliguria/anuria, rising creatinine** → obstructive uropathy (stage IIIB); needs ultrasound and discussion about nephrostomy/stent vs palliation.
> - **Fever with foul discharge** → infected necrotic tumour or pyometra.
> - **Leg swelling with pain** → DVT (cancer is prothrombotic) or pelvic wall disease.
> - **Uncontrolled pain** → palliative emergency; start oral morphine.
> - **Suicidal ideation/depression**, abandonment by partner, stigma: ask gently.

> [!REDFLAG]
> Call a senior at once for: active heavy bleeding or shock index ≥0.9; anuria/oliguria or confusion (obstructive uropathy); fever with hypotension; or suspected spinal cord compression (new leg weakness, loss of bladder/bowel control).

### Thinking like a clinician

- **Symptomatic women are not screened: they are diagnosed.** A woman with PCB and a VIA-negative cervix still needs a careful look and, if bleeding persists, colposcopy and biopsy. Adenocarcinoma can sit in the canal, invisible on VIA.
- **Biopsy the edge**, not the necrotic centre: take a **punch biopsy from the margin** of the growth, where viable tumour meets normal tissue. Necrotic tissue gives "necrosis: no viable tumour".
- **Do not ablate what might be cancer.** Ablation destroys the tissue that would have given the diagnosis and under-treats invasive disease: suspicious lesions go for biopsy.
- **Stage the patient, not the scan**: FIGO 2018 allows imaging and pathology, but most Ugandan women are staged by examination ± ultrasound of the kidneys (hydronephrosis alone = IIIB) and a chest X-ray.
- **Match treatment to stage and to the patient**: a 35-year-old with a 1.5 cm tumour and good performance status can be cured by surgery; a 60-year-old with IIIB disease and creatinine 600 µmol/L may be best served by symptom control. Ask the patient what she understands and wants.
- **Always think about the kidneys.** Check urea, creatinine and renal ultrasound before chemoradiation: cisplatin is nephrotoxic and cannot be given if creatinine clearance is poor.
- **Cancer in pregnancy** (not rare in Uganda): management depends on stage and gestation; discuss at a multidisciplinary level; delivery is by **caesarean section** (vaginal delivery through a tumour risks haemorrhage and tumour implantation in the episiotomy).
- **Common student mistakes**: calling an ulcerated cervix "cervicitis" and giving antibiotics; forgetting the rectal examination; forgetting renal function; staging hydronephrosis as stage II; offering cryotherapy to a lesion that looks invasive; forgetting palliative care and pain relief.

## 7. Differential Diagnoses

| Differential | Distinguishing clinical feature | Distinguishing investigation |
|---|---|---|
| **Cervical cancer** | Hard, friable, exophytic or ulcerated growth; contact bleeding; parametrial thickening | **Punch biopsy** |
| Cervical ectropion | Flat, regular, symmetrical red area around os; young/pregnant/COC | VIA/HPV negative; reassurance |
| Cervicitis | Mucopus, oedema, friability; resolves with antibiotics | NAAT/culture; re-examine after treatment |
| Cervical polyp | Smooth pedunculated mass, stalk visible | Avulsion + histology |
| Prolapsed submucous fibroid | Firm, pale, smooth mass with stalk through os | Ultrasound; EUA |
| Endometrial cancer extending to cervix | Post-menopausal, obese, diabetic; bulky uterus | Endometrial biopsy; histology (adenocarcinoma type) |
| Tuberculous / schistosomal cervicitis | Ulcerative or granular lesions; endemic areas; infertility | Biopsy (granulomas, ova), AFB/GeneXpert |
| Chancroid / syphilitic chancre / herpes on cervix | Painful (chancroid, HSV) or painless (syphilis) ulcer; partner history | RPR/TPHA, swab; biopsy if not healing |
| Condylomata acuminata | Warty lesions (HPV 6/11), often multiple, vulval too | Biopsy if atypical, especially in HIV |
| Products of conception at the os | Positive hCG, recent pregnancy, open os | hCG, ultrasound |

## 8. Clinical Workup

The workup of a woman with suspected cervical cancer has three jobs, done in this order: **(1) keep her alive today** (bleeding, uraemia, sepsis; section 9), **(2) prove the diagnosis** (biopsy) and **(3) stage her and check her fitness** so that the multidisciplinary team at UCI/Mulago can choose surgery, chemoradiation or palliation. At HC IV or a general hospital you can do almost all of this before referral, and doing it well saves her weeks.

### Bedside

|---|---|---|
| **Urine pregnancy test** | Pregnancy changes the whole plan (MDT, timing, caesarean delivery) | All levels |
| **HemoCue / Hb** | Anaemia is almost universal; **Hb <7 g/dL** → transfuse; target **Hb ≥10 g/dL** during radiotherapy | HC III and above |
| **Urine dipstick** | Haematuria (bladder invasion or radiation cystitis), UTI | All levels |
| **Urine output** (catheter if unwell) | Oliguria → obstructive uropathy | All levels |
| **Random blood sugar** | Diabetes (steroids with chemotherapy, surgical risk) | HC III and above |
| **Rapid HIV test** | If status unknown; HIV changes treatment tolerance and ART planning | All levels |
| **Point-of-care ultrasound** of the kidneys | **Hydronephrosis in minutes**: if present (and no other cause), the stage is **at least IIIB** | HC IV and above with an operator |
| **VIA** (only if no obvious growth) | A frank growth needs a **biopsy**, not VIA; VIA is for women without a visible lesion | HC III (trained) and above |

**Screening and diagnosis of pre-cancer**

| Test | How / what you look for | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **VIA** | Apply **3–5% acetic acid**, wait **1 minute**; **positive** = dense, well-defined **acetowhite** lesion touching/adjacent to the SCJ; **suspicious for cancer** = growth/ulcer | Cheap, immediate, allows same-visit treatment; subjective; poor after menopause (SCJ inside canal) |
| **HPV DNA test** (clinician- or **self-collected** vaginal sample; GeneXpert/careHPV) | High-risk HPV positive/negative (± genotype 16/18) | **WHO preferred primary test**; very high negative predictive value → long intervals |
| **Cytology (Pap/LBC)** | Dyskaryosis (ASC-US, LSIL, HSIL) | Needs lab and pathologist; used mostly in private/tertiary settings |
| **Colposcopy** | Magnified view: acetowhite, mosaicism, punctation, atypical vessels; guides biopsy | Referral hospitals |
| **Punch biopsy** | Histology: CIN grade or invasion | Required for any suspected cancer |
| **LEEP/cone specimen** | Histology of the whole TZ, margins | Diagnostic and therapeutic |

|---|---|---|---|---|
| **FBC** | All | Anaemia; high WBC (infection); low platelets | Transfuse to **Hb ≥10 g/dL** for radiotherapy (hypoxic tumour is radioresistant); chemotherapy needs adequate counts | HC IV and above |
| **Urea, creatinine, electrolytes (± eGFR/creatinine clearance)** | All, and **weekly during cisplatin** | Raised creatinine = obstructive uropathy (IIIB) or other renal disease; low Mg/K on cisplatin | **Cisplatin is withheld if creatinine clearance is below about 50 mL/min** (carboplatin may be substituted); **drain the kidneys first** (nephrostomy) if she is for curative treatment | HC IV (some), general hospitals and above |
| **HIV test, CD4, viral load** | All (known positive: CD4 and VL) | Unsuppressed VL, low CD4 | Optimise ART before and during chemoradiation; co-trimoxazole prophylaxis per HIV guidelines | HIV clinics; CD4/VL via hub samples |
| **LFTs, RBS, hepatitis B** | Before chemotherapy/surgery | Raised enzymes, diabetes | Fitness, drug choice | General hospital and above |
| **Group and cross-match** | Anaemic, bleeding, or before surgery | Compatible units | Blood ready for transfusion/surgery | HC IV and above |

### Imaging

| Test | When | Expected / abnormal finding | How it changes management | Available at |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Renal ultrasound** | All | **Hydronephrosis** (uni- or bilateral), non-functioning kidney | **Upstages to IIIB** (unless another cause); nephrostomy discussion | Most general hospitals and many HC IVs |
| **Chest X-ray** | All | Lung metastases, effusion | **IVB** → palliative intent | Widely available |
| **Abdominal ultrasound** | All | Liver metastases, ascites, para-aortic nodes | IVB / IIIC2 suspicion | General hospital and above |
| **IVU (intravenous urogram)** | Where ultrasound is inconclusive and renal function allows | Ureteric obstruction, non-functioning kidney | Same staging meaning as hydronephrosis (IIIB); contrast is risky if creatinine is high | Regional/national referral |
| **MRI pelvis** | Where available, especially if surgery is planned | Tumour size, parametrial invasion, nodes | Best for size and parametrium; separates IB2 (surgery) from IIB (chemoradiation) | UCI, Mulago, private centres |
| **CT chest/abdomen/pelvis** | Locally advanced disease, before radiotherapy planning | Pelvic nodes (**IIIC1r**), para-aortic nodes (**IIIC2r**), distant metastases | Extended-field radiotherapy for para-aortic nodes; IVB → palliation | UCI, Mulago, regional centres, private |

### Specialised / referral-level

| Test | When | Expected / abnormal finding | How it changes management | Available at |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Punch biopsy (histology)** | Every visible or suspicious lesion, **before any treatment** | Squamous cell carcinoma (about 75–80%), adenocarcinoma (about 15–20%), other | No treatment without histology | Biopsy at HC IV/general hospital; histology at regional/national referral labs (Mulago, UCI, Mbarara, Gulu, Lacor and others); turnaround often 1–3 weeks, so send early |
| **EUA with cystoscopy ± proctoscopy** | Locally advanced disease, urinary or rectal symptoms, or clinical staging needed | Bladder/rectal **mucosal** tumour (biopsy-proven) | **IVA** (bullous oedema alone does **not** make IVA) | Theatre at general/regional/national referral hospitals |
| **Colposcopy ± LEEP** | Persistent PCB with negative VIA; suspected glandular lesion; not eligible for ablation | CIN grade, invasion, margins | Diagnosis and treatment of pre-cancer | Referral hospitals |

> [!UGANDA]
> At a **general hospital or HC IV** you can do the core staging set before referral: **biopsy, Hb, creatinine, renal ultrasound, CXR and HIV status**. Sending her to UCI with these results (and the histology report) avoids repeat tests and speeds treatment. MRI/CT, cystoscopy under EUA and radiotherapy planning are done at **UCI/Mulago** and a few regional cancer centres.

### Putting it together

**Model one-line summary**: "A 48-year-old P6, HIV-positive on TLD with suppressed viral load, never screened, with 5 months of post-coital bleeding and offensive discharge; a 4.5 cm exophytic cervical growth involving the upper vagina, right parametrium thickened not reaching the side wall, left free; Hb 8.9 g/dL, creatinine 82 µmol/L, kidneys normal on ultrasound, CXR clear; biopsy: squamous cell carcinoma."

**Problem list**

1. Invasive squamous cell carcinoma of the cervix, **FIGO IIB** (clinical).
2. Anaemia (Hb 8.9 g/dL): below the radiotherapy target.
3. HIV on ART (suppressed): continue ART; check CD4.
4. Offensive discharge and pain: symptom control.
5. Social: distance to UCI, money, caregiver.

**Working diagnosis and plan**: cervical cancer FIGO stage IIB → refer to UCI for **concurrent chemoradiation** (EBRT 45–50 Gy in 25 fractions + weekly cisplatin 40 mg/m² + brachytherapy, total ≤56 days); transfuse to Hb ≥10; metronidazole for odour; analgesia; link to palliative care and counselling.

**Model case presentation (ward round)**

> "Mrs K is a 52-year-old P7, HIV-positive on TLD with suppressed viral load, never screened, who presents with 6 months of post-coital and then continuous offensive blood-stained vaginal discharge, left sciatic pain and weight loss. She is pale (Hb 7.4 g/dL). Speculum shows a 5 cm fungating, friable exophytic growth on the cervix extending onto the upper vagina; rectovaginal examination shows the left parametrium thickened and fixed to the pelvic side wall. Renal ultrasound shows left hydronephrosis; creatinine is 110 µmol/L; CXR is clear. Punch biopsy: moderately differentiated squamous cell carcinoma. My working diagnosis is cervical cancer FIGO stage IIIB (pelvic wall and hydronephrosis). Plan: transfuse to Hb ≥10, refer urgently to UCI for concurrent chemoradiation (EBRT 45–50 Gy with weekly cisplatin 40 mg/m², renal function permitting, followed by brachytherapy), start oral morphine for pain with a laxative, metronidazole for odour, link to palliative care, and counsel her and her family."

**Worked example 1: staging from the examination**

> A 42-year-old: 3 cm exophytic tumour confined to the cervix; vagina free; both parametria soft on rectovaginal examination; kidneys normal on ultrasound; CXR clear.

| Finding | Stage reasoning |
|---|---|
| Visible tumour, 3 cm, confined to the cervix | Stage IB (visible = at least IB); **≥2 and <4 cm = IB2** |
| Parametria free, vagina free | Not II |
| No hydronephrosis, CXR clear | Not IIIB/IVB |

- **Stage IB2** → options: **radical hysterectomy + pelvic lymphadenectomy** (open approach) or **primary chemoradiation**. If MRI/CT or surgery later shows **positive pelvic nodes** she becomes **IIIC1r** (imaging) or **IIIC1p** (pathology) and needs chemoradiation.

**Worked example 2: how one finding changes the stage**

| Same 4 cm tumour, plus... | FIGO 2018 stage | Treatment intent |
|---|---|---|
| Nothing else, confined to the cervix | **IB3** (≥4 cm) | Chemoradiation (preferred) |
| Upper-vaginal involvement, parametria free | **IIA2** | Chemoradiation |
| Right parametrium thickened, **gap** to side wall | **IIB** | Chemoradiation |
| Tumour reaches the **lower third of the vagina** | **IIIA** | Chemoradiation |
| Parametrium **fixed to the side wall**, or **hydronephrosis** on ultrasound (even with free parametria) | **IIIB** | Chemoradiation if kidneys can be drained and renal function allows; otherwise palliation |
| Enlarged **pelvic nodes** on CT | **IIIC1r** | Chemoradiation |
| Enlarged **para-aortic nodes** on CT | **IIIC2r** | Extended-field chemoradiation |
| Biopsy-proven **bladder mucosa** tumour at cystoscopy | **IVA** | Chemoradiation (fistula risk) or palliation |
| Lung or liver metastases, supraclavicular node | **IVB** | Palliative chemotherapy/RT and supportive care |

**Worked example 3: can she have cisplatin?**

> A 55-year-old, weight 50 kg, creatinine 150 µmol/L, bilateral hydronephrosis.

- **Cockcroft–Gault** (women): creatinine clearance ≈ (140 − age) × weight (kg) × 1.04 ÷ creatinine (µmol/L) = 85 × 50 × 1.04 ÷ 150 ≈ **29 mL/min**.
- Below about 50 mL/min → **no cisplatin** as it stands. Stage is **IIIB** (hydronephrosis). Options: **nephrostomy** then re-check renal function and proceed with chemoradiation, or radiotherapy alone/carboplatin per the oncologist, or palliation if frail and that is her wish. Exact cut-offs vary between oncology units.

## 9. Management

### Immediate / emergency

**First 5 minutes (ABCDE)**

1. **A, B, C and vitals**: pulse, BP, **shock index** (HR ÷ SBP; **≥0.9** abnormal, **≥1.4** needs urgent intervention), RR, temperature, level of consciousness.
2. **Is she bleeding heavily?** (pads soaked, clots, pallor, faintness). If yes: **call for help**, **two large-bore IV cannulae**, blood for **Hb and group and cross-match**, crystalloid, **tranexamic acid 1 g IV**, and a **tight vaginal pack** with a urinary catheter (algorithm below). Transfuse as needed.
3. **D: is she uraemic?** Drowsy or confused, acidotic (deep sighing) breathing, vomiting, hiccups, itching, little urine → catheterise and measure output, send **urea, creatinine and potassium urgently**, and do a **renal ultrasound** today. Hyperkalaemia and fluid overload can kill before the cancer does.
4. **E: is she septic?** Fever ≥38.0 °C with foul discharge (infected necrotic tumour, pyometra) or a urinary source → cultures if possible and IV antibiotics (e.g. ceftriaxone + metronidazole) per local protocol.
5. **Is she in severe pain?** Start analgesia now (paracetamol, then oral morphine 2.5–5 mg every 4 hours, lower/less often in renal failure); do not make her wait for the diagnosis.
6. **Pregnancy test** in every woman of reproductive age.

**Heavy bleeding from cervical cancer (algorithm)**

1. **Call for help**, ABC, **two large-bore IV cannulae**, bloods for Hb and **group and cross-match**, start crystalloid; **transfuse** as needed.
2. **Tranexamic acid 1 g IV** 8-hourly (then 1 g PO 8-hourly).
3. **Tight vaginal packing** with gauze (soaked in saline, or in Monsel's solution/adrenaline where available); insert a **urinary catheter** (packing obstructs the urethra). Leave pack 24–48 h; give antibiotics (e.g. metronidazole) to reduce infection and odour.
4. **Urgent haemostatic radiotherapy** (short hypofractionated course, e.g. a single large fraction): refer to UCI.
5. If bleeding continues and facilities exist: **uterine/internal iliac artery embolisation or ligation**.
6. Once stable: correct anaemia, stage, plan definitive or palliative treatment.

**Obstructive uropathy (renal failure)**

- Bilateral hydronephrosis with rising creatinine = **stage IIIB**, the **commonest cause of death** in cervical cancer.
- If she is a candidate for curative chemoradiation or wants active treatment: **percutaneous nephrostomy** (interventional radiology) or ureteric stenting (often impossible because of tumour), then treatment; dialysis is rarely feasible.
- If disease is advanced and she is frail, many women choose **palliation**: uraemia may be a peaceful death if pain, nausea and itching are controlled. This must be a **shared, honest decision with the patient and family**.

### Definitive

> [!NOTE]
> Treatment is decided by a **multidisciplinary team** (gynae-oncologist, radiation and medical oncologist, pathologist, radiologist, palliative care), in Uganda usually at **UCI/Mulago**. Your role at HC IV or a general hospital: **suspect, biopsy, basic staging (renal US, creatinine, CXR, Hb), refer promptly, and start palliation of symptoms**.

#### A. Pre-cancer: the screen-and-treat visit, step by step (HC IV / HIV clinic)

1. **Counsel and consent**: explain HPV, the test, what a positive result means and the treatment options; offer HIV testing.
2. **Take the sample** (HPV self/clinician sample) or perform **VIA**: speculum, identify the SCJ, apply 3–5% acetic acid, wait 1 minute, inspect.
3. **Classify**:
   - **VIA/HPV negative** → re-screen at the recommended interval (section 12).
   - **HPV positive** → **triage with VIA** (especially in WLWH) and assess eligibility for ablation.
   - **VIA positive**, lesion **eligible for ablation** → treat the same day.
   - **Not eligible for ablation** → **LEEP** (referral hospital).
   - **Suspicious for cancer** → **biopsy** and refer: do **not** ablate.
4. **Eligibility for ablation**: the whole lesion and the **SCJ fully visible** (TZ type 1 or 2), lesion covers **<75%** of the ectocervix, does **not extend into the canal** or onto the vagina, and there is **no suspicion of invasive or glandular disease**. Not during pregnancy (defer to 6 weeks postpartum; treat only if cancer is suspected, then biopsy, not ablation).
5. **Treat**:
   - **Thermal ablation**: reusable probe heated to **at least 100 °C** applied for **20–30 seconds** per application, overlapping to cover the whole TZ; takes minutes, needs only electricity/battery, no gas; WHO-preferred for scale-up.
   - **Cryotherapy**: CO₂ or N₂O gas; **double-freeze technique: 3 minutes freeze, 5 minutes thaw, 3 minutes freeze**, ensuring an ice-ball extends 4–5 mm beyond the lesion.
   - **LEEP/LLETZ**: under local anaesthesia (lignocaine with adrenaline), excise the TZ with a wire loop; gives a histology specimen; haemostasis with ball diathermy/Monsel's.
6. **After-care advice**: watery discharge for 2–4 weeks; **no intercourse or tampons for 4 weeks** (or condoms); return for heavy bleeding, fever, offensive discharge or severe pain.
7. **Follow-up**: re-screen at **12 months**; HIV-positive women continue shorter intervals.

> [!PEARL]
> **Why ablation is enough for CIN**: CIN is confined to the epithelium and the crypts (up to about 5 mm deep). Ablation destroys to that depth, so the diseased TZ is replaced by healthy squamous epithelium. It cannot treat invasion, which is why suspected cancer must be excluded first.

#### B. CIN found on histology (referral level)

| Finding | Management |
|---|---|
| **CIN 1** | Usually regresses: **follow-up** (repeat test at 12 months); treat if persistent ≥2 years or in WLWH per local policy |
| **CIN 2–3** | **Treat**: LEEP (preferred when available, gives margins) or ablation if eligible |
| **AIS** | **Excision** (cone/LEEP with clear margins), not ablation; hysterectomy when family complete |
| **Pregnancy with CIN** | Colposcopy to exclude invasion; **defer treatment** until ≥6 weeks postpartum |

#### C. Invasive cancer by stage

| Stage | Standard treatment | Notes / fertility-sparing |
|---|---|---|
| **IA1** (no LVSI) | **Cone biopsy/LEEP with clear margins** (fertility) or **simple (extrafascial) hysterectomy** | Node risk <1% |
| **IA1 with LVSI, IA2** | **Modified radical hysterectomy + pelvic lymphadenectomy** (or cone with clear margins + pelvic nodes if fertility desired) | Radical trachelectomy in selected young women |
| **IB1, IB2, IIA1** | **Radical (Wertheim-type) hysterectomy + bilateral pelvic lymphadenectomy** **or** primary **chemoradiation** (equal cure rates; surgery preserves ovarian function and vaginal elasticity in young women) | Radical trachelectomy for IB1 (≤2 cm) wanting fertility; open approach preferred (LACC trial showed worse survival with minimally invasive radical hysterectomy) |
| **IB3, IIA2** | **Concurrent chemoradiation** (preferred) | Avoid surgery + radiation "double treatment" |
| **IIB, III (A, B, C1), IVA** | **Concurrent chemoradiation**: EBRT + weekly cisplatin + brachytherapy | Extended-field RT if para-aortic nodes (IIIC2) |
| **IVB** | **Palliative systemic chemotherapy** (carboplatin/cisplatin + paclitaxel) and/or **palliative RT** to symptomatic sites; **best supportive care** | Performance status and wishes guide intensity |

**Radical hysterectomy (Wertheim/Meigs; Querleu-Morrow type C)** removes the uterus, cervix, **parametria**, **upper third (about 2 cm) of the vagina** and pelvic lymph nodes; the **ovaries can be conserved** in young women with squamous cancer. The ureters are dissected out of their tunnels: risks include **ureteric injury/fistula**, **bladder dysfunction** (autonomic nerve damage), lymphocyst and lymphoedema.

**Adjuvant treatment after radical hysterectomy**

- **High-risk** (Peters criteria): **positive nodes, positive parametria or positive margins** → **chemoradiation**.
- **Intermediate-risk** (Sedlis criteria: combinations of LVSI, deep stromal invasion, tumour size) → **pelvic radiotherapy**.

**Chemoradiation: what it involves (UCI practice guideline)**

| Component | Regimen | Why |
|---|---|---|
| **External beam RT (EBRT)** | **45–50 Gy in 25 fractions** (daily, Monday–Friday, 5 weeks) to the pelvis | Treats tumour, parametria and pelvic nodes |
| **Concurrent cisplatin** | **40 mg/m² IV weekly × 5–6 cycles** (with pre-hydration and antiemetics); check creatinine each week | **Radiosensitiser**: improves survival by about 10% over RT alone |
| **Brachytherapy** (HDR) | e.g. **24–28 Gy in 3–4 fractions** to the tumour (intracavitary tandem and ovoids/ring ± interstitial needles) | Delivers a very high dose to the cervix with rapid fall-off, sparing bladder and rectum |
| **Overall treatment time** | **≤56 days (8 weeks)** | Every day of delay beyond this lets tumour cells repopulate and reduces local control |
| **Supportive** | Keep **Hb ≥10 g/dL**, nutrition, ART, antiemetics, manage diarrhoea/cystitis | Anaemia → hypoxic tumour → radioresistance |

#### D. Palliative care: the reality for most Ugandan patients

| Symptom | Management |
|---|---|
| **Pain** | WHO analgesic ladder: **paracetamol 1 g PO 6–8-hourly** ± NSAID (e.g. ibuprofen 400 mg 8-hourly if renal function allows) → **oral morphine** (immediate-release solution) starting **2.5–5 mg every 4 hours** with the same dose for breakthrough, titrated daily; **always prescribe a laxative** (e.g. bisacodyl) with morphine; reduce dose/frequency in renal failure. Neuropathic pain (sciatic): add **amitriptyline 12.5–25 mg nocte**. Bone pain: palliative RT (single 8 Gy fraction) |
| **Bleeding** | Tranexamic acid 1 g PO 8-hourly; vaginal packing; **palliative/haemostatic RT** |
| **Offensive discharge** | **Metronidazole 400 mg PO 8-hourly** (or crushed metronidazole tablets applied topically/in vaginal packs), vaginal douching with saline, dressings, charcoal |
| **Fistula** (VVF/RVF) | Pads, barrier creams (zinc oxide), catheter; urinary diversion or colostomy in selected patients |
| **Leg lymphoedema** | Elevation, compression, skin care; treat cellulitis |
| **Nausea (uraemia/opioids)** | Haloperidol 1.5–3 mg nocte or metoclopramide 10 mg 8-hourly |
| **Psychosocial and spiritual** | Explain diagnosis honestly, involve family, address stigma and abandonment, link to **hospice** (e.g. Hospice Africa Uganda, Mulago palliative care unit) and home-based care |

> [!UGANDA]
> In Uganda, **oral liquid morphine is available free** in public facilities, and specially trained **nurses and clinical officers can prescribe it**. Fear of addiction is common among patients and staff: reassure that morphine for cancer pain, correctly titrated, is safe. Every woman diagnosed with advanced cervical cancer should be **linked to palliative care on the day of diagnosis**, in parallel with any oncological treatment.

### Monitoring and follow-up

- **During chemoradiation**: creatinine and electrolytes (Mg, K) weekly, FBC, hearing, emesis; keep **Hb ≥10 g/dL**; ART adherence; complete within **56 days**.
- **After treatment of pre-cancer**: re-test at **12 months**; WLWH continue shorter intervals.
- **After cancer treatment**: every **3–6 months for 2 years**, then 6–12-monthly to 5 years: symptoms, speculum and rectovaginal examination; imaging only if symptoms.
- **Vaginal dilators** after radiotherapy (prevent stenosis, allow examination and intercourse).
- Manage **menopausal symptoms** after RT (ovarian failure): HRT is not contraindicated in squamous cervical cancer.
- Watch for late RT effects: radiation proctitis/cystitis, fistula, vaginal stenosis, bowel strictures.
- **Palliative patients**: regular review of pain (titrate morphine daily), bleeding, odour, nausea, renal function only if it changes management; home-based care.

## 10. Complications

**Disease-related**

| Complication | Cause / notes |
|---|---|
| **Renal failure (obstructive uropathy)** | Parametrial/pelvic wall spread compressing the ureters: **commonest cause of death** |
| **Haemorrhage** | Tumour erosion of vessels; can be torrential |
| **Fistulae** | **Vesicovaginal** (urine leak), **rectovaginal** (faeces); from tumour invasion |
| **Anaemia** | Chronic bleeding, malnutrition |
| **Pyometra / sepsis** | Tumour occluding the canal; infected necrotic tumour |
| **Pain** | Nerve, bone and pelvic wall involvement |
| **Lymphoedema, DVT/PE** | Pelvic node and vein involvement; hypercoagulability |
| **Cachexia, malnutrition** | Advanced disease |
| **Psychosocial** | Stigma (offensive odour, fistula), marital abandonment, financial ruin, depression |

**Treatment-related**

| Treatment | Complications |
|---|---|
| **Radical hysterectomy** | Ureteric/bladder injury, ureteric fistula, bladder dysfunction (autonomic nerve damage), lymphocyst, lymphoedema |
| **Radiotherapy** | Cystitis, proctitis, diarrhoea, vaginal stenosis, fistula (radiation necrosis), ovarian failure, bowel strictures |
| **Cisplatin** | Nephrotoxicity, ototoxicity, neuropathy, emesis, myelosuppression, hypomagnesaemia/hypokalaemia; anaemia |
| **Ablation / LEEP** | Watery discharge, bleeding, infection; LEEP/cone: stenosis, cervical insufficiency |
| **Morphine** | Constipation (always a laxative), nausea, drowsiness; accumulation in renal failure |

## 11. Prognosis

**Approximate 5-year survival with treatment**: stage I **80–95%**, II **60–75%**, III **30–45%**, IVA **15–20%**, IVB **<10%**. In Uganda, overall survival is much lower because of late presentation (about 80% stage IIB or worse at UCI) and incomplete treatment (only 12% completing the full course in one UCI study). Every day of delay beyond 56 days of chemoradiation reduces local control. The commonest cause of death is renal failure from ureteric obstruction.

## 12. Prevention

WHO **90-70-90 by 2030**: **90%** of girls fully vaccinated with HPV vaccine by 15; **70%** of women screened with a high-performance test by 35 and again by 45; **90%** of women with pre-cancer treated and 90% with invasive cancer managed. Elimination threshold: **<4 per 100,000** women.

**Primary prevention: HPV vaccination**

| Item | Details |
|---|---|
| **Target** | Girls aged **9–14** (Uganda: **10-year-old girls**, through schools and health facilities); WHO secondary targets include older girls/young women (catch-up), and boys where resources allow |
| **Vaccine** | Uganda uses the quadrivalent vaccine (HPV 6, 11, 16, 18); bivalent and nonavalent vaccines also exist |
| **Schedule** | WHO 2022: **1 or 2 doses** for girls 9–14 and young women 15–20; Uganda moved to a **single dose in 2025** (previously 2 doses 6 months apart) |
| **Immunocompromised / HIV** | **At least 2 doses, ideally 3** |
| **Route** | 0.5 mL **IM** into the deltoid |
| **Key message** | Vaccination does **not** replace screening later in life |

**Secondary prevention: screening and treatment of pre-cancer (WHO 2021)**

| Population | Primary test | Start age | Interval | Approach | After treatment |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **General population** | **HPV DNA** (preferred); VIA or cytology where HPV is not yet available | **30** | **5–10 years** (HPV); **3 years** (VIA/cytology) | Screen-and-treat (or screen–triage–treat) | Re-test at **12 months** |
| **Women living with HIV** | **HPV DNA** | **25** | **3–5 years** (HPV); 3 years (VIA/cytology) | **Screen, triage and treat** | Re-test at **12 months** |

Screening can stop after 50 (general) or 65 (WLWH) if the last two screens were negative. **Self-sampling** for HPV is recommended as an option: it improves uptake in communities where women avoid speculum examinations. The screen-and-treat procedure is in section 9.

> [!UGANDA]
> Uganda's cervical cancer programme began with **VIA-based "see-and-treat"** (with cryotherapy) for women aged **25–49**, with screening **every 3 years for HIV-negative** women and **annually for women living with HIV** under earlier national plans. Following the **WHO 2021 guideline**, Uganda is scaling up **HPV DNA testing** (including **self-sampling** and **GeneXpert** platforms at hubs) with **VIA triage** and **thermal ablation**, and the HIV consolidated guidelines now name HPV testing as the preferred primary test for women living with HIV. Check the current MoH algorithm at your facility: intervals are in transition.

**Tertiary prevention**: early diagnosis of symptomatic women (look and biopsy), prompt referral with the core staging set, completing chemoradiation within 56 days, early palliative care.

## 13. Special Populations

- **Women living with HIV**: about 6 times the risk; screen from 25, every 3–5 years, screen–triage–treat, re-test at 12 months after treatment; larger lesions, more recurrence, faster progression; optimise ART (suppressed viral load) and co-trimoxazole before and during chemoradiation; at least 2 (ideally 3) doses of HPV vaccine if a girl is HIV-positive.
- **Pregnancy**: CIN: colposcopy to exclude invasion and **defer treatment** until ≥6 weeks postpartum; do not ablate in pregnancy. Invasive cancer: management depends on stage and gestation, decided by the MDT; deliver by **caesarean section** (vaginal delivery through a tumour risks haemorrhage and tumour implantation in the episiotomy).
- **Young women wanting fertility**: IA1 cone/LEEP with clear margins; IA2–IB1 radical trachelectomy in selected women; ovaries can be conserved at radical hysterectomy in squamous cancer.
- **Post-menopausal women**: SCJ inside the canal, so VIA is poor; HPV testing or colposcopy preferred.
- **Renal impairment**: no cisplatin below a creatinine clearance of about 50 mL/min; drain the kidneys first; reduce morphine dose and frequency.

## 14. Key Points

### High-yield points

- Persistent high-risk HPV is necessary: **E6 degrades p53, E7 inactivates Rb**.
- **Symptomatic women are diagnosed, not screened**: look and biopsy the **edge** of any growth.
- **Never ablate a lesion that looks invasive.**
- Stage with your hands: **rectovaginal examination** separates IIB from IIIB.
- **Hydronephrosis = IIIB** whatever the size; **bullous oedema is not IVA**.
- Early disease: radical hysterectomy or chemoradiation; locally advanced (IB3–IVA): **chemoradiation within 56 days**.
- **Check the kidneys before cisplatin**; drain obstructed kidneys first.
- Keep **Hb ≥10 g/dL** during radiotherapy.
- Renal failure from ureteric obstruction is the **commonest cause of death**.
- Link every advanced case to **palliative care on the day of diagnosis**; oral morphine is free and safe.

### Exam traps

- **"VIA was negative so she does not have cancer."** Wrong: a **symptomatic** woman is **diagnosed, not screened**; persistent PCB needs colposcopy and biopsy (adenocarcinoma can hide in the canal).
- **Staging a 2 cm tumour with hydronephrosis as stage I or II.** Wrong: hydronephrosis or a non-functioning kidney = **IIIB**, whatever the tumour size (unless another cause).
- **Calling bullous oedema of the bladder IVA.** Wrong: IVA needs **biopsy-proven mucosal** invasion.
- **Ablating a suspicious lesion because it is "VIA positive".** Wrong: a growth or ulcer is **suspicious for cancer** → biopsy and refer; ablation destroys the diagnosis.
- **Biopsying the necrotic centre.** Wrong: take the punch biopsy from the **edge** of the growth.
- **Forgetting the rectovaginal examination.** Wrong: it is the only bedside way to assess the **parametria** (IIB vs IIIB).
- **Starting cisplatin without checking the kidneys.** Wrong: check **creatinine and renal ultrasound** first; drain obstructed kidneys before curative chemoradiation.
- **Choosing surgery for IB3 or IIB.** Wrong: locally advanced disease (IB3–IVA) gets **concurrent chemoradiation**; avoid surgery + radiation "double treatment".

### Questions seniors ask

**Viva questions**

1. *How does HPV cause cancer?* Persistent high-risk HPV integrates, E2 is lost, E6 degrades p53 and E7 inactivates Rb → uncontrolled proliferation and genomic instability.
2. *What are the WHO 90-70-90 targets?* 90% of girls fully vaccinated by 15; 70% of women screened with a high-performance test by 35 and again by 45; 90% of women with pre-cancer treated and 90% with invasive cancer managed.
3. *How is VIA performed and read?* 3–5% acetic acid, 1 minute; dense acetowhite lesion at the SCJ = positive; growth/ulcer = suspicious for cancer.
4. *Eligibility criteria for ablation?* Entire lesion and SCJ visible, <75% of the cervix, no extension into the canal/vagina, no suspicion of cancer, not pregnant.
5. *Stage a woman with a 3 cm tumour and hydronephrosis.* IIIB (hydronephrosis upstages to IIIB regardless of size, unless another cause).
6. *Why is cisplatin given with radiotherapy?* Radiosensitiser; improves survival; weekly 40 mg/m².
7. *What is removed in a radical hysterectomy?* Uterus, cervix, parametria, upper vagina, pelvic nodes (ovaries can be conserved).
8. *Commonest cause of death?* Renal failure from bilateral ureteric obstruction.
9. *Why is anaemia corrected during radiotherapy?* Hypoxic tumour cells are radioresistant; keep Hb ≥10 g/dL.
10. *How is cervical cancer in pregnancy delivered?* Caesarean section (avoid haemorrhage and tumour seeding of the episiotomy).

**On the ward round**

- **How do you assess the parametrium?** Rectovaginal examination (ideally EUA): thickened with a gap to the side wall = IIB; reaching the side wall = IIIB. MRI where available.
- **What is the minimum staging workup you can do before referral?** Biopsy, Hb, urea/creatinine, renal ultrasound, CXR, HIV status (± CD4/VL), and a careful speculum, bimanual and rectovaginal examination.
- **This woman has a 3 cm tumour and left hydronephrosis: what stage and what treatment?** IIIB; concurrent chemoradiation if renal function allows (nephrostomy if needed), otherwise radiotherapy alone or palliation.
- **Why give cisplatin with radiotherapy, and what do you monitor?** Radiosensitiser, improves survival; weekly creatinine and electrolytes (Mg, K), FBC, hearing, emesis.
- **Why must treatment finish within 56 days?** Tumour cells repopulate during gaps; each extra day reduces local control.
- **How do you stop heavy bleeding from a cervical tumour?** Resuscitate, TXA 1 g IV, tight vaginal pack + urinary catheter, transfuse, urgent haemostatic radiotherapy; embolisation/ligation where available.
- **Why is renal failure the commonest cause of death?** Parametrial and side-wall spread compresses both ureters → obstructive uropathy.
- **What would you tell a woman with stage IVB disease?** Honest explanation of the diagnosis; aims of care (comfort, bleeding, odour and pain control); oral morphine is safe and free; link to palliative care and hospice the same day; involve family if she wishes.

### Mnemonics

- **"E6 kills p53, E7 robs Rb"**: **6** → p**53**, **7** → **R**b ("E7 Robs").
- **FIGO sizes, "2, 4"**: IB1 <2, IB2 2–4, IB3 ≥4; IIA1 <4, IIA2 ≥4.
- **Stage III = "3 Ls"**: **L**ower third of vagina (IIIA), **L**ateral pelvic wall/hydronephrosis, the **L**oin (IIIB), **L**ymph nodes (IIIC).
- **Stage IV = "neighbours then far away"**: IVA bladder/rectum mucosa; IVB distant.
- **90-70-90**: "**V**accinate – **S**creen – **T**reat" (VST).
- **Ablation eligibility, "SEEN"**: **S**CJ visible, **E**ctocervical, **E**xtent <75%, **N**o suspicion of cancer.
- **Cryotherapy, "3-5-3"**: freeze 3 minutes, thaw 5, freeze 3.
- **Chemoradiation, "45–50 / 40 / 56"**: EBRT 45–50 Gy, cisplatin 40 mg/m² weekly, finish within 56 days.

### OSCE tips

> [!EXAM]
> **OSCE: counselling for screening**: explain what HPV is (common, sexually transmitted, usually clears), why screening (find pre-cancer early, simple treatment), how (self-sample or speculum), what a positive result means (not cancer, but needs VIA/treatment), treatment (thermal ablation: quick, little pain, same day), after-care (discharge, abstain 4 weeks), follow-up (1 year), and check understanding. Avoid blame: HPV is not a sign of unfaithfulness.

- **VIA station**: speculum, identify the SCJ, 3–5% acetic acid, wait 1 minute, report negative / positive / suspicious for cancer, state ablation eligibility.
- **Staging station**: describe size, vaginal extent, each parametrium separately (rectovaginal), rectal mucosa, nodes and kidneys, then assign the FIGO stage and say what changes it.
- **Breaking bad news / palliative station**: honest explanation, aims of care, morphine is safe and free, same-day palliative care link, involve family if she wishes.
