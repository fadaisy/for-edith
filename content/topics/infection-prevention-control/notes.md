## 1. Purpose and Indications

**Infection prevention and control (IPC)** means the evidence-based practices that prevent **health-care-associated infections (HAIs)** in patients and **occupational infections** in health workers. On an Ob/Gyn rotation it shows up everywhere: the **surgical site infection** after a CS, **puerperal sepsis** after too many vaginal examinations with unwashed hands, a **neonatal sepsis** outbreak in the special care unit, and the **needle-stick** during a perineal repair at 2 a.m. On the paediatric rotation the same skills prevent cross-infection on the neonatal unit and the children's ward, where airborne (TB, measles) and contact (diarrhoea) precautions are daily decisions.

In Uganda, IPC is a matter of life and death, not paperwork. The country has repeatedly experienced **Ebola and Marburg outbreaks** in which health workers were among the first infected (from Dr Matthew Lukwiya in Gulu in 2000 to the Sudan-virus outbreaks of 2022 and 2025). HIV and hepatitis B are common, and antimicrobial resistance is rising sharply. Yet many facilities lack running water, alcohol rub, gloves or a functioning autoclave.

**The one thing never to forget:** *treat every patient's blood and body fluids as potentially infectious* (**standard precautions**), and **clean your hands at the 5 moments**. Hand hygiene is the single most effective IPC measure, and it costs almost nothing.

### Definitions

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Health-care-associated infection (HAI)** | An infection that was not present or incubating on admission and appears ≥48 h after admission, or within 30 days of surgery (90 days if an implant is used) |
| **Standard precautions** | The minimum IPC practices for **all patients**, regardless of diagnosis |
| **Transmission-based precautions** | Extra measures, on top of standard precautions, for known or suspected infections: **contact, droplet, airborne** |
| **Decontamination** | The first step for used items: soaking in **0.5% chlorine** for 10 min to **inactivate HBV, HIV and HCV** and make items safer to handle |
| **Cleaning** | Physical removal of blood, tissue and dirt with **detergent, water and scrubbing**. Without it, no disinfectant or sterilant works reliably |
| **High-level disinfection (HLD)** | Kills all microorganisms **except some bacterial spores** |
| **Sterilisation** | Kills **all** microorganisms **including spores** |
| **Antiseptic** | A chemical used on **living tissue** (chlorhexidine, povidone-iodine, alcohol) |
| **Disinfectant** | A chemical used on **inanimate objects** (chlorine, glutaraldehyde, phenolics) |
| **PEP** | Post-exposure prophylaxis: a short course of ARVs (and HBV vaccine ± immunoglobulin) after an exposure to prevent infection |
| **Outbreak** | More cases than expected in a particular place and time, often with the same organism |

### Burden and Uganda context

- HAIs affect roughly **1 in 10 hospitalised patients in low- and middle-income countries**, about twice the rate in high-income countries (WHO). **Surgical site infection** is the commonest HAI in low-resource settings. After CS in Ugandan hospitals it runs at around **10–15%** or higher in several studies, dominated by **ESBL-producing *Klebsiella* and *E. coli* and MRSA**.
- **Puerperal sepsis** remains a leading direct cause of maternal death in Uganda. Many cases are preventable: unclean hands, frequent VEs, prolonged labour with unclean instruments, and unsafe abortion.
- **Neonatal sepsis outbreaks** in special care units are often traced to contaminated hands, shared equipment and multi-use vials.
- **Hand hygiene compliance** in Ugandan hospitals has been measured as **low at baseline**. Quality-improvement programmes (a WHO multimodal strategy with locally produced alcohol rub, training, audit and feedback) have shown large improvements in several hospitals.
- **Health worker exposure**: needle-stick injuries are very common among Ugandan interns, nurses and students, and **most are not reported**. Many health workers are **not fully vaccinated against hepatitis B**. Uganda has intermediate-to-high HBV prevalence (especially in the north and northeast).
- **Resources**: many HC IIIs and IVs have **intermittent water**, no functioning autoclave (so they rely on **boiling**), and irregular supplies of gloves and alcohol rub. So IPC in Uganda depends heavily on **0.5% chlorine** made from household bleach (**JIK**), boiling, and discipline.

### Indications: standard precautions for everyone, extra precautions by route

**Standard precautions** (every patient, every time): hand hygiene, PPE by risk assessment, respiratory hygiene and cough etiquette, safe injections and sharps, environmental cleaning, linen and waste management, reprocessing of equipment, and patient placement.

**Transmission-based precautions** (added on top):

| Type | Spread by | Examples | Key measures |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Contact** | Direct or indirect touch | MRSA/ESBL wound infection, diarrhoea, scabies, **Ebola/Marburg** (body fluids) | Gloves and gown for contact; dedicated equipment; isolation or cohorting |
| **Droplet** (>5 µm, travel about 1–2 m) | Coughing, sneezing, talking | Influenza, COVID-19, meningococcus, pertussis | **Medical mask** within 1–2 m; patient wears a mask; spacing |
| **Airborne** (<5 µm, remain suspended) | Inhalation over distance | **Pulmonary TB**, measles, varicella | **N95/FFP2 respirator** for staff; patient masked; well-ventilated or single room; open windows |

### Choosing PPE by risk assessment

| Task | Gloves | Apron/gown | Mask | Eye protection |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Taking BP, abdominal palpation (intact skin) | No | No | No | No |
| Venepuncture, cannulation | **Yes** (non-sterile) | No | No | No |
| **Vaginal examination in labour** | **Sterile** (or HLD) | Apron if splash risk | No | No |
| **Conducting a delivery / perineal repair** | **Sterile** (elbow-length if available) | **Plastic apron** | **Yes** | **Yes** (liquor and blood splash) |
| Caesarean section | Sterile, double | Sterile gown + apron | Yes | Yes |
| MVA / evacuation | Sterile/HLD | Apron | Yes | Yes |
| Handling placenta, cleaning spills, instruments | **Utility gloves** | Apron | Mask if splash | Yes if splash |
| Suspected **TB** | Standard | Standard | **N95** (staff); medical mask (patient) | As needed |
| Suspected **Ebola/Marburg** (VHF) | **Double gloves** | **Coverall/gown + apron** | **N95 or medical mask per protocol** | **Face shield + goggles**, hood, boots. **Trained buddy** for doffing |

## 2. Contraindications and Precautions

> [!NOTE]
> **Gloves do not replace hand hygiene.** Gloves have micro-holes, and hands get contaminated when gloves are removed. Clean your hands **before** putting on gloves (if a moment applies) and **always after** removing them. **Never wear the same gloves between two patients**, and never wash or alcohol-rub gloves to reuse them.

- **Alcohol rub is not enough** when hands are **visibly soiled** or contaminated with body fluids, after using the toilet, and with spore-forming organisms (e.g. *C. difficile*) or during diarrhoeal outbreaks: use **soap and water**.
- **Chlorine**: **never mix with acids or ammonia-containing cleaners** (releases toxic chlorine or chloramine gas). Do not soak instruments for **longer than 10 minutes** (corrosion). Do not use bleach on wounds or in the eye.
- **Glutaraldehyde** is toxic: use with ventilation and gloves; rinse items with boiled (HLD) or sterile (sterilisation) water.
- **Dry heat** only for glass and metal.
- **HLD is not sterilisation**: critical items (entering sterile tissue) must be sterilised wherever possible.
- **PEP is not indicated** for intact-skin exposure, for urine, faeces, saliva or vomit **without visible blood**, or **more than 72 hours** after the exposure. An **HIV-positive** worker needs ART, not PEP.
- **No routine antibiotics** for uncomplicated vaginal birth or episiotomy (WHO), and **do not prolong** surgical prophylaxis.
- **No routine environmental swabbing**: only when the epidemiology points to a source.
- **Do not aspirate or decant waste** or hand-sort bins; never recap needles by hand.

## 3. Relevant Anatomy and Normal Values

### The chain of infection

```
  Infectious agent ─► Reservoir ─► Portal of exit ─► Mode of transmission
        ▲                                                      │
        │                                                      ▼
  Susceptible host ◄──── Portal of entry ◄─────────────────────┘
```

Every IPC measure **breaks a link**:

| Link | Obstetric example | How we break it |
|---|---|---|
| Agent | *Staph. aureus*, *E. coli*, GBS, HIV, HBV | Antimicrobials; sterilisation |
| Reservoir | Patient's skin and vagina, contaminated instruments, the environment | Cleaning, disinfection, sterilisation |
| Portal of exit | Blood, liquor, lochia, respiratory droplets | Cover wounds, respiratory hygiene, safe waste |
| Mode of transmission | **Hands (the commonest)**, instruments, sharps, droplets, air | **Hand hygiene**, PPE, safe sharps, isolation |
| Portal of entry | Surgical wounds, cannula sites, the placental bed, perineal tears, needle-stick | Asepsis, sterile gloves for VE, careful technique |
| Susceptible host | Anaemic, exhausted, post-op, HIV-positive mothers; preterm babies | Nutrition, anaemia correction, vaccination (HBV, tetanus), prophylactic antibiotics when indicated |

### Spaulding classification (how clean must an item be?)

| Category | Contact | Examples in O&G | Minimum processing |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Critical** | Enters **sterile tissue or the vascular system** | CS instruments, needles, suture needles, uterine sound, curettes | **Sterilisation** |
| **Semi-critical** | Touches **mucous membranes or non-intact skin** | **Vaginal specula**, MVA cannulae and aspirators, laryngoscope blades, delivery-set scissors (HLD acceptable where sterilisation is unavailable) | **HLD** at minimum (sterilise if possible) |
| **Non-critical** | Touches **intact skin** only | BP cuffs, stethoscopes, Pinard stethoscopes, beds, mackintoshes | **Cleaning** ± low-level disinfection |

### Why each step of instrument processing matters

1. **Decontamination (0.5% chlorine, 10 min)** makes the item **safe for the person who cleans it**. It inactivates HBV and HIV. **Not longer than 10 minutes**, because chlorine corrodes steel.
2. **Cleaning** removes up to about 99% of organisms and, crucially, the **organic material (blood, tissue) that shields microbes** and inactivates disinfectants. **"You cannot sterilise dirt."**
3. **HLD or sterilisation** kills what remains. Choose by the **Spaulding category** and by what is available.
4. **Storage** keeps it clean. A sterile item that is stored wet, in a torn wrap, or opened and left exposed is **no longer sterile**.

### Why alcohol rub is preferred for routine hand hygiene

Alcohol rub (60–80% alcohol) **kills faster** than soap and water, takes **20–30 seconds**, needs **no sink or towel**, can be carried to the bedside, and is **kinder to skin** (it contains an emollient such as glycerol). Soap and water physically removes organic material and **spores** (alcohol does not kill *Clostridioides difficile* spores), so use it when **hands are visibly soiled**, after using the toilet, and during diarrhoeal outbreaks.

### Key parameters ("normal values" for IPC)

| Item | Value |
|---|---|
| Alcohol hand rub | **20–30 seconds**, about 3 mL (a palmful) |
| Soap and water handwash | **40–60 seconds** |
| Decontamination | **0.5% chlorine for 10 minutes** |
| HLD by boiling or steaming | **20 minutes** timed from the rolling boil |
| HLD by chemical soak | **0.1% chlorine** or **2% glutaraldehyde** for **20 min** |
| Autoclave | **121 °C, 106 kPa (15 lb/in²), 20 min unwrapped / 30 min wrapped** |
| Dry heat | **170 °C for 60 min** (or 160 °C for 120 min) |
| Chemical sterilisation | **2% glutaraldehyde for 8–10 hours** |
| Safety box | Close at **three-quarters full** |
| Droplet vs airborne particle size | Droplets **>5 µm** (travel 1–2 m); airborne **<5 µm** |
| Protective anti-HBs | **≥10 mIU/mL** |
| Needle-stick transmission risk | HBV **up to 30%** (HBeAg+), HCV **about 1.8%**, HIV **about 0.3%** (mucous membrane about 0.09%) |
| Sepsis warning signs (obstetric) | Temperature **≥38.0 °C or <36 °C**, pulse **>100/min**, RR **>20/min**, SBP **<90 mmHg**, confusion, urine **<30 mL/h** |

## 4. Equipment and Preparation

### Equipment

| Item | Use | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Alcohol-based hand rub (ABHR)** | Routine hand hygiene | WHO formulation: ethanol 80% or isopropyl alcohol 75% + glycerol 1.45% + hydrogen peroxide 0.125%. Many Ugandan hospitals make their own |
| **Liquid soap, running water, single-use towels** | Handwashing when visibly soiled | **Bar soap in a wet dish and shared cloth towels recontaminate hands** |
| **Examination gloves** (non-sterile) | Contact with blood or body fluids, mucous membranes, non-intact skin | Single use. **Change between patients**, and do hand hygiene after removal |
| **Sterile (surgical) gloves** | VE in labour, delivery, procedures, surgery | HLD gloves are an acceptable alternative in low-resource settings |
| **Utility (heavy-duty) gloves** | Cleaning instruments, handling waste, cleaning spills | Reusable after washing |
| **Plastic apron / fluid-resistant gown** | Protect clothing and skin from splashes | Aprons are essential at every delivery |
| **Medical (surgical) mask** | Droplet precautions; splash protection; theatre | Covers **nose and mouth** |
| **N95 / FFP2 respirator** | Airborne precautions (TB, measles), aerosol-generating procedures | Needs a **fit check** each time |
| **Goggles / face shield** | Eye and mucous membrane protection from splashes (delivery, CS, cord cutting, placenta handling) | Often forgotten, yet delivery is a major splash risk |
| **Gumboots / closed shoes** | Protect feet from spills and dropped sharps | |
| **Safety box (sharps container)** | Immediate disposal of sharps | At the point of use; closed at three-quarters full |
| **Colour-coded bins with liners** | Waste segregation | Black, green, yellow, red, brown |
| **Plastic buckets with 0.5% chlorine** | Decontamination of instruments and gloves | Prepared daily |
| **Autoclave / pressure steamer, drum or boiler** | Sterilisation / HLD | Record every cycle; use **chemical indicator tape** |

### How to make 0.5% chlorine

| Source | Concentration | How to dilute to 0.5% |
|---|---|---|
| **JIK / household bleach** | About **3.5%** available chlorine (check the label) | **1 part bleach + 6 parts clean water**. Formula: total parts water = (% concentrate ÷ % desired) − 1 = (3.5 ÷ 0.5) − 1 = 6 |
| **HTH / calcium hypochlorite powder** | 70% | **About 7 g (half a tablespoon) per litre of water**. Formula: g/L = (% desired ÷ % concentrate) × 1000 = (0.5 ÷ 70) × 1000 ≈ 7.1 |
| **Chlorine tablets (NaDCC)** | Per manufacturer | Follow the package instructions |

> [!NOTE]
> Prepare chlorine solutions **daily** (or sooner if visibly dirty), because chlorine is unstable and weakens in light and heat. Store them in covered, opaque containers. **Never mix chlorine with acids or ammonia-containing cleaners**, because this releases toxic chlorine or chloramine gas. Use **0.1% chlorine** (JIK 1 part : 34 parts water) for **HLD by chemical soak** and for rinsing some surfaces.

### Personal preparation

- **Bare below the elbows**, **no rings, watches or bracelets**, short nails, no artificial nails. Cover cuts with a waterproof dressing.
- **Hepatitis B vaccination** completed (3 doses at 0, 1 and 6 months), with the anti-HBs response checked where possible.
- Know your ward's **IPC focal person**, where the **PEP starter packs** are, and where the safety boxes and colour-coded bins are.

### Consent and communication

- **Source testing after an exposure** needs the source patient's **informed consent** (rapid HIV, HBsAg, HCV) with pre-test counselling; keep the source identified (name and bed) so this can happen quickly.
- **Isolation or cohorting**: explain to the patient and family why precautions are needed, without stigma.
- **Placentas**: ask the family's wishes before disposal (see the waste section).

## 5. Procedure Step by Step

### A. Hand hygiene: the WHO 5 Moments

```
        ┌──────────────── PATIENT ZONE ────────────────┐
        │   1 BEFORE touching patient                   │
        │   2 BEFORE clean/aseptic procedure            │
        │   3 AFTER body fluid exposure risk            │
        │   4 AFTER touching patient                    │
        │   5 AFTER touching patient surroundings       │
        └───────────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

| Moment | When | Why | Labour-ward example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1. Before touching a patient** | Before contact | Protects the **patient** from organisms on your hands | Before palpating the abdomen |
| **2. Before a clean/aseptic procedure** | Immediately before, **even if you will wear gloves** | Protects the patient from organisms entering the body | Before a VE, inserting a catheter or cannula, perineal repair |
| **3. After body-fluid exposure risk** | Immediately after, **after glove removal** | Protects **you and the environment** | After the delivery, after emptying a urine bag, after handling the placenta |
| **4. After touching a patient** | When leaving the patient | Protects you and the environment | After the ANC examination |
| **5. After touching patient surroundings** | Even if you did not touch the patient | Protects you and the environment | After adjusting the drip or the bed linen |

**Alcohol hand rub technique (20–30 seconds):**

1. Apply **a palmful** (about 3 mL) in a cupped hand, enough to cover all surfaces.
2. Rub **palm to palm**.
3. **Right palm over the left dorsum** with interlaced fingers, and vice versa.
4. **Palm to palm with fingers interlaced.**
5. **Backs of fingers** to the opposing palm with fingers interlocked.
6. **Rotational rubbing of the left thumb** clasped in the right palm, and vice versa.
7. **Rotational rubbing of the fingertips** of the right hand in the left palm, and vice versa.
8. Continue until the hands are **dry**. Then they are safe.

**Handwashing with soap and water (40–60 seconds):** wet hands, apply soap, perform the same 6 rubbing steps, rinse, **dry thoroughly with a single-use towel**, and **use the towel to turn off the tap**.

### B. Donning and doffing PPE

**Donning (put on) sequence:**

1. **Hand hygiene.**
2. **Gown or apron**: fully cover the torso from neck to knees; tie at the back.
3. **Mask or respirator**: secure the ties or bands, fit the flexible band to the **nose bridge**, and fit snugly under the chin. For an N95, do a **seal check** (breathe out, and no air should leak at the edges).
4. **Goggles or face shield**: over the face and eyes, adjusted to fit.
5. **Gloves**: extend them **over the cuffs** of the gown.

**Doffing (take off) sequence** (the principle: the outside is contaminated, so touch only the "clean" inside and ties, and remove the **most contaminated items first**):

1. **Gloves**: grasp the outside of one glove at the wrist and peel it off inside out, holding it in the gloved hand. Slide the **ungloved fingers under the remaining glove at the wrist** and peel it off over the first. Discard in the **yellow bin**.
2. **Hand hygiene** (if the hands may be contaminated).
3. **Goggles or face shield**: remove from the back by the band or earpieces, without touching the front.
4. **Gown or apron**: unfasten the ties, pull away from the neck and shoulders touching the **inside only**, turn it **inside out**, fold or roll it into a bundle, and discard. (Alternatively remove the gown and gloves together, rolling them inside out.)
5. **Mask or respirator**: grasp the bottom ties or elastics, then the top ones, and remove **without touching the front**. For airborne precautions, remove the respirator **after leaving the room**.
6. **Hand hygiene** immediately after removing all PPE.

### C. Processing instruments

**Step 1: Decontamination** (at the point of use, immediately after the procedure)

- Put on utility gloves. Place **all instruments, open**, in a **plastic bucket of 0.5% chlorine** so they are fully submerged. **Soak for 10 minutes**, then remove and rinse with water. Never leave instruments for hours, because chlorine corrodes metal.
- Wipe surfaces (couch, mackintosh, trolley) with 0.5% chlorine.
- Immerse reusable gloves (before removal) and dispose of single-use gloves in the yellow bin.

**Step 2: Cleaning**

1. Wear **utility gloves, apron and eye protection**.
2. Scrub every instrument **under the water surface** (to avoid splashing) with a soft brush, **detergent** and water. Open the hinges and clean the jaws, ratchets and teeth.
3. **Rinse** thoroughly with clean water.
4. **Dry** (air dry or with a clean towel). Wet instruments dilute chemical disinfectants and rust in storage.
5. Inspect for residual blood and damage.

**Step 3a: High-level disinfection (HLD)**

| Method | How | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Boiling** | Fully submerge, lid on; **start timing when the water reaches a rolling boil**; boil **20 minutes**; don't add anything once timing has started | The most common HLD in Ugandan health centres |
| **Steaming** | **20 minutes** in a steamer | Good for gloves and MVA cannulae |
| **Chemical** | Soak in **0.1% chlorine for 20 min** or **2% glutaraldehyde for 20 min**; then **rinse with boiled water** | Glutaraldehyde is toxic (ventilation, gloves). Concentrations for chlorine HLD vary: general IP manuals use 0.1% (made with boiled water), while Ipas MVA processing guidance uses **0.5% for 20 min**. Follow your unit's protocol |

Use HLD items immediately, or store them in a covered HLD container for up to about 1 week.

**Step 3b: Sterilisation**

| Method | Parameters | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Steam autoclave** | **121 °C, 106 kPa (15 lb/in²)**: **20 minutes unwrapped, 30 minutes wrapped** | The gold standard. Use **chemical indicator tape** on each pack, record the cycle, and use periodic biological indicators where available |
| **Dry heat (oven)** | **170 °C for 60 minutes** (or 160 °C for 120 minutes) | Only for glass and metal. Timing starts when the temperature is reached |
| **Chemical sterilisation** | **2% glutaraldehyde for 8–10 hours**, then rinse with sterile water | When heat is not possible |

**Step 4: Storage**: wrapped sterile packs go on clean, dry, covered shelves off the floor. The pack is sterile until it is **opened, torn, wet or dropped** (event-related sterility). Label every pack with the date and "sterilised by".

### D. Health-care waste segregation (Uganda MoH colour code)

| Container | Waste | Examples in Ob/Gyn | Final disposal |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Black bin / liner** | **Non-infectious general waste** | Paper, packaging, food leftovers | Municipal collection / burning |
| **Green bin / liner** | **Non-infectious plastics** (recyclable) [VERIFY: green category and its use differ between MoH documents and facilities] | Clean drug packaging, bottles | Recycling / municipal |
| **Yellow bin / liner** | **Infectious waste** | Blood-soaked gauze, swabs and pads, used gloves, IV giving sets, catheters | Incineration |
| **Red bin / liner** | **Highly infectious waste** | Laboratory cultures and specimens, waste from isolation of highly infectious diseases (Ebola/VHF); anatomical waste per facility policy | Incineration (after autoclaving where required) |
| **Yellow safety box** | **Sharps** | Needles, blades, suture needles, broken ampoules, cannula stylets | Close at **three-quarters full**, seal, incinerate |
| **Brown bin / liner** | **Pharmaceutical and chemical waste** | Expired drugs, chemicals | Return to NMS or special disposal |

> [!UGANDA]
> **Placentas**: in Uganda many families wish to take the placenta home for culturally important burial. Respect this. Hand it over in a **sealed, leak-proof bag**, and explain how to handle it (gloves or bag, and burying it deep). Otherwise, dispose of placentas in the **placenta pit** or incinerator per facility policy. Do **not** put placentas in the black general bin.

**Segregation rules**: segregate **at the point of generation** (at the bedside, not later), fill bins to **no more than three-quarters**, never decant or hand-sort waste, and label bins with the ward and date.

### E. Safe sharps practice

1. **Minimise injections**: use oral drugs where possible.
2. **Never recap needles** by hand. If recapping is unavoidable, use the **one-handed scoop technique**.
3. **Don't bend, break or remove needles** from syringes by hand.
4. **Dispose of the sharp immediately, at the point of use, by the user**, into a safety box within arm's reach.
5. Use a **neutral zone (kidney dish)** for passing sharps in theatre and at delivery. Announce "sharp".
6. Suture with **instruments, not fingers** (use forceps to hold tissue and to handle the needle). During perineal repair, don't feel for the needle tip with a finger.
7. **Double gloving** at CS and major surgery.
8. **Get vaccinated against hepatitis B** (3 doses at 0, 1 and 6 months), and check the anti-HBs response where possible.

### F. Environmental cleaning and spills

- Clean **from clean to dirty and top to bottom**. Use detergent and water for routine cleaning, then disinfect high-touch and blood-contaminated surfaces.
- **Blood or body-fluid spill**: put on utility gloves (and apron and eye protection if large). **Cover the spill with 0.5% chlorine** (or absorbent material soaked in chlorine), leave for about **10 minutes**, wipe up with disposable cloths into the yellow bin, clean with detergent and water, then dry.
- After **each delivery**: clean and disinfect the delivery bed and mackintosh, change the linen, and clean the floor under the bed.
- **Linen**: handle soiled linen with gloves, **don't shake** it, bag it at the bedside, and transport it in leak-proof bags. Heavily soiled linen is soaked in 0.5% chlorine, then washed.

### G. IPC in the labour ward (putting it together)

| Practice | Why |
|---|---|
| **Hand hygiene at the 5 moments**, especially **before every VE** | Hands carry *E. coli*, *Staph* and GBS into the genital tract |
| **Sterile (or HLD) gloves for VE**; limit VEs to **every 4 hours** in the active first stage unless indicated | Each VE after ROM increases the risk of chorioamnionitis |
| Clean perineum with **clean water** before VE and delivery | Mechanical cleansing |
| **Sterile delivery set** for each woman (scissors, cord clamps, artery forceps) | Tetanus and sepsis prevention (maternal and neonatal) |
| **Full PPE for delivery**: apron, gloves, mask and **eye protection**, boots | Splashes of liquor and blood are common; HIV/HBV |
| **Antibiotic prophylaxis only when indicated**: CS (before incision), manual removal of the placenta, 3rd/4th-degree tears | WHO does **not** recommend routine antibiotics for uncomplicated vaginal birth or episiotomy (resistance) |
| **Vaginal cleansing with povidone-iodine before CS** in labour or with ROM | Reduces endometritis |
| **Clean cord care: chlorhexidine 7.1% digluconate (4% chlorhexidine) gel** to the cord stump (Uganda policy); nothing else on the cord | Prevents omphalitis and neonatal sepsis. No cow dung, herbs or ash |
| **Tetracycline 1% eye ointment** for every newborn | Prevents ophthalmia neonatorum |
| **Skin-to-skin, early exclusive breastfeeding** | Colonises the baby with maternal flora and provides immune protection |
| **Clean the bed, mackintosh and floor after every birth** | Environmental reservoir |
| **One woman per bed** | Overcrowding (two mothers per bed) spreads infection |

### H. Surgical site infection prevention bundle (WHO)

**Before surgery**: the patient bathes with soap; **no shaving** (clip if necessary); treat remote infections; correct anaemia and control blood sugar; give **antibiotic prophylaxis before incision** (for CS, within 60 min before the skin incision).

**In theatre**: surgical hand antisepsis; **chlorhexidine-alcohol skin prep**; sterile drapes and gowns; minimal traffic; **gentle tissue handling**; normothermia; **FiO₂ 80%** under general anaesthesia with intubation (WHO recommendation); change gloves after vaginal manipulation; consider **triclosan-coated sutures** where available.

**After surgery**: **do not prolong prophylactic antibiotics**; use a sterile dressing for 24–48 h; hand hygiene before touching the wound; watch for SSI (day 3–7) and educate the woman about it.

### I. Examining a patient with a suspected HAI (Hutchison order)

1. **Introduction and consent**; explain; chaperone for breast and perineal examination.
2. **Hand hygiene**; gloves and apron if the wound is draining (contact precautions).
3. **Position and exposure**: semi-recumbent; expose each area in turn, preserving dignity.
4. **General inspection and vital signs**: well or unwell, toxic, pallor, dehydration; temperature, pulse, BP, RR, SpO₂, conscious level, urine output; compare with the sepsis warning signs (section 3) and the obstetric early-warning chart.
5. **Hands and arms**: cannula sites (phlebitis).
6. **Face and neck**: mucosa, hydration, lymph nodes.
7. **Chest**: crackles, reduced air entry (hospital-acquired pneumonia); **breasts** (mastitis).
8. **Abdomen**: inspect and palpate the **wound**; palpate the **uterus** (tenderness, involution).
9. **Perineum and lochia**: repair site; offensive lochia.
10. **Catheter and urine**: cloudy urine, suprapubic pain.
11. **Calves**: swelling, tenderness (DVT as a non-infective cause of fever).
12. **Complete**: thank the patient, remove PPE in order, **hand hygiene**, summarise and plan.

Look at the **wound** (depth, bleeding, contamination) or the splashed eye or mouth: puncture, laceration, bleeding, skin integrity. Check the exposed person's general health (for the baseline).

- **Autoclave indicator tape not changed colour, or a wet or torn pack**: **not sterile**. Do not use it.

### Bedside tests

| Test | Use |
|---|---|

| **Rapid HBsAg test** (source and worker) | Guides the hepatitis B plan |

### Laboratory and imaging

| Test | When | Expected / abnormal finding | How it changes management |
|---|---|---|---|
| **HIV test of the exposed worker** (rapid test at all levels) | **Baseline**, then about **6 weeks** and **3 months** (some protocols add 6 months) | Negative expected. A positive baseline = infection **before** the exposure | Positive baseline: stop PEP, start ART. Seroconversion: ART and incident review |
| **Source HIV test and viral load** (rapid test at all levels; VL from the ART clinic records or CPHL samples) | At the time of exposure, with consent | Negative, or positive with suppressed VL, lowers the risk | Negative source: PEP can stop. Positive or unknown: complete 28 days |

| **HCV antibody**; HCV RNA (referral/private labs) | Baseline and follow-up if the source is HCV-positive or unknown | Seroconversion | Refer for direct-acting antiviral treatment |

| **Pus swab for culture and sensitivity** (RRH and national referral microbiology; rarely HC IV) | Every infected wound, **before antibiotics** | **ESBL *Klebsiella*/*E. coli***, **MRSA**, *Pseudomonas* | Targeted antibiotics; the same organism in several patients supports a common source |

| **Environmental swabs** (national/regional microbiology) | **Only when the epidemiology points to a source** (e.g. a shared suction machine) | The outbreak organism on equipment or fluids | Remove or reprocess the source. Routine environmental swabbing is **not** recommended |
| **Organism typing** (national reference laboratories; rarely available) | Complex outbreaks | Same strain in several patients | Confirms an outbreak, but **you do not need it to start control measures** |

## 7. Clinical Acumen

IPC acumen is about **risk assessment and noticing**: seeing the missed moment, predicting the exposure, and knowing what to do when something goes wrong. There are two situations you must be able to work up step by step: **(A) an occupational exposure** (a needle-stick, cut or blood splash to you or a colleague), and **(B) a suspected health-care-associated infection or outbreak** (for example a woman with a post-CS wound infection, or several women or babies infected on the same ward).

### Focused history: A. after an exposure (each answer changes the PEP decision)

| Question | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| **When did it happen?** | PEP ideally within **2 h**, never after **72 h** |
| **Type**: percutaneous, mucous membrane, non-intact skin, or intact skin | **Intact skin exposure needs no PEP** |
| **Device and depth**: **hollow-bore needle** (cannula, venepuncture) vs solid suture needle; deep injury; **visible blood** on the device | **Hollow-bore, deep and visibly bloody** injuries carry the highest risk |
| **Body fluid**: blood, bloody fluid, **liquor, vaginal secretions** (infectious) vs urine, faeces, vomit without visible blood (low risk) | Decides whether the exposure is significant |
| **Glove worn? Double gloves?** | A glove wipes off some of the blood and lowers the dose |
| **Source**: HIV status, **on ART? last viral load?** HBsAg, HCV | A suppressed source gives a very low risk, but an unknown or unsuppressed source means PEP; **never delay PEP waiting for source results** |
| **Your own HIV status** (last test), **hepatitis B vaccination** (number of doses) and **anti-HBs** if known | An HIV-positive worker needs ART, not PEP; an unvaccinated worker needs HBV vaccine ± HBIG |
| **Pregnancy or breastfeeding** | TLD is safe in pregnancy; it affects counselling |
| **Other medicines, kidney disease** | TDF (renal); DTG interacts with polyvalent cations: take DTG 2 h before or 6 h after iron, calcium or antacids |

### Focused history: B. suspected HAI or outbreak (ask of each case)

| Question | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| **What procedure and when?** Date of CS or delivery, theatre, operator, **day of onset** of infection | SSIs appear mostly on **day 4–7**; a common theatre, day or team points to a common source |
| **Antibiotic prophylaxis: which drug, and when?** | Given after cord clamping or not at all means less protection |
| **Labour details**: duration, ROM duration, **number of VEs**, prolonged obstructed labour | Host risk vs system failure |
| **Skin preparation, shaving, wound dressing, who changed it** | Common breaches |
| **For a neonatal cluster**: shared equipment (suction, bag-mask, incubators), **multi-dose vials**, how IV fluids and feeds are prepared, hand hygiene before handling babies | Typical sources of Gram-negative neonatal outbreaks |
| **Recent changes**: new staff, broken autoclave, water or chlorine shortage, new supplier | Outbreaks often follow a system change |
| **Is she the only one?** | Other similar infections recently suggest an **outbreak** |

### Red flags

> [!REDFLAG]
> - **Needle-stick or splash from any patient**: report and assess **immediately**. The PEP window is closing.
> - **A cluster** of SSIs, puerperal sepsis or neonatal sepsis in the same ward in a short time: an **outbreak**. Notify the IPC focal person urgently.
> - **Fever with bleeding, from an area with an ongoing Ebola/Marburg outbreak** or contact with a sick or dead person: **isolate, full VHF PPE, notify the district immediately**. Do not examine vaginally or deliver without full PPE.
> - **Cough for 2 weeks or more with weight loss in a ward patient**: suspect TB. Mask the patient, give staff N95s, and investigate (GeneXpert).
> - **Autoclave indicator tape not changed colour, or a wet or torn pack**: **not sterile**. Do not use it.
> - **Overfilled safety box, or sharps in a general bin**: a hazard to cleaners and waste handlers.

### Thinking like a clinician

1. **Assume every patient is infectious.** You cannot tell HIV or HBV status by looking, and many women are in the window period at delivery. That is the logic of **standard precautions**.
2. **Match processing to the Spaulding category**: a speculum (mucous membrane) needs **HLD**; a curette entering the uterus (sterile cavity) needs **sterilisation**. When an autoclave is unavailable, HLD by boiling is the accepted fallback for semi-critical items.
3. **"If X then think Y"**:
   - A foul-smelling SSI 5 days after CS with a **cluster** of similar cases means an IPC breach (theatre, antibiotic timing, the autoclave). Audit, don't just treat.
   - A health worker who is **unvaccinated for HBV** after a needle-stick: HBV is **100 times more infectious** than HIV. Give the HBV vaccine (± HBIG) urgently.
   - A **suppressed source viral load**: the HIV transmission risk is negligible, but most Ugandan protocols still give PEP if there is any doubt or the VL result is old.
4. **Common student mistakes**:
   - Wearing gloves from patient to patient ("I'm wearing gloves, so it's fine").
   - Skipping moment 2 because "I'll wear sterile gloves anyway".
   - Wearing a mask below the nose. Not using eye protection at deliveries.
   - Recapping needles.
   - Delaying reporting a needle-stick until the end of the shift, or not reporting at all ("it was only a suture needle").
   - Leaving instruments in chlorine all day, or skipping cleaning and "sterilising" dirty instruments.
   - Throwing gloves into the black bin.

### Putting it together

**A. Model summary of an exposure:** "A 23-year-old student sustained a **deep percutaneous injury** with a **hollow-bore cannula needle, visibly bloody**, at 14:10, from a woman of **unknown HIV status**. First aid done. Worker HIV-negative at baseline, **3 doses of HBV vaccine, anti-HBs unknown**. Assessment: **significant exposure, source unknown**. Plan: **TLD 1 tablet daily for 28 days, first dose now (within 2 hours)**, source HIV and HBsAg tests with consent, worker anti-HBs, follow-up at 2 weeks, HIV tests at 6 weeks and 3 months, incident report."

**Worked example 1: the source result comes back.** The student above started TLD at 14:40. At 16:00 the source's rapid HIV test is **reactive** and she tells you she stopped ART a year ago; the HBsAg is **negative**. The student's anti-HBs is later **25 mIU/mL**. *Interpretation:* a high-risk HIV exposure (hollow needle, visible blood, **unsuppressed source**); **protected against hepatitis B**. *Plan:* **complete 28 days of TLD**, adherence support, review at 2 weeks, HIV tests at **6 weeks and 3 months**, condoms and no blood donation until the 3-month test is negative. No HBIG needed. Also link the **source woman back to HIV care** (restart ART, check viral load) and make an eMTCT plan for her baby.

**B. Model summary of a suspected outbreak:** "In the last 3 weeks, **5 women** developed **SSIs after CS** on our ward, compared with about 1 a month usually. **4 of 5 pus cultures grew ESBL *Klebsiella***. Onsets were on days 4–6. All were operated in theatre 2."

**Worked example 2: interpreting the outbreak investigation.**

- **Case definition**: "Any woman with a superficial, deep or organ/space SSI within 30 days of a CS done in our hospital from 1 to 21 March."
- **Line list**: name, age, date of surgery, theatre, surgeon, antibiotic and its timing, labour duration, onset day, culture result, outcome.
- **Findings of the audit**: antibiotic given **after cord clamping** in 4 of 5 cases; the theatre 2 autoclave has **no indicator tape in stock** for 2 weeks; chlorine buckets are made **weekly**; hand rub is missing from the post-op ward.
- *Interpretation:* more cases than expected, clustered in place and time, with the **same organism**: a likely **outbreak** with several system failures (prophylaxis timing, unverified sterilisation, weak decontamination and hand hygiene).
- *Control measures:* treat each woman (drain, culture-guided antibiotics); **contact precautions and cohorting**; **antibiotic before incision** at every CS (make it part of the Time out); stop using theatre 2's autoclave until verified, with indicator tape and a spore test; **fresh chlorine daily**; alcohol rub at every bed; **feed back** the results to staff; continue **SSI surveillance** (post-discharge follow-up to 30 days) to confirm the outbreak is over.

## 8. Complications and Troubleshooting

### First 5 minutes after an exposure (needle-stick, cut or splash)

1. **Make the patient safe.** Say "sharp injury", hand over or finish the critical step (e.g. clamp a bleeder), and put the sharp in the **safety box**.
2. **First aid now**: let a percutaneous wound **bleed freely**, **do not squeeze or suck**, and **wash with soap and running water**. Flush eyes or mouth with **plenty of clean water or saline for 10–15 minutes**. No bleach or alcohol on the wound.
3. **Note the time.** PEP works best **within 2 hours** and must start **within 72 hours**.
4. **Report immediately** to the in-charge or IPC focal person. Do not wait until the end of the shift.
5. **Keep the source patient identified** (name and bed) so that consent and testing can be done quickly.

### Needle-stick / sharps injury or splash: full algorithm

1. **First aid immediately**:
   - **Percutaneous (needle-stick, cut)**: let the wound **bleed freely**, **do not squeeze or suck**, and **wash with soap and running water** for several minutes. Don't use bleach, alcohol or other caustics on the wound.
   - **Mucous membrane (eye, mouth)**: **flush with plenty of clean water or saline** for 10–15 minutes. Remove contact lenses. Don't use soap in the eye.
   - **Intact skin**: wash with soap and water. No PEP needed.
2. **Report immediately** to the ward in-charge or IPC focal person, and **document** the incident.
3. **Assess the exposure risk**: the type (percutaneous or mucous membrane is significant; intact skin is not) and the material (blood, bloody fluid, liquor, vaginal secretions are infectious; urine, faeces or vomit without visible blood are low risk).
4. **Assess the source** (with informed consent): **rapid HIV test**, **HBsAg**, HCV antibody. Check records for known status and **viral load**. **Do not delay PEP while waiting for source results.**
5. **Assess the exposed worker**: **baseline HIV test** (with counselling), HBsAg and anti-HBs where available, HBV vaccination history, **pregnancy test** if relevant, creatinine if available (not required before the first dose).
6. **Start PEP as soon as possible**, ideally within **2 hours**, **no later than 72 hours**, if the exposure is significant and the source is HIV-positive or of unknown status.
7. **Hepatitis B**: see the table below.
8. **Counsel**: adherence (28 days), side effects, **condom use and no blood donation until the 3-month test is negative**, and psychological support.
9. **Follow up**: review at 2 weeks (adherence, side effects), and **repeat HIV testing at about 6 weeks and 3 months** after the exposure (some protocols add 6 months, especially with HCV co-exposure). Check HCV and HBV markers per exposure.
10. **Prevent recurrence**: review how it happened (a root-cause analysis without blame).

### HIV PEP drug table (Uganda / WHO)

| Drug | Dose | Route | Frequency | Duration | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **TDF/3TC/DTG (TLD)**: tenofovir DF 300 mg + lamivudine 300 mg + dolutegravir 50 mg | **1 tablet** | Oral | **Once daily** | **28 days** | **Preferred regimen for adults and adolescents** (WHO 2024; Uganda consolidated guidelines). Safe in pregnancy. Give the **full 28-day supply** at the first visit. Take DTG 2 h before or 6 h after iron, calcium or antacids |
| TDF/3TC + **ATV/r** (atazanavir 300 mg / ritonavir 100 mg) | 1 tablet TDF/3TC + ATV/r | Oral | Once daily | 28 days | Alternative if DTG is unavailable or not tolerated |
| TDF/3TC + LPV/r | LPV/r 400/100 mg | Oral | Twice daily | 28 days | Alternative (more side effects) |

> [!DRUG]
> **PEP is an emergency.** Don't wait for the source's result, or for a doctor to "see you tomorrow". Every Ugandan hospital and ART clinic, and many casualty departments, keep **PEP starter packs**. The 72-hour limit is absolute. Beyond it, PEP is not recommended, but still test and follow up.

> [!UGANDA]
> The Uganda **National IPC Guidelines** (MoH, 2013, with later editions) and the national health-care waste management guidelines set the standards you'll be examined on: **0.5% chlorine** for decontamination, the **autoclave cycle**, and the **colour-coded bins**. The national **PEP policy** makes PEP free at all public ART sites. Every hospital should have an **IPC committee and focal person**. Ask on the ward who yours is, because that's who you report a needle-stick to. Uganda's PEP regimen (TLD) matches WHO 2024; check that the HIV follow-up testing schedule in your unit matches the current consolidated guidelines [VERIFY: Uganda follow-up test timings may differ from WHO 2024].

### Hepatitis B after exposure

| Exposed worker | Source HBsAg positive (or unknown, high risk) | Source HBsAg negative |
|---|---|---|
| **Unvaccinated / incomplete** | **HBV vaccine dose 1 now** (complete 0, 1, 6 months) **+ HBIG** (hepatitis B immunoglobulin) as soon as possible, ideally within 24 h (up to 7 days) where available | Start or complete vaccination |
| **Vaccinated, known responder** (anti-HBs ≥10 mIU/mL) | No treatment | No treatment |
| **Vaccinated, non-responder** | HBIG + revaccination (or HBIG ×2) | Consider revaccination |
| **Vaccinated, response unknown** | Test anti-HBs. If <10, give HBIG + a vaccine booster | No treatment |

**Hepatitis C**: there is no vaccine or PEP. Test at baseline and follow up (HCV RNA at about 3–6 weeks or antibody at 3–6 months). Refer for **direct-acting antiviral treatment** if infection occurs.

### A woman with a suspected ward infection (first 5 minutes)

1. **Assess her first, not the paperwork**: airway, breathing, pulse, BP, RR, temperature, SpO₂, conscious level, urine output. **Sepsis warning signs** include temperature **≥38.0 °C or <36 °C**, pulse **>100/min**, RR **>20/min**, SBP **<90 mmHg**, confusion, or urine **<30 mL/h**. Use your unit's obstetric early-warning chart.
2. If she is septic: **cultures (blood, pus, urine) if they do not delay treatment**, then **IV antibiotics within the first hour** and IV fluids. Call a senior.
3. **Apply the right precautions straight away**: **contact precautions** (gloves and apron, dedicated equipment) for a draining wound or a suspected MDR organism; isolate or cohort if possible.
4. **Ask "is she the only one?"** If there have been other similar infections on the ward recently, it may be an **outbreak**: tell the ward in-charge and **IPC focal person today**.
5. **Red flag for VHF**: fever with unexplained bleeding plus travel to or contact with an outbreak area (Ebola/Marburg). **Isolate, full VHF PPE, notify the district at once.**

### Managing a suspected HAI outbreak (e.g. cluster of post-CS SSIs)

1. **Recognise** it: more cases than expected in a place and time.
2. **Notify** the IPC focal person and committee and the ward leadership.
3. **Treat** patients (culture-guided antibiotics; drainage).
4. **Investigate**: case definition, line list, cultures (pus, blood), and **audit practices**: antibiotic timing, skin prep, theatre cleaning, the autoclave log and indicators, reused items, multi-dose vials, hand-rub availability.
5. **Control**: fix the breach (e.g. repair the autoclave, retrain staff, single-use vials), and cohort or isolate if MDR organisms are present.
6. **Monitor and feed back**: SSI surveillance and hand hygiene compliance audits.

### Suspected Ebola/Marburg (VHF) in a pregnant woman (Uganda-specific)

1. **Isolate** her immediately in a designated area. **Limit staff** to trained personnel with **full VHF PPE**.
2. **Notify** the district surveillance focal person or MoH hotline immediately.
3. **Minimise invasive procedures**. Any delivery must be conducted in full PPE with safe disposal of all waste (**red**, highly infectious).
4. Monitor all exposed staff for 21 days.

(VHF in pregnancy carries very high maternal and near-total fetal mortality, and delivery is a major exposure event for health workers.)

### Troubleshooting common IPC failures

| Problem | Why it matters | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| No running water | Handwashing impossible | Alcohol rub; a tippy-tap or bucket with a tap; report |
| No alcohol rub | Moments missed | Locally produced WHO formulation; soap and water |
| Autoclave broken | Critical items cannot be sterilised | HLD by boiling for semi-critical items; send critical items to a working sterile service; do not use unverified packs |
| Chlorine made weekly or uncovered | Weak, ineffective solution | Make **daily**; covered, opaque containers |
| Instruments left in chlorine all day | Corrosion; damaged instruments | **10 minutes** only, then clean |
| Safety box full | Needle-stick risk to staff and cleaners | Close at three-quarters; replace; never compress |
| Gloves out of stock | Exposure risk | Prioritise sterile gloves for VE and delivery; report through the IPC committee |

## 9. Documentation and Presenting Findings

### What to document

- **Exposure incident report**: date and **time**, what happened (device, depth, visible blood, glove worn), body fluid, source (identified, consent obtained, test results), first aid given, worker baseline tests, **time PEP started**, HBV plan, follow-up dates, and a blame-free root-cause note.
- **Instrument processing**: every autoclave cycle in the **logbook**, indicator tape on every pack, each pack labelled with the **date and "sterilised by"**.
- **Outbreak**: the case definition, the line list, the audit findings, the control measures and the feedback given.
- **Waste**: bins labelled with the **ward and date**.

### Model presentation (needle-stick incident, reporting to your supervisor)

> "Doctor, I need to report an occupational exposure. At 02:15, while repairing a second-degree tear on a 24-year-old P2 whose HIV status is positive (on TLD, last viral load 6 months ago undetectable), I sustained a **percutaneous injury with a solid suture needle through my glove**, with visible bleeding. I immediately let it bleed and washed it with soap and running water. My last HIV test was negative 3 months ago, and I had 3 doses of hepatitis B vaccine in first year but don't know my antibody level. I'm asking for **PEP (TLD for 28 days) now**, a baseline HIV test, anti-HBs, and an incident report."

| 8. Thumbs | Rotational rubbing of each thumb | Yes |
| 9. Fingertips | Rotational rubbing of fingertips in the opposite palm | Yes |
| 10. Dry | Continues until dry (20–30 s); does not wipe or wave dry | Yes |

**Station 2: "Don and doff PPE for a delivery / isolation patient"**

| Step | Done well when | Critical? |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Hand hygiene | Done first | Yes |
| 2. Gown/apron | On and tied | No |
| 3. Mask | On, nose bridge moulded, under the chin (seal check for N95) | Yes |
| 4. Eye protection | Goggles/face shield on | No |
| 5. Gloves | On, over the gown cuffs | No |
| 6. Gloves off first | Glove-to-glove, skin-to-skin technique | Yes |
| 7. Hand hygiene | After glove removal | Yes |
| 8. Eye protection off | Removed from behind | No |
| 9. Gown off | Touching the inside only, rolled inside out | Yes |
| 10. Mask off | By the ties or elastics, without touching the front | Yes |
| 11. Hand hygiene | At the end | Yes |
| 12. Disposal | PPE into the correct (yellow) bin | No |

**Station 3: "Process this used delivery set" / "Make 0.5% chlorine"**

| Step | Done well when | Critical? |
|---|---|---|
| Decontaminate | Utility gloves; instruments open and submerged in 0.5% chlorine for 10 min | Yes |
| Clean | Under water, brush, detergent, hinges opened; rinse; dry | Yes |
| HLD or sterilise | Boil 20 min from the rolling boil, or autoclave 121 °C at 106 kPa for 20/30 min | Yes |
| Store | Dry, covered, labelled, off the floor | No |
| Chlorine | States the JIK dilution (1:6 for 3.5% JIK) and the formula | Yes |

**Station 4: "Your colleague has just had a needle-stick"**

| Step | Done well when | Critical? |
|---|---|---|
| First aid | Bleed freely, no squeezing, soap and running water | Yes |
| Report | Immediately, to the in-charge or IPC focal person | Yes |
| Assess | Exposure type and fluid; source status and viral load | Yes |
| Baseline tests | Worker HIV, HBsAg, anti-HBs, pregnancy test; source tests with consent | No |

| Counselling and follow-up | Adherence, condoms, no blood donation; tests at 6 weeks and 3 months | No |

## 11. Key Points

### High-yield points

- **Standard precautions for every patient**; hand hygiene at the **5 moments** is the single most effective measure.
- **Gloves never replace hand hygiene.**
- **D-C-H/S-S**: decontaminate (0.5% chlorine, 10 min), clean, HLD or sterilise, store. You cannot sterilise dirt.
- **Spaulding**: critical → sterilise; semi-critical → HLD; non-critical → clean.
- **Bins**: black general, yellow infectious, red highly infectious, brown pharmaceutical; sharps in a safety box closed at three-quarters.
- **Needle-stick**: bleed, wash, report, PEP (TLD × 28 days) ideally within 2 h, never after 72 h; never wait for the source result.
- **Hepatitis B** is the biggest occupational threat and is vaccine-preventable.
- **Outbreak**: more cases than expected; notify, define, line-list, culture, audit, control, feed back.
- SSI isolates in Uganda are often **ESBL Klebsiella/E. coli and MRSA**; culture before antibiotics.

### Numbers & doses

| Item | Value |
|---|---|
| Alcohol hand rub | **20–30 seconds** |
| Soap and water handwash | **40–60 seconds** (when hands are visibly soiled, after the toilet, spores/diarrhoea) |
| Decontamination | **0.5% chlorine for 10 minutes** (no longer: corrosion) |
| 0.5% chlorine from 3.5% JIK | **1 part JIK + 6 parts water** |
| 0.5% chlorine from HTH (70%) | About **7 g per litre** |
| Chlorine for HLD by soak | **0.1%** (JIK 1 : 34) for 20 min in general IP manuals; some MVA guidance uses **0.5% for 20 min**. Follow the unit protocol |
| HLD by boiling or steaming | **20 minutes** (timed from the rolling boil) |
| Glutaraldehyde 2% | **20 min = HLD**; **8–10 h = sterilisation** |
| Autoclave | **121 °C, 106 kPa (15 lb/in²), 20 min unwrapped / 30 min wrapped** |
| Dry heat | **170 °C for 60 min** (or 160 °C for 120 min) |
| HLD items storage | Covered HLD container, up to about **1 week** |
| Safety box | Close at **three-quarters full** |
| Blood spill | Cover with **0.5% chlorine for about 10 min** |
| HAI definition | Appears **≥48 h** after admission, or within **30 days** of surgery (**90 days** with an implant) |
| Droplets vs airborne | Droplets **>5 µm**, travel about **1–2 m**; airborne **<5 µm** (TB, measles, varicella) need an **N95** |
| CS antibiotic prophylaxis | Within **60 min before the skin incision** |
| VE frequency in active first stage | About **every 4 hours** unless indicated |
| HIV PEP | **TLD (TDF 300 / 3TC 300 / DTG 50) 1 tablet daily for 28 days**; start ideally within **2 h**, never after **72 h** |
| PEP follow-up | Review at **2 weeks**; HIV test at about **6 weeks and 3 months** |
| Transmission risk after needle-stick | **HBV up to 30%** (HBeAg+), **HCV about 1.8%**, **HIV about 0.3%** (mucous membrane about 0.09%) |
| HBIG | As soon as possible, ideally within **24 h** (up to 7 days) |
| Protective anti-HBs | **≥10 mIU/mL** |
| HBV vaccine schedule | **0, 1 and 6 months** |
| VHF contacts | Monitor for **21 days** |

### Exam traps

- **"Gloves replace hand hygiene."** No. Clean hands before (if a moment applies) and **always after** removing gloves.
- **"Wait for the source's HIV result before starting PEP."** No. **Start PEP now**; stop it if the source is negative.
- **"Squeeze the wound and pour bleach or spirit on it."** No. Let it **bleed freely**, wash with **soap and running water**.
- **"PEP can still be started on day 4."** No. Beyond **72 hours** PEP is not recommended; test and follow up instead.
- **"An HIV-positive health worker gets PEP."** No. She needs full **ART** and linkage to care.
- **"Leave instruments in chlorine overnight to be extra safe."** No. **10 minutes** only; longer corrodes the steel.
- **"Just sterilise the dirty instruments."** No. **Clean first**: you cannot sterilise dirt.
- **"Use alcohol rub on visibly soiled hands."** No. Use **soap and water** when hands are visibly soiled.

### Questions seniors ask

**Viva questions**

1. **List the components of standard precautions.** Hand hygiene, PPE by risk, respiratory hygiene and cough etiquette, safe injections and sharps, environmental cleaning, linen and waste management, reprocessing of equipment, and patient placement.
2. **Name the 5 moments.** Before touching a patient; before a clean/aseptic procedure; after body-fluid exposure risk; after touching a patient; after touching patient surroundings.
3. **Why decontaminate instruments before cleaning?** To inactivate HIV, HBV and HCV so that the person cleaning them is protected.
4. **Autoclave parameters?** 121 °C at 106 kPa (15 lb/in²), 20 minutes unwrapped and 30 minutes wrapped.
5. **How do you prepare 0.5% chlorine from 3.5% JIK?** 1 part JIK to 6 parts water.
6. **Risk of HIV transmission after a needle-stick from an HIV-positive source?** About 0.3% (mucous membrane about 0.09%), and much lower if the source is virally suppressed or PEP is taken.
7. **PEP regimen, timing and duration?** TDF/3TC/DTG once daily for 28 days, started as soon as possible (ideally within 2 h, no later than 72 h).
8. **Which colour bin for a blood-soaked swab? For an expired drug?** Yellow (infectious). Brown (pharmaceutical).
9. **When must you use soap and water instead of alcohol rub?** When hands are visibly soiled or contaminated with body fluids, after using the toilet, and with spore-forming organisms (e.g. *C. difficile*) or during diarrhoeal outbreaks.

**On the ward round**

1. **You have just had a needle-stick. What do you do, in order?** Make the patient safe; let it **bleed**, **wash with soap and water**; **report**; assess the exposure and source; baseline HIV test; **TLD within 2 h (max 72 h) for 28 days**; hepatitis B plan; counselling; follow-up tests at 6 weeks and 3 months.
2. **Which exposures do NOT need PEP?** **Intact skin** contact, and contact with urine, faeces, saliva or vomit **without visible blood**; and any exposure more than **72 hours** earlier.
3. **The source is HIV-positive with an undetectable viral load last month. Do you still give PEP?** The risk is very low, but most Ugandan protocols still give PEP when there is **any doubt** (an old result, poor adherence). Discuss with the PEP provider; don't simply refuse it.
4. **How do you recognise an outbreak?** **More cases than expected** in a particular place and time, often with the **same organism**. Notify the IPC focal person, write a **case definition**, make a **line list**, audit practices, control, and monitor.
5. **Why are routine environmental swabs not recommended?** They are expensive and hard to interpret. Take them **only when the epidemiology points to a source**.
6. **What is the difference between decontamination, HLD and sterilisation?** Decontamination (0.5% chlorine 10 min) makes items safe to handle; **HLD** kills all organisms except some spores; **sterilisation** kills everything, including spores.
7. **A health worker had 3 HBV vaccine doses but her anti-HBs is 4 mIU/mL; the source is HBsAg-positive. What do you give?** She is a **non-responder**: give **HBIG** and **revaccinate** (or HBIG twice), as in the hepatitis B table.

### Mnemonics

- **5 Moments = "2 Befores, 3 Afters"**: Before patient, Before aseptic; After fluids, After patient, After surroundings.
- **Instrument processing "D-C-H/S-S"**: **D**econtaminate (0.5% chlorine, 10 min), **C**lean, **H**LD or **S**terilise, **S**tore.
- **Autoclave "121-106-20-30"**: 121 °C, 106 kPa, 20 min unwrapped, 30 min wrapped.
- **HLD "Boil 20 from the bubble"**: start the clock at the rolling boil.
- **JIK "1 + 6 = 0.5"**: 1 part 3.5% JIK plus 6 parts water gives 0.5% chlorine.
- **Spaulding "Sterile tissue gets Sterilised; Mucosa gets HLD; Skin gets cleaned."**
- **Donning "Gown, Mask, Goggles, Gloves: G-M-G-G"**; doffing **reverses the dirtiest first: Gloves, Goggles, Gown, Mask.**
- **Needle-stick "BLEED-WASH-REPORT-PEP"** within **2 hours ideally, 72 hours maximum**.
- **Risk "Rule of 3s"**: HBV **30%**, HCV **3%** (actually about 1.8%, so remember it as "under 3"), HIV **0.3%**.
- **Bin colours "Black is Benign, Yellow is Yucky, Red is Really dangerous, Brown is for Bottles of drugs."**
