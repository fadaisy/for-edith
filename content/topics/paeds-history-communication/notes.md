## 1. Purpose and Indications

In paediatrics the history is taken **with and through a caregiver**: usually the mother, sometimes a father, grandmother, older sibling or guardian, and increasingly the child or adolescent themselves. It is longer than an adult history because a child's illness can only be understood against **how they were born, how they have been fed, whether they are protected by vaccines, how they are growing and developing, whether they have been exposed to HIV and TB, and the family and social world they live in**.

**Purpose**

- **Make the diagnosis.** The history usually gives you the diagnosis; the examination and tests confirm it.
- **Decide urgency.** The IMCI danger signs and the age of the child decide how fast you act and whether to refer.
- **Protect others.** In Uganda the history also tells you whether siblings are at risk (TB, HIV, measles).
- **Make a plan the family can follow.** It tells you what the family can afford, how far they live from care and whether the child can go home safely.
- **Build trust.** Good communication is how you earn a family's trust on the ward, and it is examined in every OSCE.

> [!PEARL]
> The best paediatric historians **listen first** ("Tell me what has been happening with Brian"), **then structure** (IMCI danger signs, then the system history, then the paediatric extras), and **finish by checking understanding** ("What worries you most about him?"). Parents usually know when something is seriously wrong: take their concern seriously.

> [!UGANDA]
> On Ugandan wards, histories are often taken in **Luganda, Runyankole, Lusoga or another local language**, through an interpreter (sometimes a nurse or a relative). The **Child Health Card** (immunisation, growth chart, vitamin A and deworming record) and any **referral note** from the HC III/IV are part of the history: always ask to see them. Ask about **traditional or herbal treatment** and **"ebiino" (false teeth) extraction** in infants with diarrhoea or fever, which still causes bleeding, sepsis and tetanus in some communities.

**Indications**

- **Every child seen in clinic or on the ward** needs a history; its depth depends on urgency.
- **Emergency (ETAT)**: a short, focused **SAMPLE** history during resuscitation, and the full history once the child is stable (section 2).
- **Follow-up visits**: an interval history (what has changed, adherence, new symptoms, growth since the last visit).
- **Adolescents (10–19 years)**: a history with the parent plus a confidential **HEEADSSS** psychosocial history with the young person alone (section 5, step 15).
- **Communication tasks that grow out of the history**: explaining a diagnosis, breaking bad news, the angry parent, child protection, discharge counselling and presenting to colleagues (sections 8 and 9).

## 2. Contraindications and Precautions

There is no contraindication to taking a history, but there are situations where the **full** history must wait or must be taken differently.

- **Emergency signs (ETAT): treat first.** If the child has emergency signs, **treat first**, then take a focused history (**SAMPLE**: **S**ymptoms, **A**llergies, **M**edications, **P**ast illness, **L**ast meal, **E**vents) while resuscitating, and the full history once stable. A colleague can take the SAMPLE history while you treat.
- **Sensitive history**: **do not take a sensitive history** (sexual activity, abuse, HIV disclosure) in front of others without consent, or from a child who is too distressed: arrange a safe, private setting.
- **The caregiver is not the usual carer** (e.g. a neighbour brought the child): record this, take what history you can, and get the full history from the parent later by phone or at the next visit.
- **Interpreters**: use a trained interpreter where possible. **Avoid using a sibling (or another child) for sensitive topics.**
- **Confidentiality**: never ask the mother's HIV status, or an adolescent about sex, in front of other patients or relatives. Ask privately and respectfully.
- **Child protection concerns**: listen and record; **do not confront or accuse** the family yourself (section 8).

## 3. Relevant Anatomy and Normal Values

Anatomy is not applicable to history taking. This section holds the **normal reference values** that the history is judged against: age bands, duration thresholds, feeding norms, the UNEPI schedule and developmental milestones.

### Age bands and duration thresholds

| Item | Value | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Young infant (IMCI) | **0–2 months** | Different danger signs and fast-breathing cut-off (≥60/min) |
| Adolescence (WHO) | **10–19 years** | See alone for part of the visit; HEEADSSS |
| Age of majority (Uganda) | **18 years** | Consent, defilement law |
| Correct for prematurity | Until **2 years** of age | Subtract the weeks born early before judging milestones and growth |
| Prolonged cough | **>2 weeks** | Think TB |
| Persistent diarrhoea | **≥14 days** | HIV, malnutrition |
| Prolonged fever | **≥7 days** | Typhoid, TB, UTI, abscess |
| Prolonged rupture of membranes | **>18 hours** | Neonatal sepsis risk |
| Acute illness | **<2 weeks** | Acute vs persistent pathway |

### Feeding norms (WHO, UCG 2023)

| Item | Norm |
|---|---|
| Breastfeeding initiation | Within **1 hour** of birth; colostrum given; no pre-lacteal feeds |
| Exclusive breastfeeding | **0–6 months** |
| Breastfeeding frequency in young infants | **≥8–12 times in 24 hours**, including night feeds |
| Complementary feeding | From **6 months** (WHO 2023 guideline on complementary feeding) |
| Meal frequency | **6–8 months: 2–3 meals**; **9–23 months: 3–4 meals + 1–2 snacks** |
| Dietary diversity | At least **5 of 8 food groups** in the past day |
| Animal-source foods | Daily where possible; limit sugary drinks |

### UNEPI immunisation schedule (2022, plus 2025 malaria vaccine)

| Age | Vaccines |
|---|---|
| Birth | BCG, OPV0, Hepatitis B birth dose |
| 6 weeks | OPV1, DPT-HepB-Hib1, PCV1, Rota1, IPV1 |
| 10 weeks | OPV2, DPT-HepB-Hib2, PCV2, Rota2 |
| 14 weeks | OPV3, DPT-HepB-Hib3, PCV3, IPV2 (+ Rota3 on the 2025/26 MoH poster, brand-dependent) |
| 6, 7, 8 and 18 months | Malaria vaccine (R21) in districts where it has been introduced [VERIFY: current UNEPI malaria vaccine schedule and districts] |
| 9 months | Measles–rubella 1, yellow fever |
| 18 months | Measles–rubella 2 |
| 10 years (girls) | HPV |

Also recorded on the card: **vitamin A** (from 6 months, 6-monthly) and **deworming** (from 1 year, 6-monthly).

> [!UGANDA]
> **Rotavirus doses**: UCG 2023 lists rotavirus at **6 and 10 weeks**; the **2025/26 MoH immunisation poster** shows a third dose at **14 weeks**, depending on the vaccine brand in use. Check the card and the poster on your ward. [VERIFY: current rotavirus brand and number of doses in UNEPI]

### Developmental milestones

Cover **four domains**: **gross motor**, **fine motor and vision**, **speech, language and hearing**, **social, emotional and self-care**. **Correct for prematurity** up to 2 years.

| Age | Gross motor | Fine motor / vision | Speech / hearing | Social |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **6 weeks** | Lifts head briefly prone | Follows face/light | Startles to sound | **Social smile** |
| **3–4 months** | **Head control**; lifts chest prone | Hands open, reaches; holds rattle | Coos, turns to voice | Laughs |
| **6–8 months** | **Sits without support** (6–8 m); rolls | Palmar grasp, transfers objects | Babbles ("baba") | Stranger anxiety (7–9 m) |
| **9–10 months** | Crawls, pulls to stand | **Pincer grasp** (9–12 m) | "Mama/dada" non-specific | Waves bye-bye, plays peek-a-boo |
| **12 months** | **Walks with/without support** (12–15 m) | Neat pincer, releases objects | **2–3 words** with meaning | Drinks from cup, points |
| **18 months** | Walks well, runs, climbs stairs holding on | Tower of 3–4 cubes, scribbles | **6–20 words**; points to body parts | Feeds self with spoon, symbolic play |
| **2 years** | Runs, kicks a ball | Tower of 6–7, copies a line | **2-word phrases**, 50+ words | Parallel play, partly toilet trained |
| **3 years** | Jumps, stands on one foot briefly, rides tricycle | Copies a circle, tower of 9 | Short sentences, knows name, age | Plays with others, dry by day |
| **4 years** | Hops, goes down stairs one foot per step | Copies a cross/square, draws a person | Tells stories, asks "why" | Dresses with help, has friends |
| **5 years** | Skips | Copies a triangle | Fluent speech | Understands rules, dresses alone |

**Developmental red flags (refer)**: no social smile by **8 weeks**; no head control by **4 months**; not sitting by **9 months**; **not walking by 18 months**; no words by **18 months**; no 2-word phrases by **2.5 years**; hand preference before 12–18 months (suggests weakness of the other side); **loss of any previously acquired skill**; parental concern about hearing or vision.

## 4. Equipment and Preparation

### Equipment

| Item | Why |
|---|---|
| **A quiet, private space** with chairs at the same level | Trust, confidentiality, reduces fear |
| **Toys, picture book or a pen torch** to engage the child | A calm child is easier to observe and examine |
| **Child Health Card / Mother–Child Health Passport** | Immunisation record, growth chart, birth weight, vitamin A and deworming |
| **Referral note and previous discharge forms** | Drugs given, test results, previous diagnoses |
| **Clerking sheet** (or structured notebook) | Completeness |
| **WHO growth charts** (weight-for-age, length/height-for-age, weight-for-length/height, head circumference) and **MUAC tape** | Plot growth while you take the history |
| **A watch with a second hand** (or phone timer) | Count RR while the child is calm on the mother's lap, before you examine |
| **Interpreter** (trained where possible) | Accurate history; avoid using a sibling for sensitive topics |
| **Tissues, water** | Distressed parents |

### Preparation, consent and positioning

1. **Read the triage sheet and referral note**; check for **emergency signs** first. If present, treat first (section 2).
2. **Hand hygiene** before you approach.
3. **Setting**: a quiet, private space; sit at the same level as the caregiver; silence your phone.
4. **Consent**: explain who you are, what you will do and how long it will take; ask permission to take notes. For a sensitive history, ask permission before each sensitive area. For adolescents, explain **confidentiality and its limits** at the start (section 5, step 15).
5. **Positioning**: let the child stay **on the parent's lap**; give a toy. Get down to the child's level.
6. **Exposure**: none is needed for the history, but **observe** the child while you talk (step 1 below). Hutchison: the examination begins with the history.
7. **Interpreter**: brief them first (confidentiality, word-for-word translation, no summarising); speak to the caregiver, not to the interpreter.

## 5. Procedure Step by Step

The order below follows the structure of **Hutchison's Clinical Methods (24th edition)** for the history, with the paediatric additions placed after the past medical history: **presenting complaint → history of presenting complaint → past medical history → birth (pregnancy, delivery, neonatal) → feeding → immunisation → development → drugs and allergies → family history → social history → systems review → closing** [VERIFY: exact sequence of the paediatric history sections in Hutchison 24th edition]. In a timed station, cover every heading briefly rather than one heading in depth.

### Step 1. Introduction, informant and observation

1. Wash your hands. **Greet** the caregiver and the child by name; introduce yourself ("I am Edith, a medical student working with Dr ..."), and explain what you will do and how long it will take.
2. **Establish who the informant is**: name, relationship to the child, whether they live with the child and how well they know the illness. Ask permission to take notes.
3. Make the child comfortable: let them stay on the parent's lap; give a toy.
4. **Observe while you talk**, before touching: colour (pallor, cyanosis, jaundice), breathing (rate, indrawing, grunting, nasal flaring), alertness and interaction with the mother, posture, wasting or oedema, dysmorphic features, skin rashes, how the child and carer interact (warmth, fear). Count the **respiratory rate** for a full minute while the child is calm.

### Step 2. Biodata

Name, **age** (date of birth: in months for infants, exact days for neonates), sex, address (village, parish, district: tells you **malaria transmission**, distance and referral route), religion and tribe (context, not stereotypes), **informant and relationship**, date of admission, inpatient number.

### Step 3. Presenting complaint(s)

- In the **caregiver's own words**, with **duration**: "Fever for 3 days, cough for 2 days, convulsions once this morning."
- Order them **chronologically** (or by importance).

### Step 4. History of presenting illness (HPI)

1. Start **open**: "Tell me what has been happening since he became unwell."
2. Then **clarify each symptom** (onset, duration, progression, severity, pattern, associated features, relieving/aggravating factors). For pain, adapt **SOCRATES**; in young children ask how they show pain (crying, refusing to walk, pulling the ear).
3. **Always ask the IMCI general danger signs**: **unable to drink or breastfeed? vomits everything? convulsions? lethargic or unconscious?**
4. **IMCI main symptoms**: cough or difficult breathing; diarrhoea (duration, blood, dehydration signs); fever (duration, malaria risk, measles in last 3 months); ear problem; and in young infants, feeding problems, jaundice, umbilical redness, skin pustules.
5. **Systems relevant to the complaint**: e.g. fever → rash, neck stiffness, urinary symptoms, ear discharge, joint swelling.
6. **What has been done so far**: where they went (drug shop, clinic, traditional healer), **what treatment was given** (names, doses, how many days), tests done and results, and the response.
7. **Effect on the child**: feeding, urine output (wet nappies), activity, sleep, school.
8. **Pertinent negatives**: say what is absent (e.g. "no convulsions, no blood in stool").
9. **Contacts**: anyone else sick at home (TB cough, measles, diarrhoea), recent travel.

### Step 5. Past medical history

- Previous illnesses and **admissions** (dates, diagnoses, treatment), **surgeries**, **blood transfusions** (number, when: think sickle cell, HIV, haemolysis), chronic illnesses (asthma, epilepsy, sickle cell disease, heart disease, HIV, TB), previous TB treatment.
- **Similar episodes** in the past.

### Step 6. Birth (perinatal) history

Essential in children **under 2 years**, and in any child with **developmental or neurological problems**.

| Period | Ask | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| **Antenatal** | Planned pregnancy? Number of **ANC visits**, maternal **HIV, syphilis (RPR), hepatitis B**, malaria and IPTp, **hypertension/pre-eclampsia**, **diabetes**, fever or rash, drugs, alcohol, smoking, herbal medicine, bleeding, twins | Congenital infections, IUGR, prematurity, HIV exposure, fetal alcohol |
| **Labour and delivery** | **Gestation** at birth, **place** (home, HC, hospital) and attendant, **mode** (SVD, assisted, CS and why), duration of labour, prolonged rupture of membranes (>18 h), maternal fever, meconium | Birth asphyxia, neonatal sepsis risk, birth trauma |
| **At birth** | **Birth weight**, **cried immediately?** Needed **resuscitation** (bag and mask?), Apgar if known | Low birth weight, hypoxic-ischaemic encephalopathy |
| **Neonatal period** | **NICU/SCU admission** and why, **jaundice** (phototherapy, exchange transfusion), convulsions, feeding difficulties, infections, oxygen, length of stay, vitamin K, eye ointment, cord care | Cerebral palsy, hearing loss, kernicterus, chronic lung disease |

**HIV exposure** belongs here and in the feeding history: mother's status and when diagnosed, ART and adherence, last viral load, infant prophylaxis, breastfeeding, and **EID (DNA PCR) results**.

### Step 7. Feeding and nutrition history

- **Breastfeeding**: initiated **within 1 hour**? **Colostrum** given? **Pre-lacteal feeds** (water, sugar water, cow's milk, herbs)? **Exclusive breastfeeding** to 6 months? Frequency (≥8–12 times in 24 h in young infants), night feeds, attachment problems, breast problems, maternal HIV status and ART adherence.
- **Replacement feeding**: what milk, how prepared (dilution, clean water, bottles vs cup), how much and how often, cost.
- **Complementary feeding** (WHO 2023 guideline on complementary feeding): started at **6 months**? What foods: porridge (thin or thick?), mashed matooke, beans, groundnut paste, eggs, fish/meat, vegetables and fruit; **meal frequency** (6–8 months: 2–3 meals; 9–23 months: 3–4 meals + 1–2 snacks); **dietary diversity** (at least **5 of 8 food groups** in the past day); **animal-source foods**; sugary drinks.
- **24-hour dietary recall**: "Tell me everything she ate and drank yesterday, from waking up."
- **Feeding during illness**: continued or stopped? Appetite now?
- **Older children**: meals per day, school lunches, food insecurity at home, pica (soil eating: iron deficiency, worms).
- **Growth**: ask about weight loss, clothes becoming loose; **look at the growth chart** on the card.

### Step 8. Immunisation history

1. **Check the Child Health Card**; if no card, ask by site and age ("an injection in the right arm at birth that left a scar", "drops in the mouth", "injections in the thighs at 6, 10 and 14 weeks", "an injection in the arm at 9 months").
2. **Look for the BCG scar** (right upper arm).
3. Compare with the **UNEPI schedule** (section 3).
4. Record **vitamin A** (from 6 months, 6-monthly) and **deworming** (from 1 year, 6-monthly).
5. State the conclusion: "**Fully immunised for age**", "**partially immunised** (missed MR at 9 months)", or "**unknown, no card**". Missed vaccines are given at discharge.

### Step 9. Developmental history

1. Ask: "**When did she first smile at you? Hold her head up? Sit without support? Walk? Say her first words?**"
2. Ask about regression: "**Is there anything he could do before that he can't do now?**" (regression is always a red flag).
3. Cover **four domains**: **gross motor**, **fine motor and vision**, **speech, language and hearing**, **social, emotional and self-care**. Ask about school performance in older children.
4. **Correct for prematurity** up to 2 years (subtract the weeks born early).
5. Compare with the milestones table and red flags in section 3.

### Step 10. Drug and allergy history

Current medicines (including **ART, cotrimoxazole, TB drugs, anticonvulsants**), doses and adherence, recent antibiotics and antimalarials, **herbal and traditional remedies**, and **allergies** (what happened).

### Step 11. Family history

- Parents: **ages, occupation, education, health**, **HIV status and treatment**, alive or dead (cause), relationship (living together? polygamous household?), **consanguinity**.
- Siblings: number, ages, health, **deaths and causes** ("Have you lost any children?" Ask gently: a sibling death from neonatal sepsis, sickle cell or HIV changes your thinking).
- **Inherited and familial conditions**: sickle cell disease (or "a sibling who needed frequent transfusions"), epilepsy, asthma/atopy, diabetes, heart disease, bleeding disorders, deafness.
- **Infectious contacts**: **TB in the household** (anyone coughing for >2 weeks or on TB treatment), measles, hepatitis B.
- Draw a **simple family tree (genogram)** where relevant.

### Step 12. Social history

- **Who lives at home** and who is the main carer when the mother works; who brought the child today.
- **Housing**: number of rooms and people (crowding: TB, pneumonia), **cooking fuel** (charcoal/firewood indoors: pneumonia, asthma, burns), **water source** and treatment, **toilet** type (diarrhoea, worms), **mosquito net use** last night, smoking at home.
- **Income and occupation**, ability to buy food and medicines, health insurance (rare), **distance and transport** to the facility (affects follow-up and when to return).
- **School**: class, performance, absence, bullying.
- **Child's activities and care**: day care, alone at home, older siblings caring.
- **Pets and animals** (rabies, brucellosis from raw milk).
- **Social support** and stresses (separation, domestic violence, alcohol, mental illness in the carer).

### Step 13. Review of systems

A brief run through each system not already covered: general (fever, weight loss, night sweats), skin, eyes, ENT (ear discharge), respiratory, cardiovascular (sweating with feeds, fast breathing, blue spells), gastrointestinal, genitourinary (urine stream, wetting), musculoskeletal, neurological (headache, fits, weakness), endocrine (polyuria, polydipsia).

### Step 14. Closing the history

1. **Summarise** back to the caregiver ("So, Brian has had fever for 3 days and a cough for 2, and this morning he had a fit lasting about 5 minutes ... have I got that right?").
2. Ask **ICE**: "What do you think is causing this? What worries you most? What were you hoping we would do today?"
3. Ask "**Is there anything else** you want to tell me?"
4. Thank them and explain what happens next (examination, tests, the doctor's review).

Throughout: use **open then closed questions**, **signposting** ("Now I am going to ask about his birth"), **summarising**, plain language without jargon, and empathic responses.

### Step 15. The adolescent history: HEEADSSS

Adolescents (10–19 years) should be seen **with the parent** for the main history and **alone** for part of it. Explain **confidentiality and its limits** at the start: "What you tell me stays between us, unless I am worried that you or someone else is at risk of serious harm; if so, I would talk with you first about who needs to know."

HEEADSSS is the expanded form of the original **HEADSS** psychosocial screen; both cover the same ground.

| Domain | Example questions |
|---|---|
| **H**ome | Who do you live with? How do you get on? Have you ever had to leave home? Do you feel safe at home? |
| **E**ducation / employment | Which class? How is school going? Any repeated years, suspensions, bullying? Do you work to earn money? |
| **E**ating | How do you feel about your body/weight? Any dieting, vomiting, skipping meals? Enough food at home? |
| **A**ctivities | What do you do for fun? Friends? Sports? Phone and social media use? |
| **D**rugs | Do any of your friends drink alcohol, smoke, chew miraa (khat) or use marijuana (bhang)? Do you? |
| **S**exuality | Are you in a relationship? Have you ever had sex? Was it consensual? Do you use condoms or contraception? Any STI symptoms? Pregnancy worries? |
| **S**uicide / depression (mood) | How is your mood? Do you ever feel hopeless? Have you ever thought of hurting yourself or ending your life? |
| **S**afety | Has anyone ever hurt you or touched you in a way you did not want? Road safety, violence, online safety |

Start with the **least threatening** domains (Home, Education) and move to the more personal ones, as recommended in the RCH Melbourne adolescent guideline. For adolescents living with HIV, also ask about **disclosure** (do they know their status?), **adherence**, viral load and stigma.

> [!UGANDA]
> Uganda's age of majority is **18 years**; the **Children Act (Cap 59, as amended 2016)** protects children from abuse and requires that their best interests guide decisions [VERIFY: chapter number in the 2023 revised edition of the Laws of Uganda]. Ugandan HIV testing policy allows **adolescents to consent to HIV testing** from a younger age than for other procedures (commonly cited as 12 years when they understand the implications) [VERIFY: age in the current MoH HIV Testing Services policy]. Defilement (sex with a person under 18) is a crime: a sexually active young adolescent may need a protection assessment as well as health care. **Sauti 116** is the national child helpline.

## 6. Findings and Interpretation

### What each part of the history tells you

| Part of the history | Finding | What it means |
|---|---|---|
| **Age** | Febrile 3-week-old | Sepsis, meningitis |
| | Wheezy 6-month-old | Bronchiolitis |
| | Limping 5-year-old | Septic arthritis, sickle cell |
| | 14-year-old with weight loss | TB, HIV, diabetes, eating disorder |
| **Duration** | Acute (<2 weeks) vs persistent | Sets the pathway |
| | **Cough >2 weeks** | TB |
| | **Diarrhoea ≥14 days** | Persistent diarrhoea (HIV, malnutrition) |
| | **Fever ≥7 days** | Typhoid, TB, UTI, abscess |
| **Danger signs** | Any IMCI general danger sign | Decides **urgency and referral** |
| **Treatment already received** | Antimalarials, antibiotics, herbs | Changes interpretation (RDT positive after recent antimalarials, partially treated meningitis) and avoids duplicate dosing |
| **HIV exposure** | Mother positive or unknown | Changes the differential (PCP in a 3-month-old with severe pneumonia; TB; persistent diarrhoea; failure to thrive) and the tests (DNA PCR) |
| **Perinatal history** | Prematurity, asphyxia, jaundice needing exchange | Explains developmental delay, cerebral palsy, deafness and recurrent wheeze in ex-preterms |
| **Feeding history** | Early cessation of breastfeeding, diluted milk, thin porridge, low diversity | Explains malnutrition, anaemia and diarrhoea; the most important part of the history in a child with poor growth |
| **Immunisation** | Missed or unknown doses | Changes the likelihood of measles, pertussis, diphtheria, tetanus, Hib/pneumococcal meningitis and the decision for LP after a febrile seizure |
| **Development** | Delay or regression | Detects disability early and sets expectations |
| **Family history** | Sibling deaths, sickle cell, TB contact | Inherited disease, infectious risk to the child and siblings |
| **Social history** | Crowding, indoor charcoal, no net, food insecurity, distance | Decides whether a plan is realistic (can the family afford RUTF, return for review, store medicines safely?) and detects neglect and abuse |

### Patterns to recognise

- **Fever + convulsions + coma** in a malaria area → cerebral malaria, meningitis, hypoglycaemia.
- **Cough + wheeze in infancy** → bronchiolitis or asthma.
- **Persistent diarrhoea + weight loss + oral thrush** → HIV.

**Worked examples: turning a history into a differential**

- "Fever for 3 weeks, cough, night sweats, weight loss, grandfather coughing for months" → **TB** (with HIV, malnutrition and typhoid as differentials).
- "Wheeze and cough in a 4-month-old, ex-preterm 32 weeks, first episode, coryza, sibling with a cold" → **bronchiolitis**.
- "Sudden screaming episodes, drawing up legs, vomiting, redcurrant jelly stool in a 9-month-old" → **intussusception** (urgent ultrasound and surgical review).

### Bedside tests the history prompts

**Malaria RDT** (fever), **glucose** (convulsions, lethargy, poor feeding), **HemoCue Hb** (pallor, sickle cell), **urine dipstick** (fever without focus, urinary symptoms, oedema), **HIV rapid test** for the mother or child (unknown status), and **plot the growth chart** from the card's previous weights.

### Laboratory tests and imaging the history prompts

| Test | When (from the history) | Expected / abnormal finding | How it changes management |
|---|---|---|---|
| **HIV DNA PCR / rapid test** | Mother HIV-positive or unknown status; recurrent infections, failure to thrive, oral thrush | Positive PCR (<18 months) or antibody (≥18 months) | ART, cotrimoxazole; testing of siblings (HC III and above via hub) |
| **Xpert MTB/RIF Ultra** (sputum, gastric aspirate, stool), **CXR** | Household TB contact, cough >2 weeks, weight loss | MTB detected; hilar nodes | TB treatment or TPT for contacts (HC IV and above for CXR) |
| **Hb electrophoresis / POC sickle test** | Family history of sickle cell, recurrent painful swellings, transfusions | HbSS | Sickle cell clinic, penicillin V, folic acid, hydroxyurea (regional/national referral) |
| **Hearing assessment** | Neonatal jaundice needing exchange, meningitis, speech delay | Hearing loss | Audiology, hearing aids, speech therapy (national referral) |
| **Developmental assessment** (formal tools, e.g. Malawi Developmental Assessment Tool, used in some Ugandan units) | Delayed milestones on history | Domain-specific delay | Physiotherapy, occupational therapy, special education (regional/national) |
| **Thyroid function** | Prolonged neonatal jaundice, constipation, delay, large tongue | Low T4, high TSH | Levothyroxine (national referral) |
| **Stool microscopy** | Chronic diarrhoea, pica, worms seen | Ova, cysts | Antihelminthics |

Link the history to a **targeted examination**: weight, length/height, MUAC, oedema and Z-scores (see General Examination & Growth Assessment), vital signs against age norms, then the system suggested by the history. Confirm historical findings ("he looks yellow") with your own examination.

## 7. Clinical Acumen

### The order of approach: making the history serve the child's safety

**First 5 minutes**

1. **Look before you talk**: triage the child (ETAT). Emergency signs → call for help and treat (airway, oxygen, glucose, fluids, anticonvulsant) while a colleague takes a **SAMPLE** history.
2. If no emergency signs, check **priority signs** (tiny baby <2 months, temperature very high, trauma, pallor, poisoning, pain, respiratory distress, restless/irritable/lethargic, referral, malnutrition, oedema of both feet, burns): these children are assessed next, ahead of the queue.
3. Settle the child on the caregiver's lap; count the respiratory rate before the child is disturbed.

**The focused history checklist** (each item changes something):

- [ ] Informant and their reliability.
- [ ] Presenting complaints with durations.
- [ ] **Danger signs** (changes urgency).
- [ ] **Treatment already given** (changes drugs and tests).
- [ ] **HIV exposure and TB contact** (changes the differential and testing).
- [ ] **Feeding and growth** (changes nutritional management).
- [ ] **Immunisation** (changes differential and discharge plan).
- [ ] **Development** (changes long-term plan).
- [ ] **Social situation** (changes discharge and follow-up).

### Red flags in the history: when to stop and escalate

> [!REDFLAG]
> - **IMCI danger signs**: unable to drink/breastfeed, vomits everything, convulsions, lethargy/unconsciousness.
> - **Young infant (<2 months)**: not feeding well, convulsions, fast breathing (≥60/min), severe chest indrawing, fever ≥37.5°C or temperature <35.5°C, movement only when stimulated.
> - **Regression** of developmental skills.
> - **Bilious (green) vomiting** (malrotation, obstruction), **blood in stool with irritability** (intussusception).
> - **Weight loss, night sweats, cough >2 weeks** with a TB contact.
> - **Child protection concerns**: history that changes or does not explain the injury, delay in seeking care, injury inconsistent with development ("he rolled off the bed" in a 2-week-old with a fracture), repeated injuries, a frightened or withdrawn child, disclosure by the child.
> - **Caregiver distress or mental illness** that affects the child's safety.

### Thinking like a clinician

- **The history comes from someone else**: ask how they know ("Did you see the fit yourself?"), and distinguish observation from interpretation ("he had malaria" may mean "he had fever").
- **Translate local terms carefully**: "*omusujja*" (fever, often equated with malaria), "*ensimbu*" (convulsions), "*ekifuba*" (chest problem/cough), "*ebiino*" (false teeth). Ask what the caregiver means.
- **If X, then think Y**: fever + convulsions + coma in a malaria area → cerebral malaria, meningitis, hypoglycaemia; cough + wheeze in infancy → bronchiolitis or asthma; persistent diarrhoea + weight loss + oral thrush → HIV.
- **"If the history is unclear, ask a different person or ask again later."** A grandmother may know the birth history, the father may know about the TB contact.
- **Parents usually know** when something is seriously wrong: take their concern seriously.
- **Judgement call: how long a history?** A child with emergency signs gets SAMPLE now and the full history later; a well child in clinic gets the full history; a timed OSCE gets every heading briefly.

### Common student mistakes

Starting with closed questions; forgetting the danger signs; not asking for the card; accepting "fully immunised" without checking; skipping the feeding history in a malnourished child; not asking the mother's HIV status (or asking it publicly and insensitively); forgetting to ask what treatment has been given; asking an adolescent about sex in front of parents; presenting a child without the weight.

## 8. Complications and Troubleshooting

### What goes wrong when the history is poorly taken or communicated

- **Missed diagnoses**: TB (no contact history), HIV (no maternal status), intussusception (no stool description), developmental disorders (no milestones).
- **Medication errors**: duplicate antimalarials, wrong dose because the weight or previous treatment was not recorded, missed allergy.
- **Missed child protection** or neglect.
- **Loss of trust**: families who feel judged or unheard may leave against advice or not return.
- **Breaches of confidentiality**: an adolescent or mother whose HIV status is disclosed without consent may suffer stigma, violence or abandonment.
- **Poor adherence** after discharge when instructions were not understood.

### Troubleshooting

| Problem | What to do |
|---|---|
| Informant did not witness the events | Record it; ask who did; phone the parent or ask at the next visit |
| History unclear or contradictory | Ask a different person or ask again later; distinguish observation from interpretation |
| No Child Health Card | Ask by age and site, look for the BCG scar, record "unknown (no card)", follow the catch-up schedule |
| Language barrier | Trained interpreter; brief them; speak to the caregiver; avoid a sibling for sensitive topics |
| Child too distressed or a sensitive topic | Move to a private, safe setting; come back later |
| Child has emergency signs | Stop; treat; SAMPLE history; full history later |

### Communication tasks that follow the history

In a history and communication skill, "management" means handling the conversations and situations that arise from the history, and turning the history into a safe plan the family understands.

#### 1. Explaining a diagnosis and plan (the ward-round conversation)

1. **Check what they know**: "What have the doctors told you so far?"
2. **Give information in small chunks**, in plain language, in the family's language, avoiding jargon ("infection of the lungs" rather than "lower respiratory tract infection").
3. **Explain the plan**: what treatment, how long, what the family can do (breastfeeding, giving ORS, feeding).
4. **Check understanding with teach-back**: "To make sure I explained well, can you tell me how you will give the medicine at home?"
5. **Invite questions** and address concerns.
6. **Safety-net**: danger signs that mean they must return immediately, and when to come back for review.

#### 2. Breaking bad news (SPIKES)

1. **S**etting: private room, sit down, phone silenced, both parents or the person they choose present, a nurse or counsellor with you, enough time.
2. **P**erception: "What do you understand about Grace's illness so far?"
3. **I**nvitation: "Would you like me to explain the test results in detail?"
4. **K**nowledge: warning shot ("I am afraid I have some difficult news"), then clear, simple information; avoid euphemisms (say "cancer" or "HIV" if that is the diagnosis); pause.
5. **E**motions: acknowledge and respond with empathy ("I can see this is a terrible shock"); allow silence and tears.
6. **S**trategy and summary: what happens next, what support is available (counsellors, palliative care, social worker), and when you will talk again.

Common situations: a new HIV diagnosis in a child (and therefore usually the mother), a diagnosis of cerebral palsy or leukaemia, a severe disability after meningitis, and the death of a child.

#### 3. The angry or distressed parent

- Stay calm; sit down; **listen without interrupting**; acknowledge the feeling ("You are upset because he waited 3 hours to be seen; I'm sorry about that").
- Find out the real concern (fear for the child, cost, a perceived mistake).
- Explain honestly what happened and what will happen now; **do not blame colleagues**.
- Involve a senior early; if there has been an error, follow **open disclosure** (say sorry, explain, put it right, report it).

#### 4. Adolescent consultations

- See the adolescent **alone** for part of the visit (explain this to parents as routine).
- Explain **confidentiality and its limits** at the start.
- Use **HEEADSSS**; non-judgemental language; respect privacy during examination (chaperone).
- Encourage them to involve a trusted adult; respect their developing autonomy in decisions; for HIV, support **disclosure** (most programmes aim for partial disclosure from about 6–10 years and full disclosure by about 10–12 years, adapted to the child's maturity).

#### 5. Child protection: when the history suggests abuse or neglect

1. **Listen and record** the history verbatim, including who said what, and **dates and times**.
2. **Examine fully** (with a chaperone) and document injuries with a body map; measure growth (neglect).
3. **Ensure the child's immediate safety**: admit if going home would be unsafe.
4. **Inform the senior/consultant** the same day; involve the **social worker**, **probation and social welfare officer**, and police when required (defilement, serious injury). **Sauti 116** child helpline.
5. Do not confront or accuse the family yourself; be honest that you must share concerns to keep the child safe.
6. Treat injuries and infections; consider **post-exposure prophylaxis** (HIV PEP within 72 h, emergency contraception, STI prophylaxis) after sexual assault.

#### 6. Discharge counselling

Diagnosis in simple words; medicines (name, dose in mL or tablets, how often, for how long) with a **demonstration**; feeding plan; danger signs; **follow-up appointment** (date and place); **pending results** (e.g. EID PCR) and how they will be told; missed vaccines given; contact number.

## 9. Documentation and Presenting Findings

### Structure

Use a clear structure: **identification** (name, age, sex, weight, nutritional status, immunisation, HIV exposure, feeding), **presenting complaints**, **key positives and negatives**, relevant **background** (perinatal, development, family, social), **examination findings**, **summary and problem list**, **differentials**, **plan**. For handovers, **SBAR** (Situation, Background, Assessment, Recommendation) is efficient.

### Model one-line summary and problem list

"**Baby Nakato**, a **7-month-old girl**, **6.2 kg** (weight-for-length Z-score −2.5, MUAC 11.8 cm, no oedema), **born at term weighing 3.0 kg**, **HIV-exposed** (mother on TLD for 4 years, last VL suppressed; NVP prophylaxis completed; 6-week PCR negative), **partially immunised** (missed 14-week vaccines), **stopped breastfeeding at 4 months** and now fed **diluted cow's milk and thin porridge 2 times a day**, presenting with **watery diarrhoea for 16 days** and poor weight gain."

**Problem list**: (1) persistent diarrhoea, (2) moderate acute malnutrition with poor complementary feeding, (3) HIV-exposed infant needing repeat DNA PCR, (4) missed vaccines, (5) family food insecurity (father unemployed).

### Model case presentation

"**Kato** is a **2-year-and-3-month-old boy** from **Kawempe**, brought by his **mother, a reliable informant**. He weighs **9.8 kg** (weight-for-height Z-score **−2.2**, height-for-age −2.6), **MUAC 12.3 cm**, no oedema. He is **fully immunised for age** by card with a BCG scar, received vitamin A and albendazole 3 months ago, is **HIV-unexposed** (mother tested negative in ANC and 2 months ago), was **breastfed for 14 months** and now eats family foods twice a day (mostly porridge and matooke, rarely beans or animal foods). He presents with **cough for 4 weeks, fever for 2 weeks and weight loss**. His **grandmother, who shares his sleeping room, has had a cough for 3 months** and has not been tested. He has no danger signs. Born at term at Kawempe hospital by SVD, birth weight 3.2 kg, cried immediately. Milestones normal (walked at 13 months, 2-word phrases now). **Summary**: a toddler with chronic cough, fever and moderate wasting with a probable TB contact. **Differentials**: pulmonary TB, HIV-related lung disease (re-test), persistent pneumonia, foreign body. **Plan**: CXR, Xpert Ultra on gastric aspirate or stool, HIV test, nutritional assessment and counselling, contact tracing for the grandmother."

## 11. Key Points

### High-yield points

- Triage before history: emergency signs are treated first, with a SAMPLE history.
- Record the informant and use the caregiver's words, with a duration for each complaint.
- Always ask the four IMCI general danger signs.
- The paediatric extras: perinatal, feeding, immunisation (check the card), growth, development and HIV exposure.
- Never accept "fully immunised" without the card and the BCG scar.
- Regression of any skill is always a red flag.
- See adolescents alone for part of the visit; explain confidentiality and its limits; use HEEADSSS.
- ICE, signposting, summarising, teach-back; SPIKES for bad news.
- Child protection: listen, document, ensure safety, involve the senior, social worker and probation officer; do not confront the family; Sauti 116.
- Open every presentation with age, sex, weight, nutritional status, immunisation, HIV exposure and feeding.

### Numbers & doses

| Item | Value |
|---|---|
| Social smile | **6 weeks** (red flag: none by 8 weeks) |
| Head control | **3–4 months** (red flag: none by 4 months) |
| Sits unsupported | **6–8 months** (red flag: not by 9 months) |
| Pincer grasp | **9–12 months** |
| Walks | **12–15 months** (red flag: not walking by 18 months) |
| First words | **12 months**; 2-word phrases **2 years** |
| Correct for prematurity | Until **2 years** of age |
| Exclusive breastfeeding | **0–6 months**; initiation within **1 hour** of birth |
| Complementary feeding | From **6 months**; minimum **5 of 8 food groups** daily; 2–3 meals at 6–8 months, 3–4 + snacks at 9–23 months |
| Immunisation contacts | Birth, **6, 10, 14 weeks**, **9 months**, **18 months** (+ malaria vaccine schedule where introduced) |
| Prolonged cough (TB) | **>2 weeks** |
| Persistent diarrhoea | **≥14 days** |
| Prolonged rupture of membranes | **>18 hours** (neonatal sepsis risk) |
| Adolescence (WHO) | **10–19 years**; Uganda age of majority **18** |
| HIV PEP after sexual assault | Within **72 hours** |
| Child helpline (Uganda) | **116** (Sauti) |

### Exam traps

- **Trap:** taking a long history while a child with emergency signs waits. **Right:** triage and treat first; SAMPLE history during resuscitation.
- **Trap:** "Fully immunised" because the mother says so. **Right:** check the card and BCG scar, and list any missed doses.
- **Trap:** asking a 15-year-old about sexual activity in front of her mother. **Right:** see her alone after explaining confidentiality.
- **Trap:** skipping the perinatal and developmental history in a child with seizures. **Right:** birth asphyxia, neonatal jaundice and developmental delay change the diagnosis and prognosis.
- **Trap:** not asking about the mother's HIV status because "it is sensitive". **Right:** ask privately and respectfully; HIV exposure changes management.
- **Trap:** presenting the child without weight, nutritional status and immunisation status. **Right:** these belong in the opening line.
- **Trap:** confronting parents about suspected abuse yourself. **Right:** ensure safety, document, involve senior, social worker and probation officer.

### Questions seniors ask

**Viva questions**

1. *What are the IMCI general danger signs?* Unable to drink or breastfeed, vomits everything, has had convulsions (or convulsing now), lethargic or unconscious.
2. *What is in a perinatal history?* Antenatal (ANC, maternal illnesses including HIV/syphilis, drugs), labour and delivery (gestation, place, mode, complications), birth (weight, cried at birth, resuscitation), neonatal period (NICU, jaundice, infections, feeding).
3. *How do you assess immunisation status without a card?* Ask by age and site, look for the BCG scar, record as "unknown (no card)" and follow the catch-up schedule; never assume "fully immunised".
4. *What are the developmental red flags?* No smile by 8 weeks, no head control by 4 months, not sitting by 9 months, not walking by 18 months, no words by 18 months, early hand preference, any regression, concern about vision or hearing.
5. *What is HEEADSSS and why see adolescents alone?* A psychosocial screen (Home, Education/employment, Eating, Activities, Drugs, Sexuality, Suicide/mood, Safety); adolescents disclose more when confidentiality is assured.
6. *What are the components of SPIKES?* Setting, Perception, Invitation, Knowledge, Emotions/empathy, Strategy and summary.
7. *What features in the history suggest non-accidental injury?* Delay in presentation, inconsistent or changing history, mechanism not matching the injury or development, repeated injuries, a frightened child, disclosure.
8. *Why ask about the cooking fuel?* Indoor smoke from charcoal and firewood is a major risk factor for pneumonia and asthma (and burns).

**On the ward round**

1. **"Who gave you this history and how reliable is it?"** Name the informant, relationship and whether they witnessed the events.
2. **"Is this child immunised?"** Give the status by card, the BCG scar, and any missed doses and when they will be given.
3. **"What is this child's HIV exposure status?"** Mother's status and ART, viral load, infant prophylaxis, breastfeeding, PCR results.
4. **"How is she fed?"** Breastfeeding history, complementary foods, meal frequency and diversity, 24-hour recall.
5. **"Are her milestones normal?"** Give key milestones in each domain, corrected for prematurity, and any regression.
6. **"What did they already give at home or at the clinic?"** Drugs, doses, timing, herbal treatment.
7. **"Who is at home and can they cope with this plan?"** Household, carers, money, distance, and safety.
8. **"How would you break the news of a positive HIV PCR to this mother?"** SPIKES, privacy, counsellor present, start ART the same day, plan disclosure and partner testing, and arrange support.

### Mnemonics

- **Paediatric history order: "BIG PAIN FIDS"**: **B**iodata, **I**nformant, **G**rievances (complaints), **P**resenting illness, **A**ntenatal/perinatal, **I**mmunisation, **N**utrition/feeding, **F**amily, **I**llnesses (past), **D**evelopment, **S**ocial (and drugs). (A memory aid for the content; present in the order of section 5.)
- **Danger signs: "Can't Drink, Vomits All, Fits, Floppy"**.
- **Emergency history: SAMPLE** (Symptoms, Allergies, Medications, Past illness, Last meal, Events).
- **Adolescent: HEEADSSS**.
- **Bad news: SPIKES**.
- **Patient perspective: ICE** (Ideas, Concerns, Expectations).
- **Milestones "6-weeks smile, 6-months sit, 1-year walk, 2-years phrases"** (with ranges and red flags at 8 weeks, 9 months, 18 months, 2.5 years).
- **Handover: SBAR**.

### OSCE tips

- **Introduce yourself to the child too** and get down to their level.
- In a timed station, cover **danger signs, HPI, perinatal, feeding, immunisation, development, family/social** briefly; examiners look for the paediatric extras.
- **State the immunisation and nutritional status** in your summary.
- **Use ICE and summarise**: these communication marks are easy to earn and easy to forget.
- With a simulated adolescent, ask the parent to step out before sensitive questions.
