## 1. Purpose and Indications

**Vacuum extraction (ventouse)** means applying a suction cup to the fetal scalp and pulling, in time with contractions and maternal pushing, to expedite a vaginal birth in the **second stage** of labour. It helps a baby that is **almost there**. It does not pull a baby through a pelvis it cannot fit.

In Uganda, ventouse is a **life-saving but neglected skill**. A woman in prolonged second stage at an HC III or HC IV with a low head and a distressed baby can often be delivered in **minutes** with a ventouse. The alternative is a long referral and a **second-stage caesarean**, with its impacted head, extensions, haemorrhage, bladder injury and a scar for life.

**The one thing never to forget:** *ventouse is safe only when it should be easy.* If the head is high, the position unknown, or there is no descent with a good pull, **stop and do a caesarean**. Most ventouse disasters (subgaleal haemorrhage, dead babies, torn mothers) come from **persisting** when the signs said stop.

### Definitions

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Assisted (operative / instrumental) vaginal birth** | Vaginal birth assisted by vacuum or forceps |
| **Vacuum extractor / ventouse** | A cup (soft or rigid) attached to a vacuum source and traction handle |
| **Flexion point** | The point on the sagittal suture **about 3 cm anterior to the posterior fontanelle**. Traction over it flexes the head |
| **Chignon** | The artificial caput formed by scalp sucked into the cup. It resolves within hours |
| **Pop-off / detachment** | The cup losing its seal and coming off during traction |
| **Pull** | One episode of traction with one contraction |
| **Station** | The level of the **leading bony point of the skull** relative to the ischial spines (0 = at the spines; +1, +2 cm below) |
| **Fifths palpable** | How many fifths of the fetal head can be felt above the pelvic brim abdominally (5/5 to 0/5). **Always assessed before an instrumental delivery** |

### Why it matters in Uganda

- The WHO lists **7 BEmONC signal functions**: parenteral antibiotics, parenteral uterotonics, parenteral anticonvulsants, manual removal of the placenta, removal of retained products (e.g. MVA), **assisted vaginal delivery**, and neonatal resuscitation. **Assisted vaginal delivery is consistently the least-performed signal function** in sub-Saharan African EmONC assessments, including Uganda's. Reasons include no functioning equipment, lack of training and confidence, fear of blame, and a culture of "refer for CS".
- In high-income countries 10–15% of births are assisted. In many Ugandan facilities the figure is **around 1% or less**, while second-stage CS rates rise [VERIFY: Ugandan facility rate is an estimate; no national figure cited].
- **Second-stage CS** is more dangerous than CS in the first stage: deeply impacted heads, **extensions into the uterine vessels**, PPH, bladder injury, fetal skull and limb injuries, and neonatal asphyxia. A well-conducted ventouse avoids these.
- In Uganda, trained **midwives** as well as doctors perform ventouse deliveries. The *Essential Maternal and Newborn Clinical Care Guidelines* and EmONC training (e.g. ALSO/ESMOE-style courses) include vacuum extraction as a core skill.

### Indications

| Category | Examples |
|---|---|
| **Fetal** | **Suspected fetal compromise** in the second stage (persistent bradycardia, late or complicated decelerations, thick meconium with abnormal FHR) |
| **Maternal: delay in the second stage** | **Nulliparous**: active second stage lasting >2 h (NICE/RCOG allow up to 3 h total with regional analgesia). **Multiparous**: >1 h active (up to 2 h with regional). Ugandan teaching: **>2 h primigravida, >1 h multipara**. WHO 2018 / Labour Care Guide: delay is **≥3 h (nulliparous) or ≥2 h (parous)** |
| **Maternal: exhaustion** | Exhausted and unable to push effectively |
| **Maternal: medical need to shorten pushing** | Cardiac disease (NYHA III–IV), severe hypertension or hypertensive crisis, cerebrovascular disease or aneurysm, myasthenia gravis, proliferative retinopathy, spinal cord injury. **Forceps** may be preferred if maternal effort must be avoided entirely, because ventouse relies on some maternal pushing |

> [!UGANDA]
> Ugandan women rarely have epidural analgesia, so the second stage is usually shorter. Delay is commonly defined in local teaching as **>1 hour of active pushing in a multipara and >2 hours in a primigravida**. This is stricter than the WHO 2018 intrapartum recommendations and Labour Care Guide (**≥3 h nulliparous, ≥2 h parous** before calling it delay). Practice varies, so say which definition you are using. For a distressed baby with a low head at an **HC III**, a skilled ventouse **on site** may be safer than a 2-hour referral for CS. But if the head is **2/5 or more palpable**, or there is 3+ moulding, **refer or do a CS**. Never try ventouse "just to see" before a referral.

## 2. Contraindications and Precautions

| Absolute | Relative / caution |
|---|---|
| Cervix **not fully dilated** (except rare expert situations such as a second twin) | Gestation **32+0 to 36+0 weeks** (higher risk of cephalhaematoma and ICH) |
| Head **not engaged**: **≥2/5 palpable** abdominally or bony skull above the spines | **Mid-cavity** station (1/5 palpable, station 0/+1): trial in theatre by an experienced operator |
| **Unknown position** | **OP / deflexed** heads: use a posterior cup, expect a higher failure rate |
| **Face, brow or breech** presentation (and transverse lie) | **Macrosomia** (EFW >4 kg) and **BMI >30**: risk of failure and shoulder dystocia; trial in theatre |
| **Gestation <32 weeks** | **Recent fetal scalp blood sampling** or scalp electrode sites (bleeding) |
| Suspected **CPD** (3+ moulding, head high) | **Maternal HIV with unknown or unsuppressed viral load**, and hepatitis B/C: avoid where a safe alternative exists, because of scalp abrasions. **Don't let this delay an indicated birth**. Discuss with a senior |
| **Fetal bleeding disorder** (alloimmune thrombocytopenia, haemophilia) or **bone demineralisation** (osteogenesis imperfecta) | Previous failed vacuum in the same labour. Do not reapply |
| No consent, no back-up for CS, operator untrained | |

**Vacuum versus forceps: when forceps are the better instrument.** Forceps are better for a face presentation (mento-anterior), the after-coming head of a breech, a preterm baby (32–36 weeks), when maternal pushing must be avoided, and when a large caput prevents cup adhesion. Forceps need specific skills that are increasingly rare in Uganda.

**Precautions in every case**: the bladder must be empty; analgesia must be adequate; a person trained in newborn resuscitation (HBB) must be present; and the team must be able to move to an **immediate CS** if the ventouse fails. Anticipate **shoulder dystocia** and **PPH**.

## 3. Relevant Anatomy and Normal Values

### The fetal skull: landmarks you must feel

```
                 FRONT (face)
                      ▲
            ┌─────────┼─────────┐
            │     frontal suture│
            │         │         │
   coronal ─┼───◇ ANTERIOR ◇────┼─ coronal
   suture   │   FONTANELLE      │  suture
            │  (diamond, 4 sutures, large)
            │         │         │
            │   sagittal suture │
            │         │         │
            │         ●  ← FLEXION POINT
            │         │    (3 cm in front of PF,
            │         │     ~6 cm behind AF)
   lambdoid ┼────\    Y    /────┼ lambdoid
   suture   │  POSTERIOR FONTANELLE (small, Y/triangle, 3 sutures)
            └─────────┬─────────┘
                      ▼
                 BACK (occiput)
```

- **Posterior fontanelle**: small, **Y-shaped (3 sutures)**. When you feel it near the pubis, the position is **occipito-anterior (OA)**.
- **Anterior fontanelle (bregma)**: large, **diamond-shaped (4 sutures)**. If it is easily felt and central, the head is **deflexed** (often OP).
- If caput hides the sutures, feel for an **ear**: the **free edge of the pinna folds towards the occiput**. Where available, **intrapartum ultrasound** is more accurate than palpation for determining position (RCOG).

### Why the flexion point matters

| Cup placement | Presenting diameter | Result |
|---|---|---|
| **Flexing median** (centred over the flexion point, on the sagittal suture) | **Suboccipitobregmatic, 9.5 cm** (smallest) | Traction flexes the head, which autorotates and descends. This gives the highest success rate |
| **Deflexing** (too far forward, towards the anterior fontanelle) | Occipitofrontal, 11.5 cm | Traction extends the head, so a larger diameter presents, leading to failure, pop-offs and scalp trauma |
| **Paramedian** (off to one side of the sagittal suture) | Asynclitic head | Traction tilts the head, so the diameter increases and it fails to rotate |

With a **6 cm cup** centred on the flexion point, the **posterior edge of the cup lies at the posterior fontanelle** and the **anterior edge lies about 3 cm behind the anterior fontanelle**. A cup edge that is on or near the anterior fontanelle means you are **too far forward**.

> [!PEARL]
> In OP and deflexed positions the flexion point lies **far back, deep in the posterior vagina**. That is why a standard anterior cup (whose tubing comes out of the centre) cannot be placed correctly, and why **posterior cups** (Bird posterior cup, Kiwi OmniCup) have the tubing or traction cord coming out of the side or a groove. They are low-profile, so they can be slid right back to the flexion point.

### The physics: why pressure and cup size matter

The maximum traction before the cup detaches is roughly **pressure × area**. A 6 cm cup at 0.8 kg/cm² can take about **20 kg of traction** before popping off. More pressure or a bigger cup allows more traction, but also more scalp damage. The limits (pressure, number of pulls, detachments, time) are there to **cap the total force** transmitted to the baby's scalp and brain.

| Unit | Recommended working pressure |
|---|---|
| kg/cm² | **0.8** (range 0.6–0.8) |
| mmHg | **About 500–600** |
| kPa | **About 60–80** |

**Traction direction** follows the **curve of Carus** (the pelvic axis): initially **downward and backward** until the occiput passes under the symphysis, then **horizontal**, then **upward** as the head crowns and extends (a "J-shaped" path). Always pull **perpendicular to the plane of the cup**. Oblique pulling breaks the seal and tears the scalp.

### Classification of assisted vaginal birth (RCOG GTG 26, 2020)

| Type | Definition | Comment |
|---|---|---|
| **Outlet** | Scalp visible at the introitus **without separating the labia**; the skull has reached the pelvic floor; sagittal suture in the AP diameter or ≤45° from it | Easiest and safest |
| **Low** | Leading point of the skull at **station +2 cm or below**, but not on the pelvic floor. Subdivided into ≤45° or >45° rotation | Standard ventouse territory |
| **Mid** | Head **1/5 palpable** abdominally; leading point **at station 0 to +1 cm**. Subdivided into ≤45° or >45° rotation | For experienced operators only; consider a **trial in theatre** |
| **High** | Head ≥2/5 palpable, or above the spines | **Not appropriate. Do a CS** |

### Normal values you need at the bedside

| Item | Normal / required value |
|---|---|
| FHR | **110–160 bpm**; in the second stage check **every 5 minutes** (after each contraction) and between pulls |
| Contractions | Ideally **3–5 in 10 minutes**; tachysystole is >5 in 10 minutes |
| Head palpable abdominally | **0/5 or 1/5** required for ventouse |
| Bony station | **At or below the spines (0 or lower)**; +2 or below = low |
| Moulding | +1 (bones touching) or +2 (overlapping but reducible) acceptable; **+3 (irreducible) = stop** |
| Subpubic angle | ≥2 fingers |
| Gestation | Ideally **≥34 weeks**; caution 32–36; **never <32 weeks** |
| Term neonatal blood volume | About **80–90 mL/kg** (about 280 mL for a 3.2 kg baby) |

## 4. Equipment and Preparation

### Equipment

| Cup | Features | Best for | Cautions |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Soft cup** (silicone/silastic, e.g. Silc cup, Kiwi ProCup) | Bell or funnel shaped; pliable; conforms to the head | **OA, outlet and low** deliveries | **Higher failure** in OP, deflexed or large caput. **Less scalp trauma** |
| **Rigid metal cup: anterior** (Malmström, Bird anterior) | Stainless steel, mushroom shaped, 40/50/60 mm; separate chain or cord; **forms a chignon** | OA positions; outlet, low and mid | More scalp trauma (chignon, cephalhaematoma); must be reusable and sterilised |
| **Rigid metal cup: posterior** (Bird posterior) | **Tubing attached at the side**, separate traction chain | **OP and OT, deflexed heads** (reaches the flexion point) | Skilled operators only |
| **Kiwi OmniCup** (rigid plastic, disposable) | Low-profile "mushroom" cup, **integral hand pump (PalmPump) with a pressure gauge**, flexible stem; **traction force indicator** in some models | **All positions**, including OP (the stem lies in a groove, allowing a posterior application) | Single use; cost; the green zone on the gauge indicates working pressure |
| **Pump** | Hand pump or electric suction with a **gauge** | | Check the gauge, tubing and seals **before** starting |

**Other equipment**: sterile gloves, gown and PPE (eye protection); a delivery set; lubricant/antiseptic; a urinary catheter (in-out); local anaesthetic (**lidocaine 1%** for perineal infiltration or pudendal block) with syringe and needle; episiotomy scissors and suture materials; **oxytocin 10 IU** drawn up; a **newborn resuscitation set** (bag and mask, suction, warm towels) and a person trained in HBB; a documentation proforma.

> [!UGANDA]
> Many Ugandan hospitals have **metal Malmström or Bird cups** (with hand or electric pumps) or silicone cups, and increasingly the disposable **Kiwi OmniCup** (hand-held, self-contained pump), supplied through partners. Check before you need it: cracked tubing and a dead pump are common problems.

### Step 0: the first 5 minutes

The ventouse workup is a **bedside decision made in minutes**: is there a real indication, are **all the prerequisites** met, **where** should it be done (delivery room or trial in theatre), and is everything ready if it fails? Then, after the birth, there is a **post-procedure check** of mother and baby (section 9).

**When you are called to a woman in the second stage with delay or an abnormal FHR:**

1. **Mother**: pulse, BP, temperature, RR, colour, conscious level. **Collapse, shock, a hard tender uterus or heavy bleeding** point to **abruption or uterine rupture**, not a simple delay. That is a **Category 1 CS** or laparotomy, not a ventouse.
2. **Baby**: listen to the **FHR for a full minute straight after a contraction** (normal **110–160 bpm**; in the second stage check it **every 5 minutes**). A **prolonged bradycardia** needs birth **now**, by whichever route is fastest and safe.
3. **Call for help at once**: the most experienced operator available, a midwife, and a **person trained in newborn resuscitation (HBB)** with a bag and mask ready.
4. **Quick fixes while help comes**: turn her onto her **left side**, **stop oxytocin** if the FHR is abnormal or there is **tachysystole** (>5 contractions in 10 minutes), give IV fluids if she is dehydrated, and **empty her bladder**.
5. **Get the equipment out and checked in parallel** (cup, pump, tubing, gauge), plus lidocaine, a delivery set, oxytocin 10 IU drawn up and the resuscitation corner ready.
6. **Tell theatre early** if there is any doubt: a failed ventouse becomes a difficult second-stage CS with an impacted head.

### Focused history: what to ask before deciding, and why

| Question | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| **Time of full dilatation and time active pushing started** | Defines delay: **≥3 h (nulliparous) or ≥2 h (parous)** in the WHO 2018 / Labour Care Guide; common Ugandan ward teaching uses **>2 h primigravida and >1 h multipara** (longer with an epidural) |
| **Parity and previous births** (size of previous babies, previous instrumental delivery or CS) | Multiparas usually deliver quickly. Previous big babies suggest the pelvis is proven. A previous CS means a scar (short second stage, and ventouse is acceptable) |
| **Gestational age** | **<32 weeks is a contraindication**; 32–36 weeks calls for caution |
| **Duration of the first stage and of pushing** | A long first stage plus a long second stage suggests **CPD or malposition**, so ventouse is more likely to fail |
| **Partograph**: rate of dilatation, descent (fifths), moulding trend | Slow descent with **increasing moulding** means **obstruction**, and ventouse is dangerous |
| **Contractions and oxytocin augmentation** | Weak contractions ("power") may be the problem that a ventouse (with pushing) solves, or may be correctable first (oxytocin in a multipara needs great care) |
| **Liquor colour and FHR pattern** | Sets the urgency. A fetal indication shortens the decision time |
| **HIV status and viral load; hepatitis B** | Scalp abrasions; consider alternatives if the VL is unknown or unsuppressed; neonatal prophylaxis. It must not delay an indicated birth |
| **Diabetes, obesity, estimated fetal weight, a previous shoulder dystocia** | **Shoulder dystocia** risk. Think "trial in theatre" |
| **Medical conditions** (cardiac, severe pre-eclampsia) | Changes the indication and the choice of instrument |
| **Bleeding disorders in the family; recent fetal scalp procedures** | Contraindications |
| **When she last passed urine** | A full bladder obstructs descent and is injured by traction |

### Prerequisites checklist (RCOG)

| Area | Requirement |
|---|---|
| **Mother** | Clear **explanation and verbal consent** (written for a trial in theatre); **analgesia** adequate (perineal infiltration or **pudendal block** for low or outlet; regional or spinal for mid-cavity or rotational); **bladder empty** (in-out catheter; deflate an indwelling balloon); **lithotomy** or dorsal position; **aseptic technique** |
| **Fetus** | **Cephalic**; **position known**; **head ≤1/5 palpable abdominally**; **bony station at or below the spines**; **moulding and caput** assessed (3+ moulding means stop); gestation ≥34 weeks ideally (never <32) |
| **Birth canal** | **Cervix fully dilated**; **membranes ruptured**; pelvis clinically adequate |
| **Staff and set-up** | Trained operator (or supervised trainee); equipment checked; **newborn resuscitator** present; ability to move to **immediate CS** if it fails; anticipation of **shoulder dystocia and PPH** |

### Consent, positioning and exposure

- **Consent**: explain to the woman (and her companion) what will happen and why, the alternatives (continued pushing, CS), and the main risks (failure, perineal tears, scalp marks, rarely serious scalp bleeding in the baby). Verbal consent in the delivery room; **written consent for a trial in theatre**. Tell her she will need to **push with each contraction**.
- **Position**: lithotomy or dorsal with the buttocks at the edge of the bed. Ensure good light.
- **Exposure and privacy**: expose only the abdomen and perineum; keep a chaperone or companion present; drape.
- **Decide the place**: **delivery room** (outlet or low, OA, good chance of success) or **trial in theatre** (mid-cavity, OP, 1/5 palpable, EFW >4 kg, BMI >30, prolonged labour). A trial in theatre lets you move to CS within minutes.

## 5. Procedure Step by Step

### A. Focused examination before applying the cup (Hutchison order)

Do the examination **in this order** and write each finding down. **Every prerequisite must be met.**

1. **Introduction and consent**: introduce yourself, confirm her identity, explain, gain consent; chaperone present.
2. **Hand hygiene**.
3. **Position and exposure**: dorsal, abdomen exposed, then lithotomy for the VE.
4. **General inspection**: well or unwell, exhaustion, dehydration, pallor, distress; **vitals** (pulse, BP, temperature, RR). Shock, a hard tender uterus or heavy bleeding means abruption or rupture, not a ventouse.
5. **Abdomen (always before the VE)**:
   - **Inspection**: shape, scars (previous CS), **Bandl's ring** (obstruction), a distended bladder.
   - **Fundal height and lie**: longitudinal; estimated fetal size.
   - **Presentation**: cephalic.
   - **Engagement: fifths of the head palpable**: **0/5 or 1/5 required**. **2/5 or more = the head is not engaged: no ventouse.**
   - **Contractions**: frequency and strength (ideally **3–5 in 10 minutes**).
   - **Bladder**: palpable?
   - **Auscultation of the fetal heart** after a contraction (Pinard or Doppler).
6. **Vaginal examination**:

| Item | What you need | Stop if |
|---|---|---|
| **Cervix** | **Fully dilated**, no rim | Any cervix felt around the head |
| **Membranes** | **Ruptured** (or perform ARM) | |
| **Station of the bony skull** (not the caput) | **At or below the ischial spines (0 or lower)**; +2 or below = low | Above the spines |
| **Position** | **Known exactly**: follow the sagittal suture to a fontanelle. **Posterior fontanelle** (small, Y-shaped, 3 sutures) near the pubis = **OA**; **anterior fontanelle** (large, diamond, 4 sutures) near the pubis = **OP**. If caput hides the sutures, feel for an **ear** (the free edge of the pinna points to the occiput) or use **ultrasound** | Position unknown |
| **Moulding** | +1 (bones touching) or +2 (overlapping but reducible) | **+3 (overlapping, irreducible)**, especially with the head ≥1/5 palpable: **CPD** |
| **Caput** | Small (graded + to +++) | Large caput reaching the introitus while the head is still palpable abdominally: **the caput is lying** |
| **Asynclitism** | Sagittal suture central | Marked asynclitism: higher failure rate |
| **Pelvis** | Adequate sacral curve, spines not prominent, subpubic angle ≥2 fingers | Contracted pelvis |
| **Liquor** | Clear | Thick meconium or blood: urgency; resuscitator present |

7. **Put fifths and station together**: they must agree (table in section 6). If they don't, **trust the abdomen**.

### B. Bedside checks before and during

| Test | When | What it tells you |
|---|---|---|
| **FHR by Pinard or Doppler** | **Every 5 minutes** (after each contraction) in the second stage; **between pulls** | Urgency; a deteriorating FHR with birth not imminent means stop and go to CS |
| **Partograph review** | Before deciding | Rate of dilatation and descent, moulding trend, time in the second stage |
| **In-out catheter** | Before every application | Empties the bladder; note the volume. **Blood-stained urine** suggests obstruction |
| **Urine dipstick** | If there is time | **Ketones** (dehydration, exhaustion), protein (pre-eclampsia), blood (obstruction) |
| **HemoCue Hb** | If she looks pale or is known to be anaemic | Readiness for PPH (group and cross-match if low) |
| **Intrapartum ultrasound** (where available and you are trained) | Position uncertain | Fetal orbits and midline echo show the position more reliably than palpation |
| **Equipment check** | Before insertion | Occlude the cup on a gloved palm: the gauge must reach working pressure and hold |
| **Contraction count** | Before and during | Pull only with contractions; weak contractions reduce success |

### C. The procedure

1. **Explain** to the woman (and her companion) what will happen and why, and get **consent**. Tell her she will need to **push with each contraction**.
2. **Position**: lithotomy or dorsal with the buttocks at the edge of the bed. Ensure good light.
3. **PPE and asepsis**: hand hygiene, sterile gloves, apron, eye protection. Clean the vulva and perineum and drape.
4. **Empty the bladder** with an in-out catheter.
5. **Re-examine** (abdominally and vaginally): fifths palpable, dilatation, station of the bony skull, **position** (fontanelles and sutures), caput and moulding. **If any prerequisite is not met, stop.**
6. **Analgesia**: perineal infiltration with **lidocaine 1% (up to about 20 mL)**, or a **pudendal block** (lidocaine 1%, 10 mL each side, max total **about 3–4.5 mg/kg without adrenaline**).
7. **Check the equipment**: connect the cup to the pump. Test for vacuum by occluding the cup against a gloved palm.
8. **Insert the cup**: separate the labia, compress the cup (soft) or insert it sideways (rigid) with pressure on the posterior vaginal wall, and slide it onto the scalp.
9. **Locate the flexion point**: place the **centre of the cup over the sagittal suture about 3 cm in front of the posterior fontanelle**. Check that the anterior edge is about 3 cm from the anterior fontanelle.
10. **Create initial vacuum** (about **0.2 kg/cm²**, about 150 mmHg). **Sweep a finger around the whole cup circumference** to make sure no **cervix or vaginal wall** is trapped under it (trapping causes tears and failure).
11. **Increase to working pressure** (about **0.8 kg/cm²**, 500–600 mmHg, or the green zone). Rapid build-up over 1–2 minutes is as effective as the old stepwise method.
12. **Traction with the contraction and maternal push**:
    - **Dominant hand** pulls on the handle, perpendicular to the cup, along the pelvic curve: **downward first, then horizontal, then upward** as the head crowns.
    - **Non-dominant hand**: the **thumb on the cup** and the **index finger on the scalp** next to it (the "finger-thumb" position) to feel for **descent** and for early **cup slippage**, and to keep the head flexed.
    - **Do not rock or twist** the cup. The head **autorotates** as it descends.
13. **Between contractions**: stop pulling, check the FHR, and reassess descent. (With metal cups some operators reduce the vacuum to about 0.2 kg/cm²; with the Kiwi, follow the manufacturer.)
14. **Assess each pull**: there must be **descent with every pull**. Expect the head to be **crowning or delivered within 3 pulls**.
15. **Episiotomy**: **not routine** with ventouse. Consider a **mediolateral** episiotomy (at about 60° from the midline at crowning) if the perineum is tight and the baby needs rapid delivery, or if tearing seems imminent.
16. **Delivery of the head**: guard the perineum. When the **jaw is reachable**, **release the vacuum** and remove the cup. Deliver the shoulders and body normally, and **anticipate shoulder dystocia** (call for help early).
17. **Third stage**: **oxytocin 10 IU IM** (active management), controlled cord traction, uterine massage. Watch for **PPH**.
18. **Inspect** the vagina, cervix and perineum for tears and repair them. Perform a **PR exam** to exclude an anal sphincter injury (OASI), before and after the repair.
19. **Examine the baby**: scalp (chignon, abrasions, lacerations, swelling), head circumference, and **vitamin K 1 mg IM** (routine in Uganda). Hand over for **enhanced neonatal observations**.
20. **Document** (section 9) and **debrief** the woman.

### D. When to abandon

> [!REDFLAG]
> **Stop and proceed to caesarean section if:**
> - There is **no descent with a pull** that was correctly applied and done with a contraction.
> - Delivery is **not imminent after 3 pulls** (the head should be crowning or delivered).
> - The cup **detaches twice** (some guidance allows up to 3 detachments). **Do not keep reapplying.**
> - The total cup application time exceeds about **15–20 minutes**. The WHO *Managing Complications in Pregnancy and Childbirth* manual allows up to 30 minutes, but modern guidance (RCOG) is stricter.
> - **Fetal condition deteriorates** and the birth is not imminent.
>
> **Avoid sequential instruments** (ventouse, then forceps). This raises the risk of intracranial haemorrhage and other neonatal trauma. In Uganda, where forceps skills are scarce, the fallback is **CS**. Tell theatre early, and warn them that the head may be deeply impacted.

## 6. Findings and Interpretation

### Fifths and station together (they must agree)

| Abdomen | VE (bony skull) | Classification (RCOG) | Where and who |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0/5 | Scalp visible without parting the labia | **Outlet** | Delivery room; trained midwife or doctor |
| 0/5 | **+2 or lower**, not on the pelvic floor | **Low** | Delivery room; trained operator |
| **1/5** | **0 to +1** | **Mid-cavity** | **Trial in theatre** by an experienced operator, ready for immediate CS |
| **≥2/5** | Above the spines (or caput low but skull high) | **High** | **No ventouse. CS** |

If the abdomen and the VE disagree (e.g. "station +2" but 2/5 palpable), **trust the abdomen**: a big caput has misled the VE.

### What each examination finding means

| Finding | Meaning | Action |
|---|---|---|
| Head 0/5 or 1/5 palpable, bony skull at or below the spines | Engaged; ventouse possible | Proceed if all other prerequisites are met |
| Head ≥2/5 palpable | Not engaged | No ventouse; CS |
| Small Y-shaped fontanelle anteriorly | OA | Standard cup placement |
| Large diamond fontanelle anteriorly, or easily felt and central | OP or deflexed | Posterior cup (Kiwi, Bird posterior); trial in theatre; higher failure |
| Moulding +3 with head ≥1/5 palpable | CPD | No ventouse; CS |
| Large caput, oedematous vulva, prolonged labour | Obstructed labour | CS |
| Marked asynclitism | Paramedian presentation | Higher failure; reassess |
| Cervical rim felt | Not fully dilated | No ventouse |
| Thick meconium, abnormal FHR | Fetal compromise | Urgency; resuscitator present |
| Blood-stained urine on catheterisation | Obstruction (bladder compression) | Reconsider; CS more likely |
| Bandl's ring, haematuria, tender lower segment | Obstructed labour, impending rupture | No ventouse; urgent CS |

### Laboratory and imaging

Most ventouse decisions are made **without lab results**. Do not delay a clearly indicated ventouse for tests. The table shows what matters before and, especially, after the birth.

| Test | When | Expected / abnormal finding | How it changes management |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Maternal Hb** (HemoCue at HC III/IV; FBC at hospital) | Before, if anaemic; after, if PPH | Hb <11 g/dL anaemia; **<7 g/dL severe** | Group and cross-match; low threshold to treat PPH; iron afterwards |

### Scalp swellings after birth

> [!PEARL]
> **Cephalhaematoma vs subgaleal haemorrhage vs caput succedaneum** is a classic viva.
> - **Caput**: scalp oedema, **crosses sutures**, present **at birth**, soft and pitting, gone in 1–2 days.
> - **Cephalhaematoma**: **subperiosteal**, does **not** cross sutures, appears **hours later**, resolves in weeks.
> - **Subgaleal**: **subaponeurotic**, **crosses sutures**, **enlarges** over hours, can cause **shock**, and is life-threatening.
>
> The loose space under the galea can hold **several hundred mL**, **most of a term baby's blood volume** (about 80–90 mL/kg, so about 280 mL for a 3.2 kg baby).

| Swelling | Layer | Crosses sutures? | Timing | Significance |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Chignon** | Scalp sucked into the cup | Limited to the cup site | At birth | Normal; resolves in **12–24 h** |
| **Caput succedaneum** | Scalp oedema | Yes | At birth | Benign; gone in 1–2 days |
| **Cephalhaematoma** | Subperiosteal | No | Hours after birth | Resolves over weeks; jaundice, anaemia |
| **Subgaleal haemorrhage** | Subaponeurotic | Yes, orbits to nape | Enlarges over hours | Hypovolaemic shock; emergency |

## 7. Clinical Acumen

### Red flags

> [!REDFLAG]
> - **Head 2/5 or more palpable abdominally**, even if the caput is "at the introitus": the head is not engaged. **No ventouse.**
> - **+3 moulding, big caput, oedematous vulva, prolonged labour**: obstructed labour. CS.
> - **Can't determine the position** (and no ultrasound): don't apply a cup blindly. Call a senior.
> - **No descent with the first well-applied pull** in a multipara, or with the second in a primigravida: likely failure. Reassess seriously.
> - **After delivery**: a baby with **pallor, tachycardia, poor tone, a boggy fluctuant scalp swelling crossing the suture lines, or a rising head circumference** has **subgaleal haemorrhage** until proven otherwise.
> - **After delivery**: maternal **PPH**, perineal pain out of proportion (haematoma), inability to void (retention).

### Thinking like a clinician

1. **"Is this a delay of power, passenger or passage?"**
   - **Power** (weak contractions, exhaustion) with a low head in good position: a ventouse works well.
   - **Passenger** (OP, deflexed, asynclitic, big baby): ventouse may work with a posterior cup and correct placement, but failure is more likely.
   - **Passage** (CPD, contracted pelvis): **ventouse will fail and harm**. CS.
2. **Decide the place**: in the **delivery room** (outlet or low, OA, a good chance of success) or as a **trial in theatre** (mid-cavity, OP, 1/5 palpable, EFW >4 kg, BMI >30, prolonged labour). A trial in theatre lets you move to CS within minutes.
3. **Decide the instrument**: **ventouse** is first-line for most Ugandan operators because it needs less analgesia, causes less maternal trauma, and midwives are trained in it. **Forceps** are better for a face presentation (mento-anterior), the after-coming head of a breech, a preterm baby (32–36 weeks), when maternal pushing must be avoided, and when there is a large caput preventing cup adhesion.
4. **If X then think Y**:
   - Caput at the introitus but the head 2/5 palpable: the **caput is lying to you**. The skull is high. CS.
   - A pop-off on the first pull: **check the application** (flexion point? tissue trapped? wrong direction of pull?). One reapplication is allowed. A second pop-off means stop.
   - The head descends but doesn't rotate from OT or OP: flexion is probably poor. Was the cup too anterior? Consider stopping.
   - The baby is delivered but "the scalp looks swollen all over, not just the chignon": **think subgaleal haemorrhage** and start hourly observations and head measurements.
   - Collapse, shock, a hard tender uterus or heavy bleeding in the second stage: **abruption or uterine rupture**, not a delay. Category 1 CS or laparotomy.
5. **When to stop or escalate**: any prerequisite not met; no descent with a correct pull; 3 pulls without imminent birth; 2 detachments; 15–20 minutes; fetal deterioration with birth not imminent. Escalate early to the senior and theatre rather than late.
6. **Common student mistakes**:
   - Relying on VE station while ignoring abdominal fifths.
   - Placing the cup where it is easiest (just inside the introitus, often over the anterior fontanelle) rather than at the flexion point.
   - Pulling between contractions, or pulling upward too early.
   - Twisting the cup to "rotate" the head.
   - Persisting after pop-offs.
   - Forgetting the bladder, the paediatrician (or HBB-trained person), oxytocin for the third stage, and the PR exam after the repair.

### Putting it together: worked examples

**Model decision summary:** "Mrs A., P0+0 at 39+5 weeks, **fully dilated for 3 h, pushing for 2 h 15 min**, exhausted, FHR 150 with early decelerations. Head **0/5 palpable**, bony station **+2**, **LOA**, caput +, moulding +1, clear liquor, bladder emptied. Impression: **prolonged second stage with maternal exhaustion; low-cavity, OA; all prerequisites met**. Plan: **ventouse in the delivery room** after consent and perineal infiltration, with a newborn resuscitator present, theatre informed as back-up, and oxytocin 10 IU IM for the third stage."

**Worked example 1: "Go now" (fetal indication).** A P2 has been pushing for **1 h 15 min**. The FHR drops to **90 bpm for 3 minutes** and does not recover. On examination the head is **0/5**, the bony skull is **+3** and just visible with the labia parted, **LOA**, moulding +1. *Interpretation:* **suspected fetal compromise** with a **low (nearly outlet) OA head**; every prerequisite is met, and a CS would take far longer. *Action:* call for help and the resuscitator, empty the bladder, infiltrate the perineum, and do a **ventouse now in the delivery room**, expecting delivery in **1–2 pulls**. If there is **no descent with the first correct pull**, stop and move to Category 1 CS.

**Worked example 2: "Trial in theatre or CS" (reading the findings).** A primigravida at 40 weeks has pushed for **2 h 30 min** after a 16-hour first stage augmented with oxytocin. The head is **1/5 palpable**; VE: bony station **0 to +1**, **caput ++**, **moulding +2**, the anterior fontanelle is felt behind the pubis, so the position is **direct OP**. EFW 3.9 kg. *Interpretation:* a **mid-cavity OP** head after a **long labour** (both passenger and possibly passage factors): the failure risk is **high**. *Action:* this is **not** a delivery-room ventouse. It is either a **trial in theatre** by an experienced operator with a **posterior cup** (Kiwi OmniCup or Bird posterior), with consent for CS and the anaesthetist ready, or a **CS** directly. If the head were **2/5 palpable** or moulding **+3**, the answer would be **CS only**.

## 8. Complications and Troubleshooting

### Immediate maternal aftercare

| Issue | Action |
|---|---|
| **Third stage and PPH prevention** | **Oxytocin 10 IU IM** (or IV slowly) immediately after birth. Controlled cord traction. Check the uterus every 15 min for the first hour. Be ready to treat PPH (bimanual compression, oxytocin infusion 20 IU in 1 L, **tranexamic acid 1 g IV over 10 min**, misoprostol 800 µg sublingual) |
| **Genital tract trauma** | Inspect the cervix, vagina and perineum. Repair tears and episiotomy with **2-0 Vicryl Rapide** (or chromic catgut). **PR exam** before and after repair to detect **OASI**. Third/fourth-degree tears go to an experienced operator in theatre |
| **Antibiotic prophylaxis** | RCOG (ANODE trial): a **single IV dose of amoxicillin-clavulanate (1.2 g)** after assisted vaginal birth halves maternal infection. If it is unavailable, follow the unit protocol (e.g. a single dose of **ampicillin 2 g IV ± metronidazole 500 mg IV**). Give a full course only for established infection or third/fourth-degree tears per protocol |
| **Analgesia** | **Paracetamol 1 g oral 6-hourly** + **diclofenac 50 mg oral 8-hourly** (or ibuprofen 400 mg 8-hourly) for 3–5 days, unless contraindicated (severe pre-eclampsia with renal impairment, asthma, PPH with oliguria) |
| **Bladder care** | High risk of **urinary retention** (swelling, pain, regional block). Ensure she **voids within about 6 hours** and check the volume. If she can't void or the bladder is palpable, catheterise (and consider an indwelling catheter for 24–48 h if the residual is large) |
| **VTE assessment** | Assisted vaginal birth is a VTE risk factor. Add other risks (age >35, obesity, PPH, prolonged labour, immobility, sickle cell). Give **enoxaparin 40 mg SC daily** if the score warrants it, plus early mobilisation |
| **Debrief** | Explain why the ventouse was needed, how it went, what the chignon is, what to watch for in the baby, and the implications for the next birth (**most women who have had an assisted delivery have a spontaneous vaginal birth next time**) |

> [!UGANDA]
> The ANODE single-dose amoxicillin-clavulanate recommendation comes from RCOG/UK practice; Ugandan units often use ampicillin ± metronidazole by local protocol, and amoxicillin-clavulanate IV may not be stocked [VERIFY: whether UCG 2023 or the Uganda EMHSLU lists IV amoxicillin-clavulanate for this indication].

### Neonatal complications

| Complication | Features | Management |
|---|---|---|
| **Chignon** | Soft, circumscribed swelling exactly where the cup was | Reassure. It **resolves in hours (12–24 h)** |
| **Scalp abrasions / lacerations** | From twisting, prolonged traction or rotation | Clean; keep dry; they usually heal. Watch for infection |
| **Cephalhaematoma** | **Subperiosteal** bleed, **limited by suture lines** (does not cross them), usually over a parietal bone, appears **hours after birth**, firm and fluctuant | **No aspiration** (infection risk). It resolves over **weeks to months**. Watch for **jaundice** (breakdown of blood) and anaemia. Rarely calcifies |
| **Subgaleal (subaponeurotic) haemorrhage** | Bleeding between the **epicranial aponeurosis (galea) and the periosteum**. **Crosses suture lines**, spreading from the orbits to the nape and around the ears. A boggy, fluctuant, shifting swelling; **pitting of the scalp**; ears pushed forward; **increasing head circumference** (about **1 cm increase ≈ 40 mL blood**); **pallor, tachycardia, hypotonia, hypovolaemic shock**; later DIC | **Emergency**: call the paediatrician or neonatal team. **Hourly** obs (HR, RR, colour, tone, cap refill), **measure head circumference every hour**, Hb/PCV, coagulation screen. **Volume**: normal saline **10–20 mL/kg** IV, then **blood transfusion** (O-negative or cross-matched), FFP for coagulopathy, **vitamin K 1 mg IM/IV**. Transfer to NICU. Mortality can reach 12–25% |
| **Intracranial haemorrhage** | Apnoea, seizures, poor feeding, bulging fontanelle, altered tone | Neonatal team; cranial ultrasound or CT; supportive care |
| **Retinal haemorrhages** | Common (vacuum > forceps), asymptomatic | Resolve spontaneously |
| **Neonatal jaundice** | Resorption of haematoma | Bilirubin monitoring; phototherapy |
| **Shoulder dystocia and brachial plexus injury** | Associated with macrosomia and instrumental birth | Anticipate: call for help, McRoberts, suprapubic pressure (HELPERR). Follow up the brachial plexus |

**Routine neonatal observation after every ventouse**: examine the scalp and measure the head circumference at birth. Do **regular observations (at least hourly for the first few hours, then 2–4-hourly for 12–24 h)** of HR, RR, colour, tone and feeding, and of the **size and consistency of scalp swelling**. Teach the mother and ward staff what to look for. Give **vitamin K 1 mg IM** to every baby.

### Troubleshooting during the procedure

| Problem | Likely cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Cup will not hold vacuum on the palm test | Cracked tubing, worn seal, dead pump | Replace before insertion; never start with faulty equipment |
| Pop-off on the first pull | Wrong placement (too anterior), trapped tissue, oblique pull, large caput | Check the flexion point, finger sweep, pull perpendicular; **one reapplication** only |
| Second pop-off | Failing ventouse | **Stop**; CS |
| No descent with a correct pull | Malposition, CPD | **Stop**; CS |
| Head descends but does not rotate from OT/OP | Poor flexion; cup too anterior | Consider stopping; do not twist the cup |
| Large caput prevents cup adhesion | Prolonged labour | Consider forceps (if skilled) or CS |
| Shoulders do not deliver | Shoulder dystocia | Call for help; HELPERR (McRoberts, suprapubic pressure) |

### Failed ventouse: what next?

1. **Stop.** Do not reapply the cup or switch to forceps unless the head is at the outlet and an experienced operator judges it safe.
2. **Call the senior and theatre**: this is usually a **Category 1 or 2 CS** depending on the FHR.
3. Keep monitoring the FHR. Stop any oxytocin if there is fetal distress. Consider **acute tocolysis** (terbutaline 0.25 mg SC) for an abnormal FHR while preparing.
4. **Warn the team of an impacted head**: the assistant may need to **disimpact the head vaginally** ("push"), or the surgeon may deliver by reverse breech extraction ("pull"). Expect **extensions** and **PPH**.
5. **Neonatal team present at the CS**: failed ventouse followed by CS carries a high risk of neonatal injury and asphyxia.
6. **Document** everything and **debrief** the woman and the team.

### Model case presentation

Before the procedure the head was **1/5 palpable**, the cervix fully dilated, the bony station **+2**, position **left occipito-anterior**, caput +, moulding +1, clear liquor. After consent, in-out catheterisation and perineal infiltration with 10 mL lidocaine 1%, a **Kiwi OmniCup** was applied at the flexion point. She had a second-degree tear, repaired with 2-0 Vicryl Rapide; the PR exam was normal. EBL 400 mL. She received oxytocin 10 IU IM and a single dose of IV antibiotics. **Plan**: paracetamol and diclofenac, **void within 6 hours** with a volume check, VTE risk assessment, **neonatal observations and head circumference every hour for 4 hours then 4-hourly for 24 hours**, and a debrief today."

## 11. Key Points

### High-yield points

- **Ventouse is safe only when it should be easy.** Stop when the signs say stop.
- Assisted vaginal delivery is a **BEmONC signal function** and the least performed in Uganda.
- **Prerequisites**: fully dilated, membranes ruptured, head **≤1/5 palpable** and **at or below the spines**, position known, no +3 moulding, empty bladder, consent, analgesia, trained operator, resuscitator, CS back-up.
- **Abdomen before VE**; if they disagree, **trust the abdomen**.
- Cup on the **flexion point** (3 cm in front of the posterior fontanelle) for a flexing median application.
- **0.8 kg/cm²**, pull **with contractions**, **perpendicular**, along the **J-shaped** pelvic curve; never twist.
- **3-2-1-20** stopping rules; no sequential instruments; fallback is CS.
- Vacuum: more failure, cephalhaematoma and retinal haemorrhage; forceps: more maternal perineal trauma.
- **Subgaleal haemorrhage** crosses sutures and can kill within hours: hourly scalp checks and head circumference.
- After every ventouse: **oxytocin 10 IU IM**, PR exam, void within 6 h, single-dose antibiotic, VTE assessment, vitamin K for the baby.

| Analgesia | Paracetamol **1 g 6-hourly** + diclofenac **50 mg 8-hourly** (or ibuprofen 400 mg 8-hourly), 3–5 days |
| VTE prophylaxis | Enoxaparin **40 mg SC daily** if the risk score warrants |
| Acute tocolysis | Terbutaline **0.25 mg SC** |
| Voiding after birth | Within about **6 hours** |
| Neonatal vitamin K | **1 mg IM** |
| Subgaleal haemorrhage | **1 cm rise in head circumference ≈ 40 mL of blood**; normal saline **10–20 mL/kg**, then blood; mortality up to 12–25% |
| Chignon | Resolves in **12–24 h** |
| Episiotomy (if needed) | **Mediolateral, about 60°** from the midline at crowning; not routine |

### Exam traps

- **"The caput is at the introitus, so the head is low."** No. **Check the fifths abdominally**; if 2/5 or more is palpable, the skull is high: CS.
- **"Try the ventouse before referring, just to see."** No. A ventouse is done only when **every prerequisite is met**; otherwise refer or do a CS.
- **"Twist the cup to rotate an OP head."** No. **Never rotate**; correct placement on the flexion point lets the head **autorotate**.
- **"Pull steadily between contractions to save time."** No. Pull **only with contractions and maternal pushing**.
- **"If the ventouse fails, switch to forceps."** Avoid **sequential instruments** (more intracranial haemorrhage); the usual fallback in Uganda is **CS**.
- **"Put the cup where it sits easily, just inside the introitus."** That is usually over the **anterior fontanelle** (deflexing). Place it on the **flexion point**.
- **"A cephalhaematoma crosses the suture lines."** No. A **cephalhaematoma stops at the sutures**; **caput and subgaleal haemorrhage cross them**.
- **"Aspirate a cephalhaematoma."** No. Leave it (infection risk); watch for jaundice.

### Questions seniors ask

**Viva questions**

1. **Where is the flexion point and why does it matter?** On the sagittal suture about 3 cm in front of the posterior fontanelle. Traction there flexes the head so the smallest diameter (suboccipitobregmatic, 9.5 cm) presents.
2. **What pressure do you use?** About 0.8 kg/cm² (500–600 mmHg, about 80 kPa).
3. **Name two contraindications specific to vacuum rather than forceps.** Gestation <32 weeks (caution <36), and face presentation (forceps can deliver a mento-anterior face). Also fetal bleeding disorders.
4. **Compare vacuum and forceps.** Vacuum fails more often and causes more cephalhaematoma and retinal haemorrhage, but less maternal perineal trauma, and needs less analgesia. Forceps are more successful but cause more OASI and facial injuries.
5. **Distinguish caput, cephalhaematoma and subgaleal haemorrhage.** Caput crosses sutures and is present at birth; cephalhaematoma stops at sutures and appears hours later; subgaleal crosses sutures, enlarges over hours and can cause shock (section 6).
6. **Why empty the bladder?** A full bladder obstructs descent, is at risk of injury, and causes post-partum retention.
7. **Why is a sequential instrument delivery discouraged?** It causes more neonatal intracranial haemorrhage and trauma, and more maternal trauma.

**On the ward round**

1. **What must you check before applying a ventouse?** **Fully dilated**, **membranes ruptured**, head **≤1/5 palpable** with the bony skull **at or below the spines**, **position known**, moulding not +3, bladder empty, consent, analgesia, a trained operator, a newborn resuscitator and **CS back-up**.
2. **The VE says +2 but you can feel 2/5 of the head abdominally. What do you do?** **Trust the abdomen**: the caput is misleading you. No ventouse; CS (or a senior review).
3. **How do you tell OA from OP on VE?** Follow the sagittal suture: a **small Y-shaped posterior fontanelle** anteriorly means **OA**; the **large diamond anterior fontanelle** anteriorly means **OP**. If unsure, feel an ear or use ultrasound.
4. **When would you do a trial in theatre?** **Mid-cavity** (1/5 palpable, station 0 to +1), **OP**, **EFW >4 kg**, BMI >30, or a long labour, where failure is more likely and immediate CS must be possible.
5. **When do you abandon?** No descent with a correct pull; not imminent after **3 pulls**; **2 detachments**; about **15–20 minutes**; or fetal deterioration with birth not imminent.
6. **What observations does the baby need after a ventouse, and why?** Scalp and **head circumference** at birth, then **at least hourly** HR, RR, colour, tone and scalp swelling for the first few hours, to catch **subgaleal haemorrhage** early. This is the most important post-delivery observation.
7. **What maternal checks follow a ventouse?** Oxytocin 10 IU IM, EBL, inspection and **PR exam** (OASI), repair, **voiding within 6 hours**, a single antibiotic dose, analgesia, VTE assessment and a **debrief**.

### OSCE tips

> [!EXAM]
> In the ventouse OSCE, examiners watch for three things above all: **(1) checking fifths abdominally**, **(2) correct cup placement over the flexion point with a finger sweep for trapped tissue**, and **(3) traction with contractions along the pelvic curve, with clearly stated stopping rules**. Say the rules out loud.

- Say "I would call for help and a newborn resuscitator" at the start; it is a critical step.
