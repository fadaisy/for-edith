## 1. Purpose and Indications

This is a **skills topic**. By the end of the rotation you should be able to: name every instrument in a delivery pack and say what it is for; choose the right suture for the right tissue; conduct a clean, controlled normal delivery on a mannequin and (supervised) on the ward; decide when an episiotomy is truly needed and cut and repair one; and **classify perineal tears correctly**, repairing first- and second-degree tears and **recognising third- and fourth-degree tears** so they go to the right person in theatre.

Why it matters in Uganda: perineal trauma is common, especially in primigravidae and with instrumental deliveries. **Missed anal sphincter injury** leaves women with lifelong faecal incontinence, a hidden disability that causes shame and marital breakdown. Poor asepsis and retained swabs lead to **puerperal sepsis**. Most of this is prevented by **good technique and a careful examination after birth**.

> [!PEARL]
> The one thing never to forget: after every vaginal birth, **inspect the perineum, vagina and cervix in good light and do a rectal examination** before you decide what the tear is. You cannot classify what you have not looked at.

### Burden and Uganda context

- Most vaginal births involve some trauma; about 60-70% of women need suturing in many series. OASI occurs in roughly 1-6% of vaginal births depending on how carefully it is looked for; it is under-diagnosed where examination is poor.
- **Risk factors for OASI**: primiparity, birth weight over 4 kg, OP position, **instrumental delivery (especially forceps)**, prolonged second stage, **midline episiotomy**, shoulder dystocia, Asian ethnicity, induction, precipitate delivery.
- **Ugandan realities**: high patient volumes with one midwife per shift, **chromic catgut** still found in some units (more pain and tissue reaction than synthetic sutures), inadequate light, and variable availability of local anaesthetic. Women living with **HIV** and those with **FGM** scarring (in some communities of the north-east) need special care.
- **Obstetric fistula** (VVF/RVF) is a different injury (pressure necrosis from obstructed labour), but a badly repaired fourth-degree tear or a suture through the rectum can also cause a **rectovaginal fistula**.

### Indications

- **Conducting a delivery**: every spontaneous vaginal birth with a longitudinal lie, cephalic presentation and no indication for operative delivery (see the pre-delivery checks in section 4).
- **Repair**: first-degree tears that bleed, gape or distort anatomy; all second-degree tears and episiotomies.
- **Episiotomy (restrictive use only)**:
  - **Fetal distress** in the second stage, when birth needs to be hastened and the perineum is the obstruction
  - **Assisted vaginal delivery** (forceps always; vacuum only if the perineum is tight)
  - **Rigid or scarred perineum** causing delay (e.g. previous third-degree tear repair; FGM type III requires anterior defibulation instead)
  - **Shoulder dystocia** to allow internal manoeuvres (it does not release the impacted shoulder itself, but gives room for your hand)
  - **Breech delivery** in selected cases

## 2. Contraindications and Precautions

**Episiotomy is not indicated**: routinely in primigravidae; to prevent tears (it does not); for preterm babies routinely.

**When a student should NOT repair, and must call a senior**

- Any **third- or fourth-degree tear** or uncertainty about the sphincter
- **Heavy bleeding** from a tear that you cannot see the apex of, or cervical/high vaginal tears
- A **haematoma**
- Poor light, no assistant, or the woman is unstable (manage PPH first)

**When a normal delivery by you is not appropriate (call a senior)**: breech, transverse, face or brow found late; head still 2/5 or more at full dilatation; OP/OT with a slow second stage; moulding +++; abnormal FHR; exhausted, bleeding or BP 160/110 or more; rigid, scarred or FGM type III perineum.

**Other precautions**

- **No ergometrine** in hypertension, pre-eclampsia or cardiac disease.
- **Do not give oxytocin** until a second twin has been excluded.
- **HIV**: avoid unnecessary episiotomy; safe sharps practice (blunt-tip needles if available, never pass sharps hand-to-hand).
- **Lignocaine**: aspirate before every injection; keep within the maximum dose.
- **Never fundal pressure** and never traction on the neck.
- **Cord traction only during a contraction with counter-traction** (uterine inversion risk).

## 3. Relevant Anatomy and Normal Values

### The perineum: why the repair is done the way it is

```
           clitoris
        urethral meatus
         vaginal opening
  bulbospongiosus  |  bulbospongiosus
       \           |           /
  superficial transverse perineal muscles
              \    |    /
            PERINEAL BODY  <- central tendon where the muscles meet
                   |
       external anal sphincter (skeletal, voluntary, red-brown)
       internal anal sphincter (smooth, pale, thickening of gut
                                 circular muscle, involuntary)
              anorectal mucosa
```

- The **perineal body** is the keystone of the pelvic floor. The perineal muscles (bulbospongiosus and superficial transverse perineal) insert here. A good second-degree repair re-attaches these muscles to rebuild the perineal body; a poor repair leaves a gaping introitus, dyspareunia and prolapse later.
- The **external anal sphincter (EAS)** is striated muscle, dark red, about 3-4 cm long; when torn, its ends retract into their capsule (so you must grasp them with Allis forceps). It provides **voluntary** continence (squeeze).
- The **internal anal sphincter (IAS)** is pale, smooth muscle, a continuation of the rectal circular muscle; it provides **resting tone** and most passive continence (about 70-80%). Missed IAS injury causes passive soiling and flatus incontinence.
- The **pudendal nerve (S2-S4)** supplies perineal sensation and the EAS; a **pudendal block** at the ischial spines anaesthetises the perineum.
- **Blood supply**: the internal pudendal artery. The perineum heals well because it is vascular, but it bleeds readily.

### Episiotomy types

| Type | Description | Comments |
|---|---|---|
| **Mediolateral** | From the posterior fourchette directed laterally, **about 60 degrees from the midline at crowning** (ends up about 45 degrees after delivery) | **Standard in Uganda and the UK**. Lower risk of extension into the sphincter |
| **Midline (median)** | Straight down towards the anus | Easier to repair, less pain, but **higher risk of third/fourth-degree extension**; not used in our setting |
| **Lateral / J-shaped** | Starts lateral or curves | Not recommended |

### Normal values

| Item | Normal |
|---|---|
| FHR in second stage | **110-160 bpm**, checked **every 5 min** (or after each contraction) |
| Blood loss | **Under 500 mL** (measured) |
| Shock index (pulse ÷ SBP) | **Under 0.9** |
| Uterus after delivery | Firm, contracted, at or below the umbilicus |
| Placenta | Complete cotyledons and membranes; cord with **3 vessels** (2 arteries, 1 vein); central or eccentric insertion |
| Voiding after delivery | Within **6 h** |
| Retained placenta | Not delivered **30 min** after birth |
| Cord ties | About **2 cm and 5 cm** from the umbilicus |

## 4. Equipment and Preparation

### The delivery pack (typical Ugandan labour ward)

| Item | Purpose |
|---|---|
| **2 artery forceps** (Kocher's or Spencer Wells) | Clamp the cord (and bleeding vessels) |
| **Cord scissors** (blunt-ended, curved) | Cut the cord between clamps |
| **Episiotomy scissors** (Braun-Stadler; angled, blunt tip) | Cut an episiotomy (the blunt tip protects the baby's head) |
| **Needle holder** (Mayo-Hegar) | Hold the needle during repair |
| **Toothed dissecting forceps** | Hold tissue during repair |
| **Sponge-holding forceps** (Rampley's/ring forceps) | Clean the vulva, remove membranes, inspect the cervix |
| **Gallipot** | Antiseptic solution (e.g. chlorhexidine or povidone iodine) |
| **Kidney dish** | Receive the placenta, used instruments |
| **Cord ties or plastic cord clamps** | Tie the cord at about 2 cm and 5 cm from the umbilicus |
| **Gauze swabs, cotton wool, sanitary pads** | Cleaning, perineal support, counting |
| **Sterile drapes/towels (at least 2 for the baby)** | Drying and wrapping the baby; creating a sterile field |
| **Sterile gloves** (and elbow-length gloves for manual removal) | Asepsis |
| **Urinary catheter** (Nelaton or Foley) | Empty the bladder in the second or third stage if needed |

**Separate items to have ready**: oxytocin 10 IU (fridge), 2 mL and 10 mL syringes and needles, 1% lignocaine, suture materials, a **speculum (Sims or Cusco)** and a good light for inspection, a **penguin suction bulb** and a **bag and mask (sizes 0 and 1)**, a clock with a second hand, a baby cap, a weighing scale, vitamin K, tetracycline eye ointment, chlorhexidine gel, a **calibrated under-buttock drape** where available, and a sharps container.

### Suture materials: principles

| Property | Options | Clinical meaning |
|---|---|---|
| **Absorbable vs non-absorbable** | Absorbable: polyglactin 910 (Vicryl, Vicryl Rapide), polyglycolic acid (Dexon), polydioxanone (PDS), chromic catgut. Non-absorbable: nylon, silk, polypropylene (Prolene) | **Perineal repairs use absorbable sutures** so they do not need removal |
| **Natural vs synthetic** | Natural: catgut (sheep/cattle intestine), silk. Synthetic: Vicryl, PDS, nylon | Synthetics cause **less tissue reaction and less pain**; catgut is absorbed by inflammation (proteolysis) |
| **Braided (multifilament) vs monofilament** | Braided: Vicryl, silk. Monofilament: PDS, nylon, Prolene | Braided: easier to tie, holds knots, but can harbour bacteria. Monofilament: slides through tissue, less infection |
| **Absorption time** | Vicryl Rapide: loses strength in about 10-14 days, absorbed in about 6 weeks. Vicryl: absorbed in about 8-10 weeks. PDS: strength for about 6 weeks, absorbed in about 6 months | Rapid-absorbing sutures are ideal for the perineum (less pain, fewer suture removals). **PDS** for the sphincter because it needs support for longer |
| **Size (gauge)** | Bigger number followed by "-0" = thinner (3-0 thinner than 2-0 thinner than 0 thinner than 1) | 2-0 for vaginal mucosa and muscle; 3-0 for skin and sphincter |
| **Needle** | **Round-bodied (taper)**: pushes tissue apart (muscle, mucosa, bowel). **Cutting / reverse cutting**: cuts through tough tissue (skin) | Use a **round-bodied** needle in the perineum; a large curved needle (e.g. 35 mm half-circle) is easier for the vagina |

> [!UGANDA]
> Many Ugandan units still stock **chromic catgut 2-0 or 0**. It works but causes more pain and needs removal more often. **Polyglactin (Vicryl or Vicryl Rapide) 2-0** is preferred where available (WHO, RCOG, Cochrane evidence). Use what your ward has, but know why synthetic is better.

### First 5 minutes: when you are called "she is pushing"

1. **Confirm the second stage**: urge to push, anal pouting, head visible, or full dilatation on VE.
2. **FHR now** (for 1 minute after a contraction): 110–160 bpm. Abnormal → call a senior and prepare for assisted delivery and resuscitation.
3. **Maternal pulse and BP**: BP 160/110 or more → no ergometrine, treat severe hypertension; pulse 120 or more → look for bleeding, sepsis or exhaustion.
4. **Quick risk scan of the chart**: previous CS, big baby, prolonged second stage, meconium, twins, anaemia, HIV, pre-eclampsia. Any one of these → a senior should be present or near.
5. **Set up**: gloves and PPE, delivery pack open, **oxytocin 10 IU drawn up**, newborn corner ready with **bag and mask tested**, clock visible, someone to call for help.

### Focused history: what to ask, and why

**Before delivery** (a 1-minute checklist; each answer changes what you prepare):

| Question | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| **Parity, previous deliveries and perineal trauma** (third-degree tear, repair, incontinence, fistula repair) | Previous OASI or fistula repair: a senior should decide the mode of delivery and be present; watch for recurrence |
| **Previous CS** | Watch for scar rupture; delivery where theatre is available |
| **Estimated fetal weight, diabetes, prolonged second stage** | Macrosomia: prepare for **shoulder dystocia** (HELPERR, extra help, a senior in the room); OASI risk |
| **Twins, or uncertain number** | Do not give oxytocin until a second baby is excluded |
| **Meconium, abnormal FHR, preterm** | Skilled resuscitator at the birth |
| **HIV status**, viral load | Avoid unnecessary episiotomy; safe sharps handling; infant prophylaxis ready |
| **Hypertension/pre-eclampsia, cardiac disease** | No ergometrine |
| **Anaemia, previous PPH, grand multiparity** | IV line in place, blood grouped, active PPH prevention |
| **FGM** | May need anterior defibulation; scarred perineum tears unpredictably |
| **Allergies** (lignocaine, iodine, latex) | Choose safe agents |
| **Bleeding disorders, anticoagulants** | Haematoma risk |

**After delivery**: ask about **pain** (severe perineal or rectal pain = haematoma), **dizziness** (blood loss), **urge to pass urine** (retention), and feeling of **anal urgency or loss of control** (missed OASI).

### Pre-delivery examination (Hutchison order)

1. **Introduce yourself, consent, privacy, companion**; **hand hygiene**.
2. **General inspection**: distress, exhaustion, pallor, bleeding; **vital signs** (pulse, BP).
3. **Obstetric abdominal examination**: inspection, fundal height, **lie**, **presentation**, **descent in fifths**, contractions, **auscultation of the FHR** for 1 minute after a contraction.
4. **VE** (if not recently confirmed): full dilatation, position, moulding, caput, station.
5. **Perineum**: stretching normally, or rigid/scarred/FGM.

| Check | Normal to proceed | Stop and call a senior if... |
|---|---|---|
| Lie and presentation | Longitudinal, cephalic | Breech, transverse, face/brow found late |
| Descent | 0/5–1/5 palpable, head visible or low | Head still 2/5 or more at full dilatation |
| Position (VE) | OA (posterior fontanelle anterior) | OP/OT with slow second stage |
| Moulding / caput | 0 to + | +++ (CPD) |
| FHR | 110–160, no late decelerations | Abnormal → expedite (vacuum if criteria met) |
| Maternal state | Pulse, BP normal; able to push | Exhausted, bleeding, BP 160/110 or more |
| Perineum | Stretching normally | Rigid, scarred or FGM type III (needs defibulation) |

### Consent and positioning

- **Consent** for delivery, any episiotomy (explain why it is needed) and any repair.
- **Position** the woman as she prefers (semi-recumbent, left lateral, squatting, all fours). For the mannequin OSCE, semi-recumbent with legs apart. For **repair**: lithotomy (or at the edge of the bed) with **good light**.

## 5. Procedure Step by Step

### A. Preparing for delivery

1. **Greet and consent**: introduce yourself, explain, ask permission; ensure privacy and a companion.
2. **Hand hygiene**, apron, eye protection; **sterile gloves**.
3. **Check equipment**: open the delivery pack on a clean trolley; oxytocin 10 IU drawn up; **newborn corner** ready (warm, towels, cap, bag and mask checked, suction bulb, clock).
4. **Position** the woman as she prefers.
5. **Clean the vulva and perineum** with antiseptic (cotton swabs held in sponge forceps: from above downwards, front to back, one stroke per swab, inner to outer). Drape under the buttocks.
6. **Empty the bladder** if full and she cannot void.

### B. Conducting the delivery (simulated or real)

1. **Confirm full dilatation** (if not already confirmed) and monitor FHR every 5 min.
2. **Encourage spontaneous pushing** with contractions; rest between.
3. As the head distends the perineum, apply a **warm compress** and support the perineum with a pad in the right hand (the "hands-on" technique).
4. **Keep the head flexed**: place the fingers of the left hand gently on the occiput to control the speed of delivery, not to hold it back forcefully.
5. **At crowning**: ask her to **stop pushing and pant** (or blow), so the head delivers slowly between contractions.
6. If an episiotomy is indicated, perform it now (see C).
7. **Deliver the head** by extension: occiput, then forehead, face and chin sweep over the perineum.
8. **Wipe the baby's face** (mouth and nose) with gauze; do **not** routinely suction.
9. **Check for the cord around the neck**: loose, slip it over the head (or let the body somersault through); tight, clamp twice and cut between (only if necessary).
10. **Wait for restitution and external rotation** with the next contraction.
11. **Deliver the anterior shoulder** with gentle downward traction (hands over the parietal bones and cheeks, **never pulling on the neck**), then the **posterior shoulder** with gentle upward traction, while watching the perineum.
12. **Deliver the body** by lateral flexion (following the curve of the birth canal) and place the baby on the mother's abdomen. **Note the time of birth.**
13. **Dry thoroughly**, assess breathing, keep skin-to-skin, cover. If not breathing: Golden Minute (clear airway if needed, stimulate, then ventilate; see the neonatal resuscitation topic).
14. **Palpate the abdomen to exclude another baby**, then give **oxytocin 10 IU IM** within 1 minute of birth. Watch the blood loss from the moment the baby is born (use a **calibrated drape** where available).
15. **Delay cord clamping 1-3 minutes** (if the baby is breathing), then clamp/tie at about 2 cm and 5 cm and cut between with cord scissors, shielding the cut with gauze.
16. **Controlled cord traction** with counter-traction when the uterus contracts; deliver the placenta and membranes.
17. **Massage/check the uterus**; check tone every 15 min for 2 hours.
18. **Examine the placenta** (cotyledons, membranes, vessels, cord insertion, 3 vessels).
19. **Inspect the perineum, vagina and cervix**; perform a rectal examination; repair tears.
20. **Estimate blood loss**, clean the woman, sanitary pad, make her comfortable, help her breastfeed.
21. **Dispose** of sharps, decontaminate instruments (0.5% chlorine), hand hygiene, **document**.

### C. Performing a mediolateral episiotomy

1. **Explain** why it is needed and get consent.
2. **Infiltrate 1% lignocaine** (about **10 mL**) along the planned line: insert the needle at the fourchette, direct it about **60 degrees from the midline** (towards the ischial tuberosity on the mother's right usually), with two fingers of the other hand in the vagina between the fetal head and the perineum to protect the head. **Aspirate before injecting** (to avoid intravascular injection), inject while withdrawing. Fan into the vaginal mucosa, skin and muscle.
3. Wait **about 2 minutes** for the anaesthetic to work (test with forceps).
4. At **crowning** (when the perineum is thinned and 3-4 cm of head is visible during a contraction), insert two fingers between the head and perineum and cut with **episiotomy scissors in a single, decisive cut of about 3-4 cm**, starting at the midpoint of the fourchette and directed **60 degrees** away from the midline.
5. **Control the head** carefully after the cut (it can deliver suddenly and extend the incision).
6. If there is bleeding before birth, apply pressure with a swab.

> [!DRUG]
> **Lignocaine (lidocaine) 1%** = 10 mg/mL. Maximum dose without adrenaline is about **3 mg/kg** (about 18-20 mL for a 60 kg woman). 10 mL is usually enough for an episiotomy or second-degree repair. Always **aspirate before injecting**. Signs of toxicity: perioral tingling, metallic taste, tinnitus, drowsiness, seizures, arrhythmias; stop injecting and call for help.

### D. The fourth-stage sweep (examination after delivery)

In this order, within the first 15–30 minutes, then tone and bleeding every 15 minutes for 2 hours:

1. **Uterine tone**: firm, at or below the umbilicus?
2. **Blood loss**: measured (calibrated drape, weighed pads).
3. **Placenta and membranes**: lay out on a flat surface; maternal side, fetal side, membranes, cord vessels.
4. **Inspection in good light**: labia, periurethral area, vaginal walls (sulci), perineum, and the **cervix** with a speculum if bleeding persists with a contracted uterus. **Assess the apex** of vaginal tears (if you cannot see it, you cannot repair it safely).
5. **Rectal examination** (every time): finger in the rectum, thumb on the perineum, "**pill-rolling**" to feel the sphincter thickness; look for a **rectal mucosal tear**.
6. **Vital signs**: pulse, BP, shock index (bleeding may be from atony, not the tear).
7. **Bladder**: palpable? voided?
8. **Baby**: breathing, warm, skin-to-skin, breastfeeding.

### E. Repairing an episiotomy or a second-degree tear

Principles: **good light, adequate analgesia, an assistant if possible, identify the apex, restore anatomy layer by layer, avoid dead space, do not over-tighten** (tissues swell).

1. **Explain and get consent.** Position in lithotomy (or at the edge of the bed) with good light.
2. **Clean** the area; drape; **check the placenta is complete** and the uterus contracted before starting.
3. **Inspect systematically** (vagina, cervix if bleeding, perineum) and **do a rectal examination** to assess the sphincter (pill-rolling the EAS between the finger in the rectum and the thumb on the perineum).
4. **Re-infiltrate lignocaine** if needed (check the total dose).
5. Insert a **tampon/gauze roll with a tail** high in the vagina if blood from above obscures the view (and **remember to remove it**; count it).
6. **Vaginal mucosa**: identify the **apex**; place the first stitch **about 1 cm above the apex** (to catch retracted bleeding vessels). Close the vaginal mucosa with a **continuous non-locking** suture of **2-0 polyglactin** down to the hymenal ring, bringing the edges of the fourchette together.
7. **Perineal muscles**: bring the needle through the fourchette into the perineal wound and close the **muscle layer** (bulbospongiosus and transverse perineal) with the same continuous non-locking suture (or interrupted sutures), ensuring the perineal body is rebuilt and there is **no dead space**. Deep muscle may need a second layer.
8. **Skin**: close with a **continuous subcuticular** suture (just under the skin) ending with a buried knot in the vagina behind the hymen; or interrupted 2-0/3-0 sutures if skill is limited.
9. **Rectal examination again** to make sure **no suture has gone through the rectal mucosa** (if so, remove it and redo; otherwise a rectovaginal fistula can form).
10. **Remove the tampon/gauze roll**; **count swabs, needles and instruments**.
11. Clean, apply a pad, make her comfortable; document the degree of tear, repair, suture used, EBL and counts.

> [!NOTE]
> The **continuous non-locking technique** for all layers causes **less pain** at 10 days than interrupted sutures and uses less suture material (Cochrane). Locking ("blanket") stitches strangulate tissue and increase pain.

### F. Repairing a first-degree tear

- Small, non-bleeding, well-aligned first-degree tears and labial grazes **can be left unsutured** (after discussion with the woman), as they heal well.
- Suture if bleeding, if the edges gape, or if anatomy is distorted, using 2-0 or 3-0 polyglactin (continuous for skin/mucosa).

### G. Third- and fourth-degree tears (OASI): what the student must know

1. **Recognise** it: gaping perineum with the dark red, retracted ends of the EAS; **pale IAS**; torn anorectal mucosa. The finger in the rectum shows a thin or absent sphincter anteriorly.
2. **Call the senior doctor/obstetrician.** Do not attempt the repair.
3. Apply pressure with a pad to control bleeding; keep her comfortable; document.
4. **Repair in theatre** under **spinal or general anaesthesia** (relaxes the sphincter so the ends can be approximated), good light, proper instruments (Allis forceps).
5. **Technique** (by the experienced surgeon): anorectal mucosa with **3-0 polyglactin** (continuous or interrupted), **IAS separately with 3-0 PDS** (or 2-0 polyglactin) end-to-end, **EAS** with **3-0 PDS** or 2-0 polyglactin by **end-to-end (3a, some 3b) or overlapping (full thickness) technique**, then the perineal muscles, vagina and skin as for a second-degree repair.
6. **Post-repair care** (RCOG):
   - **Broad-spectrum antibiotics** (for example intraoperative cefuroxime 1.5 g IV + metronidazole 500 mg IV, then an oral course such as amoxicillin-clavulanate + metronidazole for about 5-7 days, per local protocol), to reduce infection and wound breakdown.
   - **Stool softeners/laxatives** (lactulose 10-15 mL twice daily for about 10 days) so that the first stools do not disrupt the repair; avoid constipating codeine.
   - **Analgesia** (paracetamol, NSAID).
   - **Urinary catheter** for about 12-24 h (pain and swelling cause retention).
   - **Physiotherapy** (pelvic floor exercises) and **follow-up at 6-12 weeks** for continence; counsel about future deliveries.

> [!UGANDA]
> At an HC III or HC IV without an experienced surgeon, a woman with a suspected OASI should be **referred** (after haemostasis with a pressure pack) to a hospital where a skilled obstetrician can repair it in theatre. A good primary repair offers the best chance of continence; later secondary repairs are much less successful.

## 6. Findings and Interpretation

### Perineal tear classification (RCOG Green-top Guideline 29, used internationally)

| Degree | Structures injured |
|---|---|
| **First** | Injury to **perineal skin and/or vaginal mucosa** only |
| **Second** | Injury to the **perineal muscles** (bulbospongiosus, transverse perinei) but **not the anal sphincter** |
| **Third** | Injury to the **anal sphincter complex** |
| 3a | **Less than 50% of the external anal sphincter (EAS)** thickness torn |
| 3b | **More than 50% of EAS** thickness torn |
| 3c | **Both EAS and internal anal sphincter (IAS)** torn |
| **Fourth** | Injury to the **anal sphincter complex (EAS and IAS) and the anorectal mucosa** |
| Rectal buttonhole | Tear of the rectal mucosa with an **intact sphincter**; not a fourth-degree tear, but must be repaired and followed up |

> [!EXAM]
> If you are unsure whether a tear is 3a or 3b, **classify it as the higher degree** (RCOG). Under-classification leads to inadequate repair.

**OASI** = obstetric anal sphincter injury (third- and fourth-degree tears).

### Other lower genital tract injuries

- **Labial and periurethral tears**: often bleed; repair if bleeding or anatomy distorted (catheterise if near the urethra).
- **Vaginal wall (sulcus) tears**: high up in the lateral fornices, often after instrumental delivery; can bleed heavily.
- **Cervical tears**: bleeding with a well-contracted uterus; inspect with a speculum and sponge forceps.
- **Vulval/paravaginal haematoma**: severe perineal pain and a tense bluish swelling after delivery; may need evacuation.

### Fourth-stage sweep: what each finding means

| Order | Check | Normal finding to document | Abnormal finding and action |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Uterine tone** | Firm, contracted, at or below the umbilicus | Soft, boggy, above the umbilicus → massage, repeat uterotonic, empty bladder; treat as PPH if bleeding |
| 2 | **Blood loss** | Under 500 mL (calibrated drape / weighed pads) | 500 mL or more, or 300 mL or more with an abnormal vital sign → E-MOTIVE bundle |
| 3 | **Placenta and membranes** | Complete: all cotyledons, membranes complete, 3 cord vessels, central/eccentric cord insertion | Missing cotyledon or vessels running to a torn membrane edge (**succenturiate lobe**) → exploration/manual removal; 2 vessels → examine the baby |
| 4 | **Perineum, vagina, cervix** in good light | Intact, or 1st/2nd-degree tear | 3rd/4th-degree, high vaginal or cervical tear → senior, theatre |
| 5 | **Rectal examination** | Sphincter intact (pill-rolling), no mucosal tear | Thin or absent sphincter anteriorly → OASI |
| 6 | **Vital signs** | Pulse under 100, BP normal, shock index under 0.9 | Shock index 0.9 or more → escalate; 1.4 or more → urgent resuscitation and blood |
| 7 | **Bladder** | Voided within 6 h | Palpable bladder, unable to void → catheterise |
| 8 | **Baby** | Breathing, warm, skin-to-skin, breastfeeding within 1 h | Grunting, cyanosis, cold → neonatal review |

### Bedside tests

| Test | How | What it tells you |
|---|---|---|
| **Placental examination** | Lay it out on a flat surface: maternal side (cotyledons fit together, no ragged area), fetal side and membranes (holes, vessels to the edge), count cord vessels, note cord length and insertion | Completeness → decides on exploration/manual removal |
| **Measured blood loss** | Calibrated under-buttock drape, or weigh pads and linen (1 g ≈ 1 mL) | Visual estimates underestimate by up to half; measuring triggers earlier PPH treatment |
| **Shock index** | Pulse ÷ systolic BP | 0.9 or more abnormal; 1.4 or more urgent intervention |
| **HemoCue** | After a large blood loss, or before discharge if anaemic | Baseline for transfusion and iron decisions (a very early Hb underestimates loss) |
| **Bedside clotting test** | 2 mL venous blood in a plain glass tube | No clot by 7 min or a clot that breaks down → coagulopathy |
| **Rectal examination** | Before and after any repair | Sphincter integrity; no suture through the mucosa |
| **Swab, needle and instrument count** | Before starting and after finishing a repair | A retained swab causes sepsis and fistula |

### Laboratory and imaging

A normal delivery needs no laboratory tests. They become relevant when the checks above are abnormal.

| Test | When | Expected / abnormal finding | How it changes management |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Hb / FBC** | PPH, pallor, symptomatic, known anaemia | Hb 11 g/dL or more before delivery; a fall after PPH (may take hours to show) | Iron (ferrous sulphate + folic acid) vs transfusion. HemoCue at HC III/IV; FBC at hospitals |
| **Group and crossmatch** | Blood loss 500 mL or more, shock, haematoma, OASI going to theatre | Compatible units | Transfusion; HC III must refer for blood |
| **Blood group of mother and cord blood** | Mother Rh negative | Baby Rh positive | **Anti-D within 72 h** |
| **Coagulation (PT/APTT, fibrinogen, platelets)** | Massive PPH, abruption, clotting test abnormal | Fibrinogen under 2 g/L = severe coagulopathy | FFP, cryoprecipitate, platelets. Regional/national referral only; bedside clotting test elsewhere |
| **Wound swab culture** | Infected perineal wound | Organism and sensitivity | Tailor antibiotics. Regional/national referral |
| **Pelvic ultrasound** | Suspected retained products, haematoma, persistent bleeding with a contracted uterus | Retained tissue; collection | Evacuation or exploration in theatre. HC IV and hospitals, operator-dependent |
| **Endoanal ultrasound / anorectal studies** | After OASI with symptoms | Sphincter defect | Plan for future delivery. Rarely available in Uganda (national referral / private) |

### Red flags

> [!REDFLAG]
> - **Brisk bleeding** from a tear with a contracted uterus: trauma causing PPH. Apply pressure, call for help, repair promptly in good light.
> - **Cannot see the apex** of a vaginal tear or suspect a **cervical/high vaginal tear**: needs a senior, possibly theatre.
> - **Sphincter involvement** (3rd/4th degree): senior repair in theatre.
> - **Severe perineal or rectal pain** and a swelling after repair, with tachycardia: **haematoma** (can conceal a litre of blood).
> - **Urinary retention** after repair: catheterise.
> - Later: **fever, foul discharge, wound breakdown** (infection); **faecal or flatus incontinence**; **passage of stool/flatus per vagina** (rectovaginal fistula).
> - **Shoulder dystocia** during delivery: HELPERR drill.

> [!REDFLAG]
> Stop and call for help if: the head delivers and retracts (**turtle sign**: shoulder dystocia), there is a **gush of bright blood with a soft uterus** (PPH), the cord **snaps** during CCT, a **mass appears at the introitus with shock** after CCT (uterine inversion), or the mother collapses.

## 7. Clinical Acumen

### Thinking like a clinician

- **"Bleeding after delivery: is it tone or trauma?"** If the uterus is well contracted and bleeding continues (often bright red, steady), **look for a tear**. If the uterus is soft, rub it up first.
- **"Is this a 2nd or 3rd?"** Only the rectal exam tells you. A perineum that looks like a 2nd-degree tear can hide a torn EAS whose ends have retracted.
- **"Should I cut an episiotomy?"** Only if there is an indication (fetal distress with a tight perineum as the delaying factor, instrumental delivery). Tears usually heal as well as or better than episiotomies.
- **"Suture through the rectum?"** Always finish with a rectal exam.
- **Shock out of proportion to visible bleeding with a firm uterus** → concealed bleeding (haematoma, high vaginal tear).
- **Visual estimates underestimate blood loss**: measure, and trust the vital signs.

### Common mistakes (pitfalls)

- Starting below the apex (bleeding continues from retracted vessels above).
- Tying too tightly (pain, necrosis).
- Leaving dead space (haematoma).
- Forgetting the vaginal tampon.
- Failing to count swabs.
- Not giving enough anaesthetic (the woman suffers and moves, and the repair is poor).
- Classifying a tear without a rectal examination.

### Worked examples

> [!EXAM]
> **Worked example 1: the placenta and the bleeding.** Twenty minutes after an SVD, a P4 has a firm uterus after oxytocin, but is still trickling. On the placenta, the maternal surface is complete, but **a blood vessel runs across the membranes to a torn hole at the edge**. **Interpretation**: a **succenturiate lobe** has probably been left behind (retained placental tissue). **Action**: call a senior, IV access, second uterotonic, **manual exploration of the uterus** under analgesia with prophylactic antibiotics; watch for PPH.

> [!EXAM]
> **Worked example 2: the vital signs tell the story.** One hour after delivery, a primipara's pad count is "moderate" and the uterus is firm. Pulse has risen from 84 to **118**, BP **98/60** (shock index 1.2), and she has **severe rectal pain**. **Interpretation**: a firm uterus with shock out of proportion to the visible bleeding and rectal pain = **concealed bleeding into a vulval/paravaginal haematoma** (or an unseen high vaginal tear). **Action**: call for help, two IV lines, crossmatch, tranexamic acid 1 g IV (within 3 h of birth), examine in good light; a large or expanding haematoma needs **evacuation in theatre**.

> [!EXAM]
> **Worked example 3: classifying the tear.** After a big baby, you see a perineal tear that reaches the anal margin. On rectal examination, with a finger in the rectum and the thumb on the perineum, the anterior sphincter feels thin, and you can see torn, dark-red external sphincter fibres, but you cannot tell whether more or less than half its thickness is torn. The pale internal sphincter and the rectal mucosa look intact. **Interpretation**: a third-degree tear; because you are unsure between 3a and 3b, **classify it as 3b** (the higher degree, RCOG). **Action**: pressure pad, do not repair yourself, call the obstetrician, repair in theatre under spinal, antibiotics, laxatives, follow-up.

### Decision flow when you finish a delivery

1. Uterus contracted? If not, massage and uterotonics first.
2. Placenta complete? If not, explore.
3. Inspect in good light: where is the bleeding from?
4. Rectal exam: is the sphincter intact?
5. **First/second-degree**: repair now (you, under supervision). **Third/fourth-degree or cervical/high vaginal**: call senior; theatre.
6. Count, document, analgesia, counsel.

## 8. Complications and Troubleshooting

### Shoulder dystocia: the HELPERR drill (know it, even as a student)

| Letter | Action |
|---|---|
| **H** | Call for **Help** (senior, midwife, neonatal help); note the time the head was delivered |
| **E** | Evaluate for **Episiotomy** (to make room for internal manoeuvres) |
| **L** | **Legs**: McRoberts manoeuvre (hyperflex the mother's thighs onto her abdomen): resolves many cases |
| **P** | **Suprapubic Pressure** (not fundal pressure) over the posterior aspect of the anterior shoulder to adduct and rotate it |
| **E** | **Enter** the vagina for internal rotation manoeuvres (Rubin II, Woods screw) |
| **R** | **Remove the posterior arm** |
| **R** | **Roll** the patient onto all fours (Gaskin manoeuvre) |

Avoid excessive downward traction on the head (brachial plexus injury, Erb's palsy) and **never fundal pressure** (worsens impaction, risks uterine rupture).

### Managing complications

| Complication | Management |
|---|---|
| **Bleeding from tear** | Pressure, good light, find the apex, suture above it; if cannot see, call senior; treat as PPH (IV access, fluids, tranexamic acid 1 g IV within 3 h of birth) |
| **Vulval/paravaginal haematoma** | Small (under 5 cm), stable: analgesia, ice packs, observe. Large or expanding: **evacuate in theatre**, ligate bleeders, drain/pack, catheterise, transfuse if needed, antibiotics |
| **Retained placental tissue** (e.g. succenturiate lobe) | Senior, IV access, second uterotonic, manual exploration under analgesia with prophylactic antibiotics |
| **Wound infection** | Clean, analgesia, antibiotics (e.g. amoxicillin-clavulanate + metronidazole, or per sensitivity); remove infected sutures if needed |
| **Wound breakdown (dehiscence)** | Usually heal by secondary intention after infection clears; resuture only when clean (senior decision) |
| **Retained swab** | Foul discharge and fever days later; remove, antibiotics; prevention by counting |
| **Suture through rectum** | Remove and resuture; if missed, may cause rectovaginal fistula: refer to fistula surgeon |
| **Urinary retention** | Catheterise |
| **Dyspareunia, pain** | Assess at 6 weeks; may need revision |
| **Anal incontinence** after OASI | Physiotherapy, endoanal ultrasound where available, colorectal/urogynaecology review; discuss mode of next delivery (elective CS if symptomatic or abnormal studies) |

### Routine postnatal perineal care

1. **Analgesia**: paracetamol **1 g orally every 6-8 hours** (max 4 g/day) plus, if no contraindication (asthma, pre-eclampsia with renal impairment, PPH with coagulopathy), **ibuprofen 400 mg orally every 8 hours** or **diclofenac 50 mg every 8 hours** with food, for about 3-5 days.
2. **Hygiene**: wash the perineum with clean water after passing urine or stool, pat dry, change pads every 4-6 hours; salt sitz baths are popular in Uganda (warm water; avoid very strong salt solutions or herbal insertions).
3. **Bladder**: ensure she passes urine within 6 hours; catheterise if not.
4. **Bowels**: fluids, fibre; laxatives after OASI.
5. **Counsel**: danger signs (fever, increasing pain, foul discharge, wound breakdown, incontinence), pelvic floor exercises, and when to resume intercourse (when healed and comfortable, usually after 6 weeks), family planning.

| Drug | Dose | Route | Frequency | Duration / notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Oxytocin (AMTSL) | 10 IU | IM | Once, within 1 min of birth | After excluding a second twin |
| Misoprostol (if no oxytocin) | 600 micrograms | Oral | Once | AMTSL alternative |
| Tranexamic acid (PPH) | 1 g over 10 min | IV | Once; second 1 g if bleeding continues after 30 min | Within 3 h of birth |
| Paracetamol | 1 g | Oral | 6-8 hourly | 3-5 days; max 4 g/day |
| Ibuprofen | 400 mg | Oral | 8 hourly with food | 3-5 days; avoid in asthma, renal impairment, severe pre-eclampsia |
| Diclofenac | 50 mg (or 100 mg rectal once after repair) | Oral/PR | 8 hourly | As above |
| Lignocaine 1% | Up to about 3 mg/kg (usually 10 mL) | Local infiltration | Once during repair | Aspirate first |
| Lactulose (after OASI) | 10-15 mL | Oral | Twice daily | About 10 days |
| Antibiotics (after OASI) | e.g. cefuroxime 1.5 g IV + metronidazole 500 mg IV intraoperatively, then oral amoxicillin-clavulanate 625 mg 8 hourly + metronidazole 400 mg 8 hourly | IV then oral | – | About 5-7 days (per local protocol) |

### Complications by source

- **Of delivery**: perineal/vaginal/cervical tears, PPH, shoulder dystocia, cord avulsion, uterine inversion (cord traction without counter-traction), neonatal injury (brachial plexus, clavicle fracture).
- **Of episiotomy**: extension to OASI, bleeding, haematoma, infection, dehiscence, dyspareunia, poor cosmetic result.
- **Of repair**: pain, infection, dehiscence, retained swab, suture through rectum (rectovaginal fistula), granulation tissue, long-term dyspareunia.
- **Of missed OASI**: faecal/flatus incontinence, perineal body deficiency, rectovaginal fistula, psychosexual problems.

### Troubleshooting during the repair

| Problem | What to do |
|---|---|
| Blood from above obscures the view | Vaginal tampon/gauze roll with a tail (count it, remove it) |
| Cannot see the apex | Better light, assistant to retract; if still not seen, call a senior |
| Woman in pain during repair | Stop; top up lignocaine within the maximum dose |
| Suture felt in the rectum on final PR | Remove and redo that layer |
| Count incorrect | Do not finish; search the vagina, bed and bins before she leaves |

## 9. Documentation and Presenting Findings

**Model one-line summary**: "A 23-year-old P1 who had an SVD of a live 3.6 kg baby at 03:10 with AMTSL; placenta complete, measured blood loss 350 mL, uterus firm, **second-degree tear with an intact sphincter** repaired with 2-0 polyglactin; vital signs normal."

**Problem list**: (1) post-SVD, day 0; (2) second-degree perineal tear, repaired; (3) PPH risk period (first 2 hours); (4) breastfeeding and PPFP.

**Working diagnosis**: "Uncomplicated spontaneous vaginal delivery with a repaired second-degree tear."

**Model presentation (postnatal ward round)**

"Mrs L.N., a 23-year-old primipara, had a spontaneous vaginal delivery at 03:10 of a live male infant, 3.6 kg, Apgar 8 and 10. AMTSL was given with oxytocin 10 IU IM; the placenta and membranes were complete; estimated blood loss 350 mL. She sustained a **second-degree perineal tear**, confirmed on rectal examination with an **intact anal sphincter**, repaired under 1% lignocaine with **continuous 2-0 Vicryl Rapide** in three layers; rectal examination after repair was normal and swab and needle counts were correct. **Today** she is afebrile, the uterus is well contracted, lochia normal, perineum mildly oedematous, she has passed urine and is breastfeeding. **Plan**: analgesia (paracetamol and ibuprofen), perineal hygiene, pelvic floor exercises, discharge in 24 h with review of the perineum at the postnatal visit."

**The delivery and repair note must record**: time of birth, sex, weight, Apgar; uterotonic and time; placenta and membranes complete; measured blood loss; degree of tear (with rectal examination findings); anaesthetic and dose; suture material and technique; rectal examination after repair; swab and needle counts; vital signs; plan.

## 11. Key Points

### High-yield points

- **Controlled delivery of the head** (flex, pant at crowning) is the best tear prevention.
- **Exclude a second twin**, then oxytocin 10 IU IM within 1 minute.
- **Delay cord clamping** 1-3 minutes in a vigorous baby.
- **Episiotomy is restrictive**, mediolateral, about 60 degrees, at crowning.
- **Rectal examination before and after** every repair.
- **Unsure 3a or 3b → call it 3b.** OASI goes to theatre with an experienced surgeon.
- First stitch **1 cm above the apex**; continuous non-locking 2-0 polyglactin.
- **Firm uterus + bleeding = trauma or retained tissue**: look in good light.
- **Measure** blood loss; PPH is 500 mL or more (or 300 mL with abnormal vital signs).
- Shoulder dystocia: **HELPERR**, McRoberts + suprapubic pressure, never fundal pressure.
- **Count** swabs, needles and instruments before and after.

### Exam traps

- **"The perineum looks like a second-degree tear, so it is one."** → Only a **rectal examination** can exclude a torn sphincter whose ends have retracted.
- **"Unsure whether 3a or 3b: call it 3a."** → Wrong. **Classify higher** (3b) so the repair is adequate.
- **"Routine episiotomy for every primigravida prevents tears."** → Wrong. WHO: **restrictive episiotomy** only; it does not prevent OASI.
- **"Bleeding after delivery: give more oxytocin."** → Only if the uterus is soft. **Firm uterus + ongoing bleeding = trauma** (tear, cervix) or retained tissue: look.
- **"Placenta looks complete on the maternal side, so all is well."** → Also check the **membranes for vessels running to a torn edge** (succenturiate lobe).
- **"Pull on the cord to speed up the third stage."** → Only CCT **during a contraction with counter-traction**; traction on a relaxed uterus causes **inversion**.
- **"Shoulder dystocia: apply fundal pressure and pull harder."** → Wrong. **McRoberts + suprapubic pressure**; fundal pressure worsens impaction; hard traction causes Erb's palsy.
- **"Visual estimate of blood loss was 300 mL, so no PPH."** → Visual estimates **underestimate**; measure, and use vital signs (shock index).

### Questions seniors ask

**Viva questions**

1. **Name the instruments in a delivery pack and their uses.** (See the equipment table, section 4.)
2. **Why mediolateral rather than midline?** Lower risk of extension into the anal sphincter.
3. **What angle and when?** About 60 degrees from the midline, at crowning.
4. **Classify a tear with less than 50% of the EAS torn.** 3a.
5. **Why rectal examination after repair?** To exclude a suture through the rectal mucosa (fistula risk) and confirm sphincter integrity.
6. **Why is the first stitch placed above the apex?** To catch retracted bleeding vessels at the apex.
7. **Which suture for the perineum and why?** Rapidly absorbed synthetic polyglactin 2-0: less pain, less need for suture removal than catgut.
8. **Maximum dose of lignocaine without adrenaline?** About 3 mg/kg.
9. **What is the management of a large vulval haematoma?** Evacuation in theatre, haemostasis, catheter, antibiotics, blood as needed.
10. **What are the key steps in shoulder dystocia?** HELPERR: call for help, McRoberts plus suprapubic pressure first; no fundal pressure.

**On the labour ward**

1. **"What do you check before you give the oxytocin?"** That there is **no second baby** (palpate the abdomen); then oxytocin 10 IU IM within 1 minute.
2. **"Walk me through your checks after the placenta is out."** Uterine tone, blood loss, placenta and membranes (and 3 vessels), perineum/vagina/cervix in good light, rectal exam, vital signs, bladder, baby.
3. **"How do you know the placenta is complete?"** Cotyledons fit together without ragged gaps, membranes are complete (both amnion and chorion), and no vessels run to a torn edge.
4. **"The uterus is firm but she keeps bleeding. What next?"** Trauma until proved otherwise: good light, speculum, find the source and the apex; call a senior for cervical or high vaginal tears; treat as PPH (IV access, TXA).
5. **"Why the first stitch above the apex?"** To catch retracted bleeding vessels at the apex.
6. **"Why a rectal exam after the repair?"** To make sure no suture has gone through the rectal mucosa (rectovaginal fistula) and to recheck the sphincter.
7. **"She has severe perineal pain and a pulse of 120 an hour after repair. What is it?"** A **haematoma** until proved otherwise; examine, resuscitate, and evacuate in theatre if large or expanding.

### Mnemonics

- **Tear degrees**: "**1 Skin, 2 Muscle, 3 Sphincter (a-b-c), 4 Mucosa**" ("Some Men Sleep Much").
- **3a/3b/3c**: "**a** = **a** bit of EAS (under half); **b** = **big** bit of EAS (over half); **c** = **complete** plus the internal sphincter."
- **Shoulder dystocia**: **HELPERR** (Help, Episiotomy, Legs, Pressure, Enter, Remove, Roll).
- **Repair order**: "**Apex, Vagina, Muscle, Skin, Rectum, Count**" ("A Very Methodical Surgeon Rechecks Carefully").
- **Suture sizes**: "**More zeros, more slender**" (3-0 is thinner than 2-0).

### OSCE tips

- **Talk as you do**: "I am supporting the perineum and keeping the head flexed... I am asking her to pant now."
- Always say "I will palpate for a second twin before giving oxytocin," and "I will do a rectal examination before and after the repair."
- On the suture station: pick up the needle holder correctly (thumb and ring finger), load the needle at the junction of the middle and posterior thirds, and use instrument ties.
