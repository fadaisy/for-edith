## 1. Definition

A **urinary tract infection (UTI)** in pregnancy ranges from **asymptomatic bacteriuria (ASB)**, bacteria growing silently in the bladder, through **acute cystitis** (bladder infection with symptoms) to **acute pyelonephritis** (kidney infection with fever and loin pain).

Pregnancy **does not make bacteriuria more common**, but it makes bacteriuria **far more dangerous**: the dilated, sluggish ureters let bacteria climb to the kidney. That is why a condition we usually ignore in non-pregnant women (ASB) is **screened for and treated** in pregnancy.

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Significant bacteriuria** | **10⁵ colony-forming units (CFU)/mL or more of a single uropathogen** on a clean-catch midstream urine (MSU) culture (lower counts count on a catheter specimen) |
| **Asymptomatic bacteriuria (ASB)** | Significant bacteriuria **without symptoms** |
| **Acute cystitis** (lower UTI) | Bladder infection: dysuria, frequency, urgency, suprapubic pain, haematuria; **no fever or loin pain** |
| **Acute pyelonephritis** (upper UTI) | Infection of the renal pelvis and parenchyma: **fever, rigors, loin (flank) pain, renal angle tenderness**, nausea/vomiting, ± lower urinary symptoms |
| **Recurrent UTI in pregnancy** | Two or more UTIs in pregnancy (or one UTI plus ASB), or relapse after treatment |
| **Relapse vs reinfection** | Relapse = same organism within 2 weeks (treatment failure, possible structural cause); reinfection = new organism later |
| **Group B streptococcal (GBS) bacteriuria** | GBS in urine at **any colony count**: a marker of heavy genital colonisation; treat and give **intrapartum antibiotic prophylaxis** |
| **Urosepsis** | Life-threatening organ dysfunction due to a dysregulated response to a urinary infection |

> [!PEARL]
> The one thing never to forget: **fever plus loin pain in a pregnant woman is pyelonephritis until proven otherwise, and every case is admitted.** Pyelonephritis in pregnancy is a sepsis risk, not an outpatient illness.

## 2. Epidemiology

- **ASB**: about **2–10%** of pregnant women worldwide. A study at **Mulago Hospital** found ASB in about **13%** of antenatal mothers, with *E. coli* the commonest isolate and high resistance to commonly used oral antibiotics [VERIFY: Mulago study citation].
- Untreated ASB progresses to **pyelonephritis in 20–40%** of pregnant women (versus about 1–2% if treated). Treating ASB reduces pyelonephritis by about three-quarters and is associated with fewer low-birth-weight babies.
- **Pyelonephritis** complicates about **1–2% of pregnancies**, mostly in the **second and third trimesters**, and is **more often right-sided**.
- **Why it matters in Uganda**: pyelonephritis is one of the commonest reasons for antenatal admission, a common cause of **fever in pregnancy** (alongside malaria), an important trigger of **preterm labour**, and a cause of **maternal sepsis**, a leading direct cause of maternal death. Antibiotic resistance is high, and urine culture is not available at most lower-level facilities.
- **Resistance in Uganda**: many community *E. coli* isolates are resistant to **co-trimoxazole, ampicillin/amoxicillin and tetracyclines**; **extended-spectrum beta-lactamase (ESBL)** producers are increasingly reported, including resistance to ceftriaxone. Nitrofurantoin generally retains good activity against *E. coli*.
- **Facility realities**: dipstick urinalysis is available at most HC IIIs/IVs (if strips are in stock); microscopy at HC IV; **culture and sensitivity** mainly at regional and national referral hospitals and private labs, with results in 2–3 days. **G6PD deficiency** is relatively common in Uganda (relevant to nitrofurantoin).

> [!UGANDA]
> WHO ANC (2016) recommends **screening for ASB** in pregnancy. Where culture is not available, WHO suggests **midstream urine Gram staining** over dipstick testing, but in most Ugandan HC IIIs the practical screen is a **urine dipstick** (nitrites and leucocyte esterase) at booking and when symptomatic. Remember: **a negative dipstick does not exclude ASB** (nitrite sensitivity is only about 50%). Send urine for culture wherever you can, and always for pyelonephritis.

## 3. Aetiology and Risk Factors

**Organisms**: *Escherichia coli* (**70–80%**), *Klebsiella*, *Proteus*, *Enterobacter*, *Enterococcus*, **group B streptococcus**, *Staphylococcus saprophyticus*. Most are gut flora that colonise the perineum and ascend the short female urethra.

**Risk factors**

- Previous UTI or pyelonephritis.
- **Diabetes** (glycosuria).
- **Sickle cell disease and trait**.
- **HIV**.
- Urinary tract anomalies or **stones**.
- Low socio-economic status, poor hygiene/water access.
- Sexual activity, multiparity.
- **Catheterisation** (including after CS).

**Pregnancy-specific predisposition** ("PROGRESS"; mechanisms in section 4): progesterone, reflux, obstruction by the uterus, glycosuria, residual urine, *E. coli* from the nearby perineum, stasis, suppressed immunity.

## 4. Pathophysiology

### Why pregnancy predisposes to ascending infection

| Change | Mechanism | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| **Progesterone** | Relaxes smooth muscle of the ureters and bladder | **Ureteric dilatation**, reduced peristalsis, **urinary stasis**; larger, floppier bladder that empties incompletely |
| **Mechanical compression** by the enlarging uterus (from about 20 weeks) at the pelvic brim | Ureters compressed where they cross the iliac vessels | **Physiological hydroureter and hydronephrosis** (up to 90% of women by the third trimester) |
| **Right more than left** | The uterus is **dextrorotated**; the **sigmoid colon cushions the left ureter**; the right ovarian vein crosses the right ureter | **Right-sided pyelonephritis** is more common |
| **Vesicoureteric reflux** | Bladder oedema/hyperaemia and displaced trigone reduce the valve effect at the ureteric orifices | Bacteria reflux up the ureters |
| **Urine composition** | **Glycosuria** (lower renal threshold), **aminoaciduria**, more alkaline urine | Better culture medium for bacteria |
| **Increased GFR and urine volume** | Dilated system holds a larger volume of stagnant urine (up to 200 mL) | Stasis |
| **Immune modulation** | Relative suppression of cell-mediated immunity | Infection spreads more easily |
| **Short female urethra** near the vagina and anus | Colonisation of the periurethral area by gut flora | Entry of organisms (same as non-pregnant) |

### Why pyelonephritis is so dangerous in pregnancy

- **Endotoxin** from Gram-negative bacteria triggers a strong cytokine response. In pregnancy, **low plasma oncotic pressure** (dilutional hypoalbuminaemia) and high plasma volume make the lungs prone to **capillary leak**: **ARDS/pulmonary oedema**, made worse by **aggressive IV fluids** and **beta-agonist tocolytics**.
- **Endotoxin damages red cell membranes** (haemolysis), causing **anaemia** in about a quarter of women.
- **Transient renal dysfunction** (fall in creatinine clearance) is common.
- **Inflammation stimulates prostaglandins and uterine contractions**: preterm labour. **Fever** itself causes fetal tachycardia and increases fetal oxygen demand.
- **ASB itself** is linked to preterm birth and low birth weight, probably through the same inflammatory pathways.

**Feature links**

- Bladder mucosal inflammation → dysuria (internal), frequency, urgency, suprapubic pain, haematuria → cystitis.
- Gram-negative bacteria reduce nitrate to nitrite → dipstick nitrites (specific, about 50% sensitive).
- Neutrophils in urine → leucocyte esterase, pus cells above 10/HPF; white cell casts → renal parenchymal (upper tract) infection.
- Renal parenchymal infection and capsular stretch → loin pain, renal angle tenderness; systemic cytokines → fever, rigors, vomiting.
- Dextrorotation and the sigmoid cushion → right-sided pyelonephritis.
- Endotoxin → vasodilatation and capillary leak → tachycardia, hypotension, raised lactate → sepsis/septic shock.
- Low oncotic pressure + capillary leak + IV fluids/beta-agonists → breathlessness, crackles, falling SpO₂ → ARDS/pulmonary oedema.
- Endotoxin damage to red cells → haemolysis → falling Hb (plus dilution from fluids).
- Renal inflammation and hypoperfusion → rising creatinine, oliguria → AKI.
- Prostaglandin release → contractions → preterm labour; maternal fever → fetal tachycardia.
- Obstruction (stone) or abscess → persistent fever after 48–72 hours of correct antibiotics.

## 5. Classification / Staging

| Category | Features | Setting of care |
|---|---|---|
| **Asymptomatic bacteriuria** | Significant bacteriuria, no symptoms | Outpatient; 7 days, test of cure |
| **Acute cystitis** (lower UTI) | Dysuria, frequency, urgency, suprapubic pain; **afebrile** | Outpatient, HC III upwards; 5–7 days |
| **Acute pyelonephritis** (upper UTI) | Fever, rigors, loin pain, renal angle tenderness, vomiting | **Admit every case** (HC IV / hospital); 10–14 days in total |
| **Urosepsis / septic shock** | Pyelonephritis + sepsis triggers (or hypotension despite fluids) | Sepsis bundle within 1 hour; HDU/ICU |
| **Recurrent UTI** | Two or more UTIs, UTI + ASB, relapse; one pyelonephritis (many units) | Prophylaxis until delivery; look for a cause |

**Sepsis screen** (suspected infection plus **any one**): temperature **above 38 °C or below 36 °C**, HR **above 100**, RR **22 or more**, SBP **100 mmHg or below**, new confusion, SpO₂ **below 95%**, oliguria, lactate **above 2 mmol/L** (severe **4 or more**).

## 6. Clinical Features

On an antenatal ward the question is almost always one of three: **Is this bacteriuria at all? Is it lower (cystitis) or upper (pyelonephritis)? Is she septic?** Keep an eye on the fetus and on the other causes of fever in Uganda.

### History

| Question | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| Burning on passing urine? Is it **internal** (in the urethra) or **external** (as urine touches sore vulval skin)? | Internal dysuria suggests UTI; external suggests vulvovaginitis (candida, trichomonas, herpes) |
| Frequency, urgency, suprapubic pain, blood in urine, smelly urine? | Cystitis. **Frequency alone is normal in pregnancy**: ask whether it is new or different |
| **Fever, rigors, loin/back pain, vomiting?** | Pyelonephritis; vomiting means oral treatment will fail |
| **Gestational age** | Drug choice (no trimethoprim in the first trimester; no nitrofurantoin or sulfonamides near term), steroids if preterm labour threatens |
| Abdominal tightening, pain, vaginal bleeding, leaking liquor, fetal movements? | **Preterm labour**, abruption, PPROM/chorioamnionitis |
| Headache, myalgia, cough, breathlessness, offensive discharge, RIF pain? | Other fever sources: malaria, pneumonia, chorioamnionitis, appendicitis; **breathlessness during treatment suggests ARDS/fluid overload** |
| Vaginal discharge, itching, new partner? | STI (chlamydia/gonorrhoea cause dysuria with sterile pyuria), vaginitis |
| Previous UTIs this pregnancy: treated with what, and was a **test of cure** done? Previous pyelonephritis, kidney stones, known urinary tract abnormality? | Recurrence or relapse changes the plan (culture, prophylaxis, imaging) |
| Diabetes, **sickle cell disease/trait**, **HIV** (and co-trimoxazole use), TB, recent catheter | Risk factors; atypical organisms; drug choices |
| **Antibiotics already taken** (including pharmacy-bought ciprofloxacin) | Affects culture yield and resistance |
| **Drug allergies** (penicillin: type of reaction), **G6PD deficiency** | Choice of antibiotic (nitrofurantoin; cephalosporins after anaphylaxis) |
| Malaria test done? Travel, ITN use | **In Uganda every febrile pregnant woman needs an mRDT/blood smear** |

### Examination

Follow the order of Hutchison's Clinical Methods (24th edition), with the obstetric abdominal examination. A febrile, unwell woman gets her vital signs and sepsis screen first (section 9).

#### Introduction, consent and preparation

- Introduce yourself, confirm identity and gestation, explain and gain consent; chaperone for abdominal, speculum and vaginal examination.
- **Hand hygiene**.
- **Position and exposure**: semi-recumbent with **left lateral tilt**; sit her forward for the renal angles.

#### General inspection and vital signs

- **Vital signs are the priority**: temperature, pulse, **respiratory rate**, BP, SpO₂, level of consciousness, urine output, on a **modified obstetric early warning score (MEOWS)** chart. Tachycardia above 100, RR 22 or more, SBP 100 or below, or confusion suggest **sepsis**.
- **General**: toxic look, rigors, hydration, pallor (haemolysis), jaundice, rash, confusion.

#### Hands, face and neck

Warm flushed peripheries (early sepsis) versus cold mottled skin and slow capillary refill (shock); conjunctival pallor; scleral jaundice; dry mucous membranes.

#### Chest

Crackles (pneumonia; later, pulmonary oedema/ARDS). **Re-examine after every fluid bolus.**

#### Abdomen and obstetric examination

- **Inspection**: size for dates, scars.
- **Palpation**: suprapubic tenderness (cystitis); bladder (retention); right iliac fossa/flank tenderness (appendicitis: the appendix moves upwards and laterally in pregnancy); RUQ (cholecystitis, HELLP); **uterine tenderness** (chorioamnionitis, abruption, red degeneration of a fibroid); **contractions**.
- **Renal angle (costovertebral angle) tenderness**: sit her forward and percuss gently (pyelonephritis).
- **Fundal height, lie, presentation, engagement**.
- **Auscultation of the fetal heart**: **fetal tachycardia** with maternal fever.

#### Completing the examination

- **Speculum** if discharge, bleeding or leaking (PPROM, cervicitis).
- **Vaginal examination** only if contractions or suspected labour (and not if PPROM without labour): cervical change = preterm labour.
- **Bedside tests**: urine dipstick, mRDT, Hb (section 8).
- Thank her, hand hygiene, summarise.

**Document key positives and negatives**

| Area | Model documentation |
|---|---|
| General | "Febrile, flushed, dry mucosae, not pale, alert" |
| Vital signs | "T 39.2 °C, HR 118, RR 22, BP 104/66, SpO₂ 97% on air: sepsis screen positive" |
| Chest | "Chest clear" (re-examine after fluids) |
| Abdomen | "Right renal angle tenderness; no RIF tenderness; bladder not palpable" |
| Obstetric | "Uterus soft, non-tender, no contractions, FHR 170" |
| Speculum | "No liquor, no discharge" |

### Red flags

> [!REDFLAG]
> - **Signs of sepsis**: temperature above 38 °C or below 36 °C, HR above 100 (persistently above 110), RR 22 or more, SBP 100 mmHg or below, confusion, SpO₂ below 95%, oliguria (below 0.5 mL/kg/h), mottled skin, raised lactate. Start the **sepsis bundle within 1 hour**.
> - **Breathlessness, falling SpO₂ or crackles** during treatment (especially after IV fluids): **ARDS or pulmonary oedema** until proven otherwise; stop IV fluids, sit her up, oxygen, senior review.
> - **Persistent fever after 48–72 hours** of appropriate IV antibiotics: resistant organism, **obstruction (stone), renal or perinephric abscess**, or wrong diagnosis.
> - **Regular contractions or cervical change**: preterm labour.
> - **Reduced fetal movements, fetal tachycardia above 160 or bradycardia**.
> - **Rising creatinine / oliguria**: AKI.
> - **Vomiting**: cannot take oral treatment; needs IV therapy.

### Thinking like a clinician

- **In Uganda, fever in pregnancy is malaria AND something else until proven otherwise.** Always do an mRDT, but a positive mRDT does not exclude pyelonephritis (both can co-exist; the mRDT can stay positive for weeks after treated malaria).
- **Distinguish cystitis from pyelonephritis clinically**: **fever and loin pain = upper tract = admit**. Cystitis does not cause high fever.
- **Pyuria is not bacteriuria.** Leucocytes without nitrites and a negative culture (sterile pyuria) suggest **chlamydia/gonorrhoea, TB of the urinary tract, contamination with vaginal discharge, or recent antibiotics**.
- **Contaminated samples** are common: many squamous epithelial cells and mixed growth mean the MSU was not clean. Repeat it.
- **Right loin pain has a big differential** in pregnancy: pyelonephritis, appendicitis, renal colic, cholecystitis, HELLP, abruption, red degeneration of a fibroid, ovarian torsion. Examine the uterus and check urine, BP and bloods.
- **When the patient does not improve**, do not simply add more antibiotics: check the culture result, image the kidneys (ultrasound for obstruction/abscess), reconsider the diagnosis (malaria, chorioamnionitis, appendicitis, pneumonia).
- **Over-hydration is a real danger**: pregnant women with pyelonephritis are prone to pulmonary oedema. Give fluids in small boluses with reassessment and keep a fluid balance chart.
- **Common student mistakes**:
  1. Prescribing **ciprofloxacin** (commonly used for UTI in non-pregnant patients in Uganda) to a pregnant woman.
  2. Treating ASB with a **single dose** or 3 days by default (use 5–7 days; WHO recommends 7 days for ASB).
  3. Forgetting a **test of cure** urine culture 1–2 weeks after treatment.
  4. Sending a woman with pyelonephritis home on oral antibiotics.
  5. Using **nitrofurantoin for pyelonephritis** (it does not achieve adequate renal tissue or blood levels).
  6. Forgetting the **fetus** (fetal heart, contractions, steroids if preterm).

## 7. Differential Diagnoses

| Differential | Distinguishing clinical feature | Distinguishing investigation |
|---|---|---|
| **Acute cystitis** | Dysuria, frequency, urgency, suprapubic pain; afebrile | Dipstick (nitrites, LE), MSU culture |
| **Acute pyelonephritis** | Fever, rigors, loin pain, renal angle tenderness, vomiting | Urinalysis, culture, FBC, blood culture, creatinine |
| **Malaria** | Fever, rigors, headache, myalgia; no urinary symptoms (but can co-exist) | mRDT / blood smear |
| **Vulvovaginal candidiasis / trichomoniasis** | External dysuria, itch, discharge | Speculum, wet mount |
| **Urethritis / cervicitis (chlamydia, gonorrhoea)** | Dysuria, discharge, sterile pyuria | NAAT where available, syndromic management |
| **Preterm labour** | Regular painful contractions, cervical change; may be triggered by UTI | Speculum/VE, CTG, urine culture |
| **Chorioamnionitis** | Fever, **uterine tenderness**, offensive liquor, fetal tachycardia, often after PROM | Clinical, FBC, HVS |
| **Placental abruption** | Constant pain, tender hard uterus, bleeding, fetal distress | Clinical, CTG, ultrasound |
| **Appendicitis** | Right-sided pain (higher in pregnancy), anorexia, low-grade fever, guarding; urinary symptoms can occur if inflamed appendix lies near the ureter | Clinical, ultrasound |
| **Renal colic / urolithiasis** | Severe colicky loin-to-groin pain, haematuria, little fever unless infected obstruction | Renal ultrasound (distinguish from physiological hydronephrosis) |
| **Pre-eclampsia / HELLP** | RUQ/epigastric pain, hypertension, proteinuria | BP, urine protein, FBC (platelets), LFTs |
| **Red degeneration of fibroid** | Localised tender mass on the uterus, mid-pregnancy, low-grade fever | Ultrasound |
| **Pneumonia (lower lobe)** | Cough, pleuritic pain, crackles; pain can refer to the loin | CXR (shielded) |
| **Urinary TB** | Chronic symptoms, sterile pyuria, HIV-positive, weight loss | Early-morning urine for GeneXpert/AFB |

## 8. Clinical Workup

Availability reflects the source notes [VERIFY: compiled from the source notes, not an MoH list].

### Bedside

| Test | How to read it | Available at |
|---|---|---|
| **Urine dipstick** (fresh sample) | **Nitrites positive**: Gram-negative bacteria (specific; only about 50% sensitive). **Leucocyte esterase**: pyuria. Both positive with symptoms = treat as UTI. LE alone = possible contamination, STI or vaginitis. **Protein**: UTI or pre-eclampsia (check BP). **Glucose**: lower renal threshold or diabetes. **Ketones**: vomiting/starvation. **Blood**: infection, stone, contamination | Most HC IIIs and IVs (if strips in stock) |
| **Look at the urine** | Cloudy, offensive urine supports infection; clear urine makes it less likely (not excluded) | All levels |
| **mRDT** (± blood smear) | Every febrile woman. A positive HRP2 mRDT can stay positive for weeks after treated malaria and does **not** exclude pyelonephritis | All levels |
| **RBS** | Undiagnosed diabetes (recurrent UTI) or stress hyperglycaemia | HC III upwards |
| **HemoCue Hb** | Haemolytic anaemia from endotoxin | HC III upwards |
| **HIV rapid test** (if status unknown) | Risk factor; changes prophylaxis (co-trimoxazole) | All levels |
| **Pulse oximetry** | Baseline and after every fluid bolus (ARDS watch) | HC IV upwards |
| **Hourly urine output** (catheter if septic) | Aim **0.5 mL/kg/h or more** | All inpatient levels |
| **Fetal heart / CTG** (viable gestation) | Tachycardia settles as maternal fever falls; persistent abnormality needs review | All levels (CTG at hospitals) |
| **Point-of-care ultrasound** | Fetal viability, liquor, placenta; **gross hydronephrosis** if trained (formal renal scan still needed) | HC IV and hospitals |

**How to collect a clean-catch MSU (teach the patient)**. The sample itself is where most mistakes happen.

1. Wash hands; separate the labia with one hand.
2. Clean the vulva **front to back** with water (not antiseptic, which can kill bacteria in the sample).
3. Pass the **first part of the stream into the toilet**, then catch the **middle part** in a sterile container without touching the inside.
4. Deliver to the lab **within 2 hours** (or refrigerate at 4 °C for up to 24 hours).
5. Label the form with **gestation, symptoms and any antibiotics already taken**, and whether it is a catheter specimen (lower counts are significant).

### Basic lab

| Test | When | Expected / abnormal finding | How it changes management (and where) |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Urine microscopy** | Symptoms, positive dipstick | Pus cells **more than 10 per high-power field**, bacteria; **white cell casts = pyelonephritis**; many squamous cells = contaminated sample | Supports diagnosis; repeat a contaminated sample. HC IV and above |
| **Gram stain of MSU** | Where culture is unavailable (WHO-suggested ASB screen) | Bacteria on Gram stain | Treat ASB empirically, then test of cure if possible |
| **FBC** | Pyelonephritis, sepsis | WBC raised; **anaemia** (haemolysis, about a quarter); **low platelets** (sepsis/DIC) | Severity; transfusion; DIC workup. HC IV upwards |
| **Creatinine, urea, electrolytes** | Pyelonephritis, sepsis, before gentamicin | Creatinine above about 77–80 µmol/L is suspicious in pregnancy (normal 35–70) | AKI: adjust or avoid gentamicin; fluid balance. Hospitals |
| **LFTs** | RUQ pain, jaundice, hypertension | Raised ALT, bilirubin | HELLP, cholecystitis, hepatitis. Hospitals |
| **Lactate** (where available) | Sepsis screen positive | **Above 2 mmol/L** = hypoperfusion; **4 or more** = severe | Fluid boluses, HDU/ICU; lactate above 2 alone meets the sepsis trigger |

### Imaging

| Test | When | Expected finding | How it changes management (and where) |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Renal ultrasound** | **No response in 48–72 h**, recurrent pyelonephritis, haematuria or colic, suspected stone | **Hydronephrosis out of proportion** to gestation, stone, renal/perinephric abscess | Urology: ureteric stent or nephrostomy; drainage. Hospitals |
| **Obstetric ultrasound** | Pyelonephritis, contractions, uncertain dates | GA, liquor, placenta, fetal wellbeing | Steroids, timing, differential (abruption, fibroid degeneration). HC IV and hospitals |
| **Chest X-ray** (shielded) | Breathless, SpO₂ below 95%, crackles | Bilateral infiltrates (ARDS/oedema), lobar consolidation | Stop fluids, oxygen, diuretic if oedema, ICU. Hospitals |
| **Renal imaging postpartum** | Recurrent pyelonephritis or suspected structural disease | Anomaly, scarring, stones | After **6–12 weeks postpartum**, when physiological dilatation has resolved |

| Test | When | Expected / abnormal finding | How it changes management (and where) |
|---|---|---|---|

Regional/national referral and private labs; results in 2–3 days |
| **Test-of-cure culture** | **1–2 weeks** after finishing treatment | Persistent bacteriuria in about 1 in 5 | Re-treat by sensitivity; consider prophylaxis |

### Putting it together

**Model one-line summary**

> "A 24-year-old G2P1 at 26 weeks with 2 days of fever, rigors, right loin pain and vomiting, febrile 39.2 °C, tachycardic 118, RR 22, BP 104/66, with right renal angle tenderness, nitrite-positive urine, negative mRDT and fetal tachycardia."

**Problem list**

1. **Acute right pyelonephritis with sepsis** (sepsis screen positive).
2. Vomiting and dehydration: needs IV therapy, but at risk of fluid overload/ARDS.
3. **Fetal tachycardia** and risk of **preterm labour** at 26 weeks.
4. Previous untreated cystitis at 14 weeks without test of cure: recurrence risk, needs prophylaxis.

**Working diagnosis**: acute pyelonephritis in pregnancy complicated by sepsis, probably *E. coli*.

**Model case presentation (ward round)**

> "Mrs B is a 24-year-old G2P1 at **26 weeks**, admitted last night with **2 days of fever, rigors, right loin pain and vomiting**, with a history of cystitis at 14 weeks that was not followed up. On admission: T 39.2 °C, HR 118, RR 22, BP 104/66, SpO₂ 97%, **right renal angle tenderness**, uterus soft and non-tender, no contractions, FHR 170. Dipstick: nitrites positive, LE 3+. **mRDT negative.** Hb 10.1 g/dL, WBC 17, creatinine 88 µmol/L. Impression: **acute right pyelonephritis with sepsis** (MEOWS triggers). She received **ceftriaxone 2 g IV** within an hour after blood and urine cultures, 1 L Ringer's lactate in boluses, and paracetamol. This morning: T 37.8 °C, HR 96, RR 18, urine output adequate, FHR 150, no contractions. **Plan**: continue ceftriaxone until afebrile 24–48 hours, then **oral cefixime to complete 14 days** guided by culture; fluid balance; watch SpO₂ for ARDS; CTG/contractions; **test of cure** in 1–2 weeks, then **nightly nitrofurantoin prophylaxis** and monthly urine cultures."

**Worked example 1: reading urine results**

**Worked example 2: the sepsis screen in pyelonephritis** (admission observations for Mrs B)

| Parameter | Value | Trigger? |
|---|---|---|
| Temperature | 39.2 °C | Yes (above 38) |
| Heart rate | 118/min | Yes (above 100) |
| Respiratory rate | 22/min | Yes (22 or more) |
| Systolic BP | 104 mmHg | No (but close to 100) |
| SpO₂ | 97% | No |
| Consciousness | Alert | No |
| Lactate | 2.6 mmol/L | Yes (above 2) |

**Interpretation**: sepsis from a urinary source (several triggers plus raised lactate), not yet septic shock.

**Action within 1 hour**: senior informed; oxygen only if SpO₂ falls below 94%; **blood and urine cultures**, then **ceftriaxone 2 g IV** (avoid gentamicin for now given the creatinine); **500 mL Ringer's lactate** boluses with chest and SpO₂ checks after each; catheter and hourly urine output; paracetamol 1 g; repeat lactate. FBC and creatinine daily. CTG: expect the fetal tachycardia to settle as the fever falls. **Review at 48–72 hours**: if still febrile, check culture (ESBL?) and arrange a **renal ultrasound**.

## 9. Management

### Immediate / emergency

**First 5 minutes (febrile pregnant woman)**

1. **Full set of vital signs on a MEOWS chart**: temperature, pulse, **RR**, BP, SpO₂, level of consciousness, and the time she last passed urine.
2. **Sepsis screen** (section 5). If positive: **call a senior** and start the **sepsis bundle within 1 hour**.
3. **IV access and bloods at the same time**: FBC, **blood cultures**, creatinine, **mRDT**, glucose, lactate where available, group and save if anaemic.
4. **Urine sample before the first antibiotic dose**: clean-catch MSU (or catheter specimen if she is too ill), for dipstick and **culture**.
5. **Start treatment without waiting for results**: **ceftriaxone 1 g IV daily (2 g if septic)**, paracetamol 1 g, and **500 mL crystalloid boluses** only if hypotensive, reassessing the chest and SpO₂ after each.
6. **Fetal heart and uterus**: FHR (tachycardia above 160 is common with maternal fever), contractions, uterine tenderness.

> [!REDFLAG]
> **Breathless, SpO₂ falling or crackles after IV fluids** = pulmonary oedema/ARDS until proven otherwise. Stop the fluids, sit her up, give oxygen and call for help.

**Urosepsis and septic shock (algorithm)**

1. **Recognise**: suspected infection + abnormal MEOWS (e.g. SBP 100 or below, RR 22 or more, altered mentation) or lactate above 2.
2. **Call for help** (senior obstetrician, anaesthetist/ICU).
3. **Within the first hour ("Sepsis Six")**:
   1. **Oxygen** to keep SpO₂ 94–98%.
   2. **Blood cultures** (and urine culture).
   3. **IV broad-spectrum antibiotics**: **ceftriaxone 2 g IV** (± gentamicin 5 mg/kg; meropenem if known ESBL or hospital-acquired).
   4. **IV fluids**: 500 mL crystalloid boluses, reassessing after each (up to about 30 mL/kg).
   5. **Lactate and Hb**.
   6. **Urine output**: catheterise, hourly measurement.
4. **Left lateral tilt** (relieve aortocaval compression) if more than 20 weeks.
5. **Persistent hypotension after fluids**: vasopressors (noradrenaline) in HDU/ICU.
6. **Source control**: relieve obstruction (stent/nephrostomy); drain abscess.
7. **Fetus**: CTG once the mother is stable; the fetal heart usually recovers with maternal resuscitation. **Deliver only for obstetric indications** or if intrauterine infection is the source.
8. **VTE prophylaxis**, glucose control, reassess every 30–60 minutes.

### Definitive

#### 1. Screening and treating asymptomatic bacteriuria

1. **Screen at booking** (ideally 12–16 weeks): urine culture where available; otherwise Gram stain or dipstick (and culture if dipstick positive).
2. **If significant bacteriuria**: treat according to sensitivities for **5–7 days** (WHO: a **7-day** regimen for ASB).
3. **Test of cure**: repeat culture **1–2 weeks after** completing treatment, then **each trimester** (or monthly in high-risk women).
4. **Two episodes** (or one episode that relapses): consider **suppressive prophylaxis** for the rest of pregnancy.
5. **GBS in urine (any count)**: treat now **and** give **intrapartum IV antibiotic prophylaxis** in labour (benzylpenicillin 3 g IV loading, then 1.8 g IV 4-hourly until delivery; clindamycin 900 mg IV 8-hourly if allergic).

#### 2. Acute cystitis (outpatient, HC III upwards)

1. Confirm: symptoms + dipstick (nitrites/LE); **send MSU for culture before the first dose** where possible.
2. Start **empirical oral antibiotic** (drug table), adjust to sensitivities when available.
3. **Advice**: drink plenty of fluids (2–3 L/day), void regularly and after intercourse, wipe front to back, paracetamol for discomfort.
4. **Return immediately** if fever, loin pain, vomiting, contractions or bleeding.
5. **Test of cure** 1–2 weeks after treatment.

#### 3. Acute pyelonephritis: admit every case (HC IV and hospital)

1. **Assess ABCDE and screen for sepsis** (MEOWS; temperature, HR, RR, BP, SpO₂, mental state, urine output).
2. **IV access**; bloods: **FBC, creatinine and electrolytes, blood cultures, mRDT/smear, glucose, lactate** (where available), group and save if anaemic.
3. **Urine for culture before antibiotics** (MSU or catheter specimen).
4. **Start IV antibiotics immediately** (within 1 hour if septic): **ceftriaxone** first line; ampicillin + gentamicin where ceftriaxone is unavailable; meropenem for ESBL or ceftriaxone failure (doses in the drug table). Continue IV until **afebrile for 24–48 hours**, then oral step-down to complete **10–14 days**.
5. **Fluids**: IV crystalloid (Ringer's lactate or 0.9% saline). If hypotensive: **500 mL bolus**, reassess, repeat to a total of about 30 mL/kg while watching for crackles and falling SpO₂. Otherwise maintenance fluids while vomiting. **Strict fluid balance chart**; aim for urine output **0.5 mL/kg/hour or more**. Stop fluids if she becomes breathless.
6. **Antipyretic and analgesia**: **paracetamol 1 g orally/IV 6-hourly** (max 4 g/day); fever increases fetal oxygen demand and uterine irritability. Avoid NSAIDs after 28 weeks.
7. **Antiemetic** if vomiting (e.g. metoclopramide 10 mg IV/IM 8-hourly).
8. **Treat co-existing malaria** if the test is positive (IV artesunate for severe malaria).
9. **Fetal assessment**: fetal heart rate (tachycardia usually settles with maternal fever), CTG if viable gestation; watch for contractions.
10. **Preterm labour**: give **corticosteroids** if 24–34 weeks and delivery is likely (dexamethasone 6 mg IM 12-hourly × 4). Contractions often settle once fever is controlled. **Avoid beta-agonist tocolytics** (salbutamol) because they increase the risk of **pulmonary oedema/ARDS**; if tocolysis is appropriate, use **nifedipine** and only when infection is being controlled.
11. **Thromboprophylaxis** (LMWH) if immobile, septic or with other risk factors.
12. **Monitor**: vital signs 1–4-hourly (MEOWS), fluid balance, SpO₂, daily FBC and creatinine in severe cases.
13. **Review at 48–72 hours**:
    - **Improving**: switch to oral when afebrile 24–48 hours and eating; discharge with the oral course.
    - **Not improving**: check culture/sensitivity, change antibiotics, **renal ultrasound** (obstruction, stone, abscess), reconsider diagnosis, involve urology (ureteric stent or percutaneous nephrostomy for infected obstruction).
14. **After treatment**: **test of cure** 1–2 weeks after, then **suppressive prophylaxis** for the rest of pregnancy (recurrence about 6–8%, higher without prophylaxis) and **monthly or each-trimester urine culture**.

#### 4. Recurrent UTI and prophylaxis

**Indications**: two or more UTIs in pregnancy, one UTI plus ASB, one episode of pyelonephritis (many units), or persistent bacteriuria after two courses.

- **Prophylaxis**: nitrofurantoin 50–100 mg nightly (or after intercourse) until delivery (switch to cephalexin from about 36 weeks), or cephalexin 250–500 mg nightly until delivery or up to 6 weeks postpartum.
- **Look for a cause**: diabetes (glucose), **sickle cell disease/trait**, HIV, **renal ultrasound** for stones/anomalies (and full imaging postpartum if recurrent), incomplete emptying.
- **Monthly urine culture** for the rest of the pregnancy.
- Behavioural advice: fluids, regular and post-coital voiding, front-to-back wiping.

#### 5. Postpartum

- **Postpartum UTI** is common after catheterisation and CS; treat as above (nitrofurantoin is compatible with breastfeeding except for G6PD-deficient or jaundiced/preterm infants).
- Women with **recurrent pyelonephritis** or suspected structural disease need **renal imaging after 6–12 weeks postpartum** (when physiological dilatation has resolved).

> [!DRUG]
> **Antibiotics to avoid in pregnancy**
> - **Fluoroquinolones** (ciprofloxacin, levofloxacin): cartilage/joint damage in animal studies; avoid (use only if no alternative, on specialist advice).
> - **Tetracyclines** (doxycycline): **tooth discolouration and impaired fetal bone growth**; maternal hepatotoxicity.
> - **Trimethoprim**: a **folate antagonist**; avoid in the **first trimester** (neural tube and other defects).
> - **Sulfonamides / co-trimoxazole** (as UTI treatment): avoid in the **first trimester** and **near term** (displaces bilirubin from albumin: **neonatal jaundice/kernicterus**; haemolysis in G6PD deficiency). HIV-positive women on **co-trimoxazole prophylaxis** continue it (the benefit outweighs the risk), but it should not be relied on as UTI treatment.
> - **Aminoglycosides** (gentamicin): **fetal ototoxicity/nephrotoxicity** risk; use only short courses when clearly needed (e.g. severe sepsis, resistant organisms), with monitoring.
> - **Nitrofurantoin near term**: neonatal haemolysis (especially in G6PD deficiency).

### Monitoring and follow-up

- **Pyelonephritis**: vital signs 1–4-hourly (MEOWS); **SpO₂ and chest after every bolus**; strict fluid balance, urine output 0.5 mL/kg/h or more; daily FBC and creatinine in severe cases; fetal heart/CTG and contractions; **review at 48–72 hours**.
- **Test of cure**: urine culture **1–2 weeks** after every treated episode.
- **Ongoing**: urine culture each trimester (monthly if high-risk or after pyelonephritis); prophylaxis until delivery where indicated.
- **Postpartum**: renal imaging at 6–12 weeks if recurrent pyelonephritis or suspected structural disease.

## 11. Prognosis

- **Treated ASB** progresses to pyelonephritis in only about 1–2% (versus 20–40% untreated); treatment reduces pyelonephritis by about three-quarters and is associated with fewer low-birth-weight babies.
- **Pyelonephritis** usually responds within 48–72 hours of appropriate IV antibiotics; failure to respond suggests resistance, obstruction, abscess or a wrong diagnosis.
- **Recurrence** after pyelonephritis is about **6–8%**, higher without prophylaxis.
- Renal dysfunction is usually transient; chronic scarring is rare.

## 12. Prevention

- **Screen for ASB at booking** (ideally 12–16 weeks) and treat; **test of cure**; repeat culture each trimester (monthly if high-risk).
- **Treat cystitis promptly** with a pregnancy-safe antibiotic and do a test of cure.
- **Prophylaxis** after recurrence or pyelonephritis until delivery.
- **Behavioural advice**: fluids (2–3 L/day), regular and post-coital voiding, front-to-back wiping.
- **Avoid unnecessary catheterisation**; remove catheters early.
- **Antimicrobial stewardship**: culture before antibiotics, avoid pharmacy-bought ciprofloxacin, use narrow-spectrum drugs by sensitivity.
- **GBS bacteriuria**: intrapartum prophylaxis prevents neonatal GBS sepsis.

## 13. Special Populations

- **First trimester**: no trimethoprim; avoid sulfonamides.
- **Near term (from about 36–38 weeks) and in labour**: no nitrofurantoin; no sulfonamides; use cephalexin.
- **G6PD deficiency** (relatively common in Uganda): avoid nitrofurantoin (maternal and neonatal haemolysis).
- **HIV**: on co-trimoxazole prophylaxis, continue it but do not rely on it as UTI treatment; sterile pyuria → think urinary TB.
- **Sickle cell disease and trait**: higher UTI risk; UTI is a crisis trigger in SCD; urine culture each trimester.
- **Diabetes**: glycosuria; recurrent UTI; check glucose.
- **Penicillin allergy**: nitrofurantoin; cephalexin only if the allergy was mild and non-anaphylactic.
- **Renal impairment**: avoid nitrofurantoin if eGFR below 45; avoid or adjust gentamicin.
- **Breastfeeding**: nitrofurantoin is compatible except for G6PD-deficient or jaundiced/preterm infants.

## 14. Key Points

### High-yield points

- Pregnancy makes bacteriuria dangerous, not commoner: screen and treat ASB.
- A negative dipstick does not exclude ASB: culture.
- Fever plus loin pain = pyelonephritis = admit.
- Culture before the first dose; test of cure 1–2 weeks after.
- Nitrofurantoin for the bladder, not the kidney, and not at term.
- No ciprofloxacin, tetracyclines, first-trimester trimethoprim or term sulfonamides.
- Ceftriaxone IV for pyelonephritis, then oral to complete 10–14 days.
- Sepsis screen and bundle within 1 hour; mRDT for every fever.
- 500 mL boluses with reassessment: ARDS is the hidden killer.
- Prophylaxis after recurrence or pyelonephritis.

### Exam traps

- **"Ciprofloxacin for UTI."** Wrong in pregnancy: **avoid fluoroquinolones**. Use nitrofurantoin, cephalexin, amoxicillin-clavulanate or ceftriaxone.
- **"Negative dipstick, so no ASB."** Wrong: nitrites are only about 50% sensitive; **culture** is the screen.
- **"Nitrofurantoin for pyelonephritis."** Wrong: poor renal tissue and blood levels. **IV ceftriaxone**.
- **"Nitrofurantoin at 39 weeks."** Wrong: neonatal haemolysis; use cephalexin near term.
- **"Treat pyelonephritis as an outpatient with oral antibiotics."** Wrong: **admit every case**.
- **"The mRDT is positive, so the fever is malaria."** Not necessarily: malaria and pyelonephritis co-exist, and HRP2 tests stay positive after treatment. Examine the loins and dip the urine.
- **"Give 3 L of fluid quickly because she is febrile."** Wrong: pregnant women with pyelonephritis are prone to **ARDS/pulmonary oedema**. **500 mL boluses** with reassessment.
- **"Salbutamol for the contractions."** Wrong: beta-agonists worsen pulmonary oedema. Treat the infection; use **nifedipine** if tocolysis is appropriate.
- **"Single-dose or 3-day treatment for ASB."** Wrong: 5–7 days (WHO 7 days).

### Questions seniors ask

**On the ward round**

1. **"How do you know this is pyelonephritis and not cystitis?"** Fever, rigors, loin pain and renal angle tenderness (upper tract). Cystitis has no fever or loin pain.
2. **"Is she septic?"** State the triggers: temperature, HR, RR, SBP, SpO₂, consciousness, urine output, lactate. One or more with infection = start the sepsis bundle within 1 hour.
3. **"Did you send a urine culture before the first dose?"** It should be sent before antibiotics; it is the only way to catch ESBL and to do a meaningful test of cure.
4. **"What antibiotic, what dose and for how long?"** Ceftriaxone 1 g IV daily (2 g if septic) until afebrile 24–48 hours, then oral cefixime, amoxicillin-clavulanate or cephalexin by sensitivity to complete 10–14 days.
5. **"She is still febrile at 72 hours. What now?"** Check culture (ESBL: meropenem), renal ultrasound for obstruction or abscess, rethink the diagnosis (malaria, chorioamnionitis, appendicitis, pneumonia), urology if obstructed.
6. **"Why is her Hb falling?"** Endotoxin-mediated **haemolysis** (about a quarter of women), plus dilution from IV fluids; also consider malaria.
7. **"What happens after discharge?"** Complete the oral course, **test of cure** in 1–2 weeks, **nightly prophylaxis** until delivery, monthly (or each-trimester) urine culture, look for a cause (diabetes, sickle cell, HIV, stones).
8. **"What does sterile pyuria make you think of?"** Chlamydia/gonorrhoea, recent antibiotics, contamination by vaginal discharge, and **urinary TB** (especially with HIV).

**Viva questions**

1. **Why is pregnancy a risk factor for pyelonephritis?** Progesterone-induced ureteric dilatation and hypoperistalsis, mechanical compression at the pelvic brim (right more than left), vesicoureteric reflux, glycosuria/aminoaciduria, larger bladder with residual urine.
2. **Why is the right side affected more?** Dextrorotation of the uterus, the sigmoid colon cushions the left ureter, and the right ovarian vein crosses the right ureter.
3. **Define ASB and explain why we treat it in pregnancy.** 10⁵ CFU/mL or more of a single organism without symptoms; 20–40% progress to pyelonephritis if untreated; treatment reduces pyelonephritis and low birth weight.
4. **Name antibiotics safe in pregnancy and those to avoid.** Safe: penicillins, cephalosporins, nitrofurantoin (not near term), fosfomycin. Avoid: fluoroquinolones, tetracyclines, trimethoprim (first trimester), sulfonamides (first trimester/term), prolonged aminoglycosides.
5. **Why not nitrofurantoin for pyelonephritis?** It concentrates in urine but achieves poor renal tissue and blood levels.
6. **Why avoid nitrofurantoin at term?** Risk of **haemolytic anaemia in the neonate** (immature glutathione system, G6PD deficiency).
7. **What are the complications of pyelonephritis in pregnancy?** Sepsis, septic shock, ARDS, anaemia, AKI, preterm labour, recurrence.
8. **What do you do if fever persists after 72 hours of IV ceftriaxone?** Review culture/sensitivity (ESBL?), renal ultrasound for obstruction/abscess, reconsider diagnosis (malaria, chorioamnionitis, appendicitis), change antibiotics, urology.
9. **What is a test of cure and when?** Repeat urine culture 1–2 weeks after treatment.
10. **What should be screened for in recurrent UTI?** Diabetes, sickle cell, HIV, stones, anomalies.

### Mnemonics

**Why pregnancy predisposes: "PROGRESS"**

- **P**rogesterone relaxes smooth muscle
- **R**eflux (vesicoureteric)
- **O**bstruction by the gravid uterus (right more than left)
- **G**lycosuria and aminoaciduria
- **R**esidual urine in a larger bladder
- **E**. coli from the nearby perineum
- **S**tasis in dilated ureters
- **S**uppressed immunity (relative)

**Antibiotics to avoid: "Stop The Fetal Toxic Antibiotics"**

- **S**ulfonamides (first trimester and term)
- **T**rimethoprim (first trimester)
- **F**luoroquinolones
- **T**etracyclines
- **A**minoglycosides (prolonged use)

**Safe choices: "Nice Pregnant Cells Face Care"**: **N**itrofurantoin (not at term), **P**enicillins (amoxicillin, co-amoxiclav), **C**ephalosporins (cephalexin, cefixime, ceftriaxone), **F**osfomycin, **C**linical culture-guided therapy.

**Pyelonephritis management: "ADMIT"**: **A**ll cases admitted; **D**raw cultures (blood, urine) first; **M**edicate IV ceftriaxone; **I**ntake/output (careful fluids, watch for ARDS); **T**est of cure and prophylaxis afterwards.

### OSCE tips

- **Urinalysis station**: state the dipstick results systematically (leucocytes, nitrites, protein, blood, glucose, ketones, specific gravity, pH) and interpret each in pregnancy (e.g. protein = UTI or pre-eclampsia; glucose = physiological or diabetes; ketones = vomiting/starvation).
- **Prescribing station**: always state gestation-appropriate drug, dose, route, frequency, duration, test of cure, and safety-net advice.
- **MSU instruction station**: teach the 5 steps of the clean-catch MSU (section 8) and ask her to repeat them back.
