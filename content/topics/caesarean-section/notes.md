## 1. Purpose and Indications

A **caesarean section (CS)** is the delivery of a baby (after fetal viability) through an incision in the abdominal wall (**laparotomy**) and the uterus (**hysterotomy**). In Uganda it is the operation that ends **obstructed labour**, rescues babies in **fetal distress**, and delivers women with **placenta praevia, cord prolapse and ruptured uterus**. It is one of the two **CEmONC signal functions** that distinguish an HC IV or hospital from an HC III. The other is blood transfusion.

But a CS is **major abdominal surgery**. It carries risks of haemorrhage, sepsis, VTE and bladder injury, and anaesthetic death. It also leaves a **scar**, and the scar raises the risk of **uterine rupture and placenta accreta** in every future pregnancy. That matters enormously in a country where women often want 5 or more children and may labour far from a theatre.

**The one thing never to forget:** a CS decision has two parts: *is it indicated?* and *how urgent is it?* Then everything, including consent, antibiotics before incision, the fetal heart check in theatre, a safe technique and good aftercare, follows a checklist. And **every woman leaves with a plan for her next birth.**

**Definitions**

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Elective (planned) CS** | Scheduled before labour, ideally at **≥39 weeks** to reduce neonatal respiratory problems (transient tachypnoea) |
| **Emergency CS** | Decided on because of a new problem in labour or pregnancy. The urgency is graded 1–3 |
| **Lower-segment CS (LSCS)** | Transverse incision in the thin, less vascular lower uterine segment. It is the standard operation |
| **Classical CS** | Vertical incision in the **upper (contractile) segment** |
| **De Lee / low vertical** | Vertical incision confined to the lower segment. It is rare |
| **Scar dehiscence** | Separation of the old scar with the **serosa (visceral peritoneum) intact**. It is often silent and found at repeat CS as a "window" |
| **Uterine rupture** | **Full-thickness** tear with communication into the peritoneal cavity. The baby or placenta may be extruded |
| **TOLAC / "trial of scar"** | Trial of labour after caesarean. "Trial of scar" is the common Ugandan ward term |
| **VBAC** | Vaginal birth after caesarean: a *successful* TOLAC |
| **ERCS** | Elective repeat caesarean section |

**Indications: absolute vs relative**

| Absolute (vaginal birth impossible or clearly dangerous) | Relative (CS is safer in *this* situation) |
|---|---|
| Major placenta praevia (placenta covering or near the os) | Fetal distress in the first stage (non-reassuring FHR) |
| True CPD / contracted pelvis; **obstructed labour with a live fetus** | Breech (especially primigravida, footling, EFW >3.5–3.8 kg or <2 kg), unless the criteria for vaginal breech are met |
| Transverse or oblique lie in labour | Previous CS ×1 with an unfavourable picture; failed TOLAC |
| **Previous classical, T- or J-incision**, or previous uterine rupture | **Two or more previous CS** (Ugandan standard practice) |
| Cord prolapse with a live fetus, not fully dilated | Failed induction; arrest of dilatation or descent despite adequate contractions |
| Pelvic mass obstructing the birth canal (lower-segment fibroid, ovarian tumour) | Severe PE or eclampsia with an unfavourable cervix and delivery needed urgently |
| Invasive cervical cancer | APH (abruption with a live fetus; minor praevia bleeding) |
| Previous successful **VVF/RVF repair** or major pelvic floor reconstruction | Twins with first twin non-cephalic; monoamniotic twins |
| Brow presentation, face mento-posterior at term in labour | Macrosomia (EFW >4.5 kg in diabetes); maternal medical disease (e.g. selected cardiac); HIV with high viral load near term (individualise) |

**Urgency classification (NICE NG192; Lucas)**

| Category | Definition | Examples | Decision-to-delivery target |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1** | Immediate threat to the life of the woman or fetus | Cord prolapse with bradycardia, suspected uterine rupture, major abruption with a live fetus, prolonged fetal bradycardia | **As soon as possible, within 30 min** |
| **2** | Maternal or fetal compromise that is **not** immediately life-threatening | Obstructed labour, non-reassuring FHR that is not pre-terminal, APH that is controlled | **Within 75 min** |
| **3** | No compromise but needs **early** delivery | Failed induction with a well baby, woman booked for elective CS who arrives in early labour | Within hours |
| **4** | Timed to suit the woman and the team | Elective CS for 2 previous scars or breech | Scheduled |

> [!EXAM]
> The urgency category describes **the patient's clinical state**, not how busy the theatre is. When you call theatre, say the category out loud ("Category 1 CS for cord prolapse with bradycardia"). Everyone then knows the clock has started.

**Robson Ten-Group Classification (WHO 2015/2017).** It uses 6 variables: **parity, previous CS, onset of labour, gestational age, number of fetuses, and presentation/lie.**

| Group | Women included |
|---|---|
| **1** | Nulliparous, single cephalic, ≥37 wk, **spontaneous labour** |
| **2** | Nulliparous, single cephalic, ≥37 wk, **induced** (2a) or **CS before labour** (2b) |
| **3** | Multiparous **without** previous CS, single cephalic, ≥37 wk, spontaneous labour |
| **4** | Multiparous without previous CS, single cephalic, ≥37 wk, induced (4a) or CS before labour (4b) |
| **5** | **All multiparous with ≥1 previous CS**, single cephalic, ≥37 wk |
| **6** | All **nulliparous breeches** |
| **7** | All **multiparous breeches** (including previous CS) |
| **8** | All **multiple pregnancies** (including previous CS) |
| **9** | All **transverse or oblique lies** (including previous CS) |
| **10** | All single cephalic **<37 weeks** (including previous CS) |

> [!PEARL]
> Why Robson? A crude CS rate ("our rate is 30%") tells you nothing about *why*. Robson groups let a hospital see which groups drive its rate. Usually **Group 5 (previous CS)** is the biggest, followed by Groups 1 and 2. That identifies where to intervene: safer TOLAC, better labour management in first-time mothers, fewer unnecessary inductions. The groups are **mutually exclusive and totally inclusive**: every woman fits exactly one.

**Burden and Uganda context**

- The WHO has long said that population CS rates **above about 10–15%** are not associated with further falls in maternal or newborn mortality. Rates **below about 5%** suggest women who need CS are not getting it.
- Uganda's national CS rate is roughly **7%** (UDHS 2022). It is rising, but it is **very unequal**: urban, wealthier and educated women have much higher rates, private hospitals in Kampala exceed 30%, and many rural districts remain below 5%. Referral hospitals such as Mulago/Kawempe, Mengo and Mbarara have rates of **30–40%**, because they receive the complicated referrals.
- HC IVs are meant to provide CEmONC (CS and transfusion). Many are limited by absent anaesthetic staff, no blood, power or water problems, or no theatre running at night.
- Leading indications in Ugandan hospitals: **previous CS, obstructed labour/CPD, fetal distress, malpresentation, APH, and hypertensive disorders.**
- **Maternal mortality after CS** in sub-Saharan Africa is many times higher than in high-income countries (about 50 times, in a 2019 Lancet Global Health meta-analysis). Most deaths come from **haemorrhage, sepsis and anaesthetic complications**, often because the operation was done late on an exhausted, infected, dehydrated woman after a long referral.
- **Uterine rupture** causes a significant share of maternal deaths in Uganda. Previous CS, obstructed labour, grand multiparity, unsupervised use of oxytocin or local herbs (*"emumbwa"*), and living far from a facility are recurring risk factors in Mulago and Mbarara studies.
- **Surgical site infection** after CS in Uganda is common (10–15% or more in several studies). Isolates at Mulago are often **ceftriaxone-resistant *Klebsiella*, *E. coli* and MRSA**. This is a strong argument for correct prophylaxis timing and asepsis rather than prolonged post-op antibiotics.

> [!UGANDA]
> Uganda's reality creates two opposite dangers. **Too few CS** means rural women die of obstructed labour and rupture. **Unnecessary CS** means a young woman with a scar may later labour at home or at an HC III, far from a theatre, and rupture. So every CS decision in Uganda should consider: *where will this woman deliver next time?*

## 2. Contraindications and Precautions

**Is the baby alive?** A CS for a **dead baby** exposes the mother to all the risk for none of the benefit. A **vaginal birth** is usually safer for her. Exceptions are praevia, transverse lie with obstruction, rupture, or failed destructive options. Always re-check the FHR (Pinard or Doppler) and, where available, confirm IUFD by ultrasound before committing.

**Stabilise first when the mother is the patient**: an eclamptic woman gets **MgSO₄ and BP control before** she goes to theatre; a shocked woman gets resuscitation *while* theatre is prepared. A dead or dying mother cannot be helped by a fast CS on an unstable circulation (except perimortem CS during arrest).

**Contraindications to spinal anaesthesia**: patient refusal, **coagulopathy** (DIC in abruption, HELLP with platelets <75–80 × 10⁹/L), **uncorrected hypovolaemia/shock** (ruptured uterus, massive APH), local sepsis or deformity at the site, raised intracranial pressure, and some cardiac lesions (fixed cardiac output). These women need **GA** with an experienced anaesthetist.

**Precautions**

| Situation | Precaution |
|---|---|
| **Suspected placenta praevia** | **No digital VE outside theatre** (double set-up only) |
| **Previous classical, T- or J-incision** | Absolute contraindication to labour; elective CS at 36–37 weeks |
| **Prophylactic LMWH** | A spinal is usually delayed until at least **12 h after a prophylactic LMWH dose** |
| **MgSO₄ running** | Potentiates muscle relaxants: tell the anaesthetist |
| **Last meal** | Aspiration risk under GA; elective CS fasting about **6 h for solids and 2 h for clear fluids** (unit practice varies) |
| **Allergies** (penicillin, latex, iodine, chlorhexidine) | Antibiotic and skin-prep choice |
| **Severe PE with renal impairment, asthma, PPH with oliguria, peptic ulcer** | Avoid NSAIDs post-op |
| **Views on blood transfusion** (e.g. a Jehovah's Witness) | Must be known and documented before, not during, a haemorrhage |
| **HIV-positive or hepatitis B** | Double gloving and extra sharps care; eMTCT plan for the baby |
| **Fever before surgery** | Cultures, then a **therapeutic** antibiotic course (not a single prophylactic dose) |
| **Oxytocin running with an abnormal FHR or suspected rupture** | Stop it |

## 3. Relevant Anatomy and Normal Values

**Why the lower segment?** In late pregnancy and labour the uterus has two functional parts:

```
          FUNDUS
     ┌───────────────┐
     │ UPPER SEGMENT │  thick, muscular, contracts & retracts,
     │  (contractile)│  very vascular (placental bed)
     ├───────────────┤  ← physiological retraction ring
     │ LOWER SEGMENT │  thin (few mm), mostly fibrous,
     │   (passive)   │  stretches; bladder lies in front
     └──────┬────────┘
          CERVIX
```

- The **lower segment** forms from the isthmus from about 28 weeks and thins further in labour. It is **thin, less vascular and "quiet"** (it hardly contracts), so an incision there bleeds less, heals as a stronger scar, and is less likely to rupture in later labour (about **0.5%**, versus **4–9%** for a classical scar).
- The lower segment is **covered by loose peritoneum (the uterovesical fold)**, which can be peeled down with the bladder. This keeps the bladder out of the way and keeps the scar extraperitoneal.
- In **obstructed labour** the lower segment becomes **very thin and stretched**, and the bladder is **drawn up** onto the abdomen. This is why bladder injury and lateral extensions into the uterine vessels are much more common in Ugandan emergency CS for obstructed labour. A pathological **Bandl's ring** is the visible retraction ring of impending rupture.
- A **very preterm uterus (<28–30 weeks)** has no well-formed lower segment. This is one reason a classical or low vertical incision is sometimes needed.
- The uterus is usually **dextrorotated** (rotated to the right): check that the round ligaments are equidistant before incising, so the incision is central and does not run into the left uterine vessels.
- **Abdominal wall layers**: skin, fat, rectus sheath, rectus muscles (separated in the midline), parietal peritoneum, then the uterovesical fold and the uterus.

**Why antibiotics *before* incision?** The antibiotic must be in the tissues when bacteria arrive, which is at incision. Giving it after cord clamping (the old practice, to "protect the baby") was shown to cause more maternal endometritis and wound infection without benefiting the baby.

**Why a left lateral tilt?** In the supine position the gravid uterus compresses the **inferior vena cava** (less venous return, so hypotension) and the aorta. After spinal anaesthesia (sympathetic block, so vasodilatation) this can cause profound hypotension and fetal hypoxia. Tilting 15° to the left moves the uterus off the vessels.

**Normal values**

| Item | Normal / threshold |
|---|---|
| Fetal heart rate | **110–160 bpm**; listen for a full minute after a contraction |
| Shock index (HR ÷ SBP) | **≥0.9 abnormal**; **≥1.4 needs urgent intervention** |
| Maternal pulse post-op | **<100** |
| Urine output | **≥30 mL/h** |
| Hb | **≥11 g/dL** normal in pregnancy; **<7 g/dL severe** |
| WBC in labour | Up to about **25 × 10⁹/L** can be normal |
| Platelets for a spinal | Above about **75–80 × 10⁹/L** |
| Fibrinogen | **<2 g/L** = severe coagulopathy |
| Bedside clotting test | Clot by **7 minutes** (no clot = coagulopathy) |
| Random blood sugar | **>11 mmol/L** is high |
| Creatinine | **>90 µmol/L** is raised in pregnancy |
| Fundus day 1 post-op | Firm, at or below the umbilicus, mildly tender |
| Fasting before elective CS | About **6 h solids, 2 h clear fluids** |
| Moulding | Graded **+1 to +3**; head palpable in **fifths (5/5 to 0/5)** |

## 4. Equipment and Preparation

**Equipment**: a CS set (scalpels, Mayo scissors, Kocher's forceps, artery forceps, **Doyen retractor**, **Green-Armytage** clamps, Babcock forceps, needle holders, swabs counted), **#1 absorbable suture** (polyglactin/Vicryl or chromic catgut) for the uterus and sheath, 3-0 Vicryl/Monocryl or nylon or staples for skin, suction, diathermy, chlorhexidine-alcohol or povidone-iodine, Foley catheter (14–16 Fr) and bag, two 16–18 G cannulae, crystalloid, cross-matched blood, spinal set and hyperbaric bupivacaine, oxytocin, a **resuscitaire with bag-valve-mask** and warm towels for the baby.

### Pre-operative assessment: history

When you clerk a woman who may need a CS, or a woman with a previous scar in labour, every question has a purpose:

| Question | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| **Gravidity, parity, gestational age (LNMP and early scan)** | Term or preterm? Robson group? Preterm changes the incision and the neonatal plan |
| **The indication in one line**; absolute or relative? | A relative indication (e.g. "slow progress") should make you ask whether augmentation or a ventouse is possible first |
| **Number of previous CS, their indications, and where done** | A **recurrent** indication (CPD, contracted pelvis) predicts a failed TOLAC. A non-recurrent one (breech, fetal distress) predicts success. **Two or more scars** means elective CS in most Ugandan units |
| **Type of uterine incision** (ask for the operation note or discharge form) | A **classical or T-shaped incision is an absolute contraindication to labour** and changes the incision, the urgency and the next-pregnancy plan. The skin scar does NOT tell you the uterine incision |
| **Post-op complications after the last CS** (fever, wound sepsis, long admission, re-operation) | Post-op sepsis may have weakened the scar and raises the rupture risk |
| **Inter-delivery interval** | Less than 18 months raises the rupture risk |
| **Any vaginal birth before or after the CS** | The single best predictor of VBAC success |
| **Onset, duration and strength of labour; time membranes ruptured; colour of liquor** | Prolonged labour or ROM increases infection risk and the need for therapeutic antibiotics. Meconium suggests fetal compromise |
| **Pushing at home or at a lower facility; traditional herbs (e.g. *emumbwa*); oxytocin given elsewhere** | These are major rupture risks; herbal oxytocics cause hyperstimulation |
| **Pain character**: constant between contractions? shoulder-tip pain? | Constant pain suggests abruption or rupture. Shoulder-tip pain means haemoperitoneum irritating the diaphragm |
| **Bleeding** (APH), **fetal movements** | Praevia or abruption. Absent movements may mean IUFD, which changes the decision (a vaginal route is usually preferred for a dead baby) |
| **Medical history**: hypertension, diabetes, sickle cell, cardiac disease, asthma, bleeding disorders, **HIV status and viral load; hepatitis B** | Anaesthetic and drug choices (e.g. avoid NSAIDs in severe PE with renal impairment), eMTCT plan, VTE risk, sharps care |
| **Current drugs**: MgSO₄, antihypertensives, anticoagulants, ARVs | LMWH timing for spinal; MgSO₄ potentiates muscle relaxants |
| **Previous anaesthetic problems** | Anaesthetic plan |
| **Time of last meal and last drink** | Aspiration risk if general anaesthesia is needed |
| **Allergies** (penicillin, latex, iodine, chlorhexidine) | Antibiotic and skin-prep choice |
| **Views on blood transfusion** | Must be documented before theatre |
| **Future fertility wishes; contraception** | Offer **tubal ligation** or **PPIUD** with proper, separate consent before theatre. Plan the next birth |

### Pre-operative assessment: examination

Follow the order of Hutchison's Clinical Methods (24th edition). In a Category 1 situation, the focused ABC, fetal heart and abdominal check (section 7) come first; do not delay delivery for a full examination.

1. **Introduction, identity, explanation and consent**; chaperone for the vaginal examination; **hand hygiene**.
2. **Position and exposure**: semi-recumbent with a **left lateral tilt** (avoid supine hypotension); expose the abdomen from xiphisternum to symphysis; keep her covered otherwise.
3. **General inspection and vital signs**: pallor (Hb for transfusion planning), **dehydration** (dry tongue, sunken eyes, **ketones** after a long labour), fever (chorioamnionitis), oedema, **weight/BMI** (antibiotic dose, choice of incision, VTE risk). **Pulse and BP** (tachycardia is the earliest sign of haemorrhage or sepsis; hypertension points to PE), RR, temperature, SpO₂, shock index.
4. **Hands, face and neck; airway and back** (for the anaesthetist, but you should notice): palmar and conjunctival pallor; obesity, short neck, limited mouth opening; skin infection or deformity over the lumbar spine (contraindicates a spinal at that site).
5. **Chest and heart**: wheeze, crackles, murmurs.
6. **Obstetric abdominal examination**:
   - **Inspection**: scars (Pfannenstiel, midline) and their healing; distension; a **Bandl's ring** (a groove running obliquely across the abdomen).
   - **Palpation**: **tenderness over the scar** (a late and unreliable sign of dehiscence); **fundal height**; **lie**; **presentation**; **engagement in fifths palpable** (5/5 to 0/5): a head that is **3/5 or more palpable** after a long labour, with moulding, means CPD; a tender, tense lower segment with Bandl's ring means **impending rupture**; contractions (frequency and strength); easily palpable fetal parts (rupture).
   - **Auscultation of the fetal heart** for a full minute after a contraction.
7. **Bladder**: distended? **Haematuria** on catheterisation suggests obstructed labour or rupture involving the bladder.
8. **Vaginal examination** (not if placenta praevia is suspected, until in theatre with a double set-up): cervical dilatation, **station, position, caput and moulding (graded +1 to +3)**, liquor colour, cord presentation or prolapse, oedematous cervix or vulva (obstruction), and the pelvis (sacral promontory, ischial spines, subpubic angle). If she is **fully dilated**, repeat the VE in theatre: the head may now be low enough for a ventouse.
9. **Complete**: thank her, cover her, hand hygiene, **summarise** with the indication and urgency category, and document positives and key negatives.

### Pre-operative investigations (what to send)

**Hb and group & cross-match** for all (2 units; 4 or more for praevia, abruption, rupture or accreta); **HIV test** if unknown; **urine dipstick**; add **platelets, creatinine, LFTs and clotting** (or a bedside clotting test) for PE, abruption, sepsis or IUFD; **malaria RDT** if febrile. Full table and interpretation in section 6.

### Decision and preparation (numbered checklist)

1. **Decide with a senior** (medical officer or obstetrician) and **assign the urgency category**. Write the indication clearly.
2. **Consent**: explain in her language (Luganda/Runyankole with an interpreter if needed) the indication, what will happen, the alternatives, and the risks. Risks include bleeding and transfusion, infection, injury to the bladder, bowel or ureter, VTE, hysterectomy (rare), anaesthetic risks, a cut to the baby (1–2%), and implications for future pregnancies (rupture, praevia/accreta). Consent separately for **tubal ligation** or **PPIUD** if requested. She is the one who consents. The husband's signature is not legally required for an adult woman, although involving him is culturally wise.
3. **IV access**: two **large-bore (16–18 G)** cannulae. Take blood for **Hb and cross-match** (2 units; 4 for praevia, abruption or rupture).
4. **Fluids**: correct dehydration (e.g. obstructed labour) with **Ringer's lactate or normal saline**. Pre-load or co-load **500–1000 mL** crystalloid for spinal.
5. **Aspiration prophylaxis** (especially in labour): **metoclopramide 10 mg IV** plus **omeprazole 40 mg IV** (or an H2 blocker if available), and where available **sodium citrate 0.3 M 30 mL orally** just before induction of anaesthesia.
6. **Urinary catheter** (Foley 14–16 Fr) to empty the bladder, keep it out of the operative field and monitor urine output.
7. **Antibiotic prophylaxis within 60 minutes before skin incision** (drug table below).
8. **Vaginal cleansing with povidone-iodine** for women in labour or with ruptured membranes. This reduces post-op endometritis.
9. **Hair**: do **not shave**. Clip only if hair is in the way (shaving causes micro-abrasions that become infected).
10. In theatre: **left lateral tilt 15°**, **check the fetal heart again**, and (if she was fully dilated) **re-examine vaginally**.
11. **Neonatal preparation**: a person trained in **Helping Babies Breathe** with a bag-valve-mask, a warm towel and a resuscitaire at every CS.
12. **WHO Surgical Safety Checklist**: Sign in, Time out (the team confirms patient, procedure, category, antibiotics given, blood available), Sign out (counts, specimens, plan).

### Anaesthesia

- **Spinal (subarachnoid) anaesthesia is the method of choice** for most CS. The mother is awake, there is **no airway risk** (failed intubation and aspiration were historically major causes of anaesthetic death in pregnancy), there is less blood loss, and she has early contact with the baby.
- **Contraindications to spinal**: section 2.
- **General anaesthesia (GA)** is used when a spinal is contraindicated or too slow (some Category 1 cases), or when it fails. It needs rapid sequence induction with cricoid pressure, and a skilled anaesthetist.
- **Spinal complications**: hypotension (the commonest; tilt, fluids, vasopressor), high or total spinal (breathlessness, arm weakness, bradycardia, apnoea), nausea, shivering, **post-dural puncture headache** (worse when upright; fluids, analgesia, caffeine; blood patch if severe), and rare meningitis.

> [!DRUG]
> Prophylaxis is **one dose**. Repeat the dose only if the operation lasts more than about 3–4 hours or blood loss exceeds 1500 mL. Prolonged "prophylactic" courses (for example ceftriaxone for 5 days after every CS) do **not** prevent SSI. They breed resistance and are recommended against by the WHO SSI guideline. Give a **therapeutic** course only when there is established infection (chorioamnionitis, prolonged obstructed labour with fever, gross contamination).

> [!UGANDA]
> Cefazolin is not stocked in many Ugandan public hospitals. The usual practice (Mulago/Kawempe, Mengo, most RRHs) is **ceftriaxone 1–2 g IV ± metronidazole 500 mg IV given at induction of anaesthesia**, i.e. before incision. A Mulago RCT showed that giving it **before incision** rather than after cord clamping reduced post-op infection. Follow your unit protocol, but insist on the **timing**. Where enoxaparin is unavailable, give **UFH 5000 IU SC 12-hourly**, and in every case use **early mobilisation and hydration**.

## 5. Procedure Step by Step

### A. Surgical steps of a lower-segment CS

1. **Position** supine with 15° left tilt. Catheter in. **Skin prep** with chlorhexidine-alcohol (or povidone-iodine), letting it **dry** (it is flammable with diathermy). Drape.
2. **Skin incision**:
   - **Joel-Cohen**: straight transverse, about **3 cm above the symphysis**, higher than Pfannenstiel. The later layers are opened mostly **bluntly**. It is faster, with less blood loss, fever and pain, and is recommended by NICE.
   - **Pfannenstiel**: curved (smile-shaped) transverse incision **2 fingerbreadths (about 2–3 cm) above the symphysis**, 12–15 cm long, with sharp dissection. It gives a good cosmetic result.
   - **Subumbilical midline**: when **rapid wide access** is needed (suspected **ruptured uterus**, a huge fibroid, anticipated hysterectomy, previous midline scar), or sometimes for a Category 1 CS by less experienced surgeons.
3. **Fat**: incise centrally, then separate with the fingers.
4. **Rectus sheath**: small transverse incision, extended laterally with **Mayo scissors** or bluntly. In a Pfannenstiel, the sheath is lifted with **Kocher's** forceps and freed from the rectus muscles. **Separate the recti** in the midline with the fingers.
5. **Parietal peritoneum**: open **high** (bladder!) with fingers, or lift it between two artery forceps, feel that nothing is caught, and incise. Extend with care.
6. **Doyen retractor** over the bladder. Check for **dextrorotation** by checking that the round ligaments are equidistant, so the incision is central and doesn't run into the left uterine vessels.
7. **Uterovesical fold**: incise transversely and push the bladder down (a bladder flap). Omitting the flap is acceptable in most cases.
8. **Uterine incision**: a transverse 2–3 cm incision in the lower segment with the scalpel, **stroke by stroke** (to avoid cutting the baby), until membranes or liquor appear. Then **extend bluntly** with the fingers laterally and slightly upwards. Blunt extension has less blood loss and fewer uncontrolled extensions than cutting with scissors.
9. **Delivery**: remove the Doyen. Slip a hand below the head, flex it and lift it out of the incision while the assistant gives **fundal pressure**. Suction the mouth and nose only if needed. Deliver the body. **Delay cord clamping for at least 1 minute** in a vigorous baby.
   - A **deeply impacted head** (second-stage CS): the assistant pushes the head up vaginally ("push"), or deliver by the feet first ("pull", reverse breech). Call a senior early.
   - **Breech or transverse**: grasp the feet (breech extraction); enlarge the incision if needed (J or inverted-T, which must be documented because it changes all future births).
10. **Oxytocin 10 IU slow IV** (or IM). **Placenta by controlled cord traction** rather than manual removal (less endometritis and blood loss). **Check the cavity is empty** with a dry swab.
11. **Uterine closure**: **Green-Armytage** clamps on the angles and bleeding edges. Close in **two layers** with **#1 absorbable (polyglactin/Vicryl or chromic catgut)**: a continuous (often locked) first layer, then an imbricating second layer. Two-layer closure is standard in Uganda and recommended by NICE, and it may reduce later rupture. **Secure the angles** beyond the apex, where most bleeding comes from.
12. **Check haemostasis**, tubes and ovaries. Perform **tubal ligation** if consented (e.g. modified Pomeroy with a Babcock).
13. **Peritoneal toilet**: remove clots and liquor from the gutters. Do the **swab, needle and instrument count**.
14. **Peritoneum**: NICE advises that visceral and parietal peritoneum need not be closed. Many Ugandan surgeons close the parietal layer; both approaches are acceptable.
15. **Rectus sheath**: continuous **#1 Vicryl (or PDS/nylon loop)**, bites about 1 cm from the edge and 1 cm apart, not strangulating.
16. **Subcutaneous fat**: close only if **>2 cm thick** (reduces wound separation).
17. **Skin**: subcuticular absorbable (3-0 Vicryl/Monocryl), interrupted nylon, or staples.
18. **Finish**: fundal massage and **vaginal toilet** (express clots), check the uterus is contracted, **estimate blood loss**, and write the **operation note** (section 9).

### B. When is a classical CS indicated?

| Indication | Why |
|---|---|
| **Very preterm** (<28–30 wk) with an undeveloped lower segment, especially with breech/transverse | No room for a safe transverse lower-segment incision |
| **Transverse lie, back down, with ruptured membranes** (arm prolapse, impacted) | The fetus cannot be reached through the lower segment |
| **Anterior placenta praevia with large vessels / accreta** | To avoid cutting through the placenta; may precede hysterectomy |
| **Large lower-segment fibroid** or dense adhesions/bladder over the lower segment | Lower segment inaccessible |
| **Invasive cervical cancer** | Avoid cutting into the tumour; may proceed to radical surgery |
| **Perimortem CS** (at 4–5 min of maternal arrest) | Speed |

> [!EXAM]
> Classical scar implications: rupture risk in later pregnancy is **4–9%**, and rupture can occur **before labour** (in the third trimester). So every future birth must be by **elective CS at 36–37 weeks, before labour**, and she must never labour at home. Document this in bold on the discharge form and her Mother–Child Health Passport.

### C. Immediate post-operative care (recovery room, first 2 hours)

1. Vital signs (**pulse, BP, RR, SpO₂**) every **15 minutes for the first hour**, then every 30 minutes for 2 hours, then 4-hourly.
2. **Uterine tone and lochia** every 15 minutes. **Rub up** the uterus if it is soft and look for PPH.
3. Wound dressing (soakage), **urine output** (≥30 mL/h), level of spinal block (recovering legs).
4. **Skin-to-skin and breastfeeding within the first hour.**
5. **Oxytocin infusion 20 IU in 1 L at about 40 drops/min** for high-risk women (prolonged labour, twins, polyhydramnios, praevia, PPH at surgery).

**Analgesia (multimodal, regular, not "PRN only")**

| Drug | Dose | Route | Frequency | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Paracetamol** | 1 g | Oral / IV | 6-hourly (max 4 g/day) | Baseline for everyone |
| **Diclofenac** | 50 mg oral 8-hourly, or 75 mg IM 12-hourly; or 100 mg rectal | Oral / IM / PR | For 48–72 h | Avoid in **severe PE with renal impairment**, asthma, PPH with oliguria, peptic ulcer. Safe in breastfeeding |
| **Tramadol** | 50–100 mg | Oral / IM / IV | 6–8-hourly PRN | Moderate pain |
| **Morphine** | 5–10 mg (0.1 mg/kg) | IM (or titrated IV) | 4-hourly PRN | Severe pain first 24 h; monitor RR and sedation |
| **Pethidine** (still used in Uganda) | 50–100 mg | IM | 4-hourly PRN | Short-acting; nausea |

### D. Ward care and ERAS (Enhanced Recovery After Surgery)

- **Eat and drink early**: sips of water within 1–2 hours and light food when hungry, if uncomplicated. The old rule of "nil by mouth until bowel sounds or flatus" is **not** needed after an uncomplicated CS and prolongs IV fluids, hunger and ileus.
- **Early mobilisation**: sit out and walk within **6–12 hours**. This prevents VTE, chest infection and ileus.
- **Catheter out** once she is mobile, **≥12 hours after the last spinal dose** (usually the next morning). Keep it **7–14 days** if the **bladder was injured and repaired**, or after prolonged obstructed labour with haematuria (to prevent a VVF from pressure necrosis).
- **Stop IV fluids** once she is drinking.
- **Thromboprophylaxis**: risk-assess every woman. Give LMWH or UFH for emergency CS in labour and any extra risk factors (obesity, age >35, PE, infection, PPH >1 L, immobility, sickle cell).
- **Wound**: remove the dressing at **24–48 h** and keep it clean and dry. Remove non-absorbable skin sutures on **day 5–7** (Pfannenstiel day 5; midline 7–10).
- **Antibiotics**: none after surgery unless there is infection.
- **Daily review**: the day-1 "head to toe, then the 6 Ps" review (section 6) and the questions: pain control; nausea or vomiting; **eating and drinking**; **passing flatus**; **passing urine** after the catheter is out; **mobilising**; **breastfeeding** and the baby's condition; **headache** (post-dural puncture headache is worse on standing; severe headache with high BP means pre-eclampsia); **calf pain or breathlessness** (VTE); **fever, rigors, foul lochia or wound discharge** (infection); **heavy bleeding**.
- **Discharge**: usually on day 2–3 if afebrile, eating, mobile, voiding, the wound is clean, Hb is adequate (give **ferrous sulphate 200 mg + folic acid** for 3 months if anaemic), and breastfeeding is established.

### E. Discharge counselling: before discharge, every woman should know

1. **Why** she had a CS, and whether the reason is likely to recur.
2. **What type of uterine incision** she has. Write it on her card.
3. The **plan for the next birth**: a hospital (HC IV or above) delivery; TOLAC versus ERCS; never deliver at home or at an HC II/III.
4. **Spacing**: wait **at least 18–24 months before the next conception** (WHO recommends a ≥24-month interpregnancy interval). Offer contraception: **PPIUD** (can be placed at CS), implant, injectable, lactational amenorrhoea, or BTL.
5. **Danger signs**: fever, foul lochia, heavy bleeding, wound discharge or opening, calf pain, breathlessness, severe headache, and not passing urine.
6. Postnatal review at **6 days and 6 weeks**. HIV-exposed baby: NVP prophylaxis and an EID plan.

### Bedside tests

| **Urine dipstick** | **Protein** (pre-eclampsia), **blood** (obstruction, bladder injury), **ketones** (dehydration after long labour) | Nitrites and leucocytes if urinary symptoms or fever |

> [!PEARL]
> **Bedside clotting test (WHO MCPC):** put **2 mL of venous blood** in a small, clean, dry plain **glass** tube and hold it in your closed fist to keep it warm. After **4 minutes** tip it gently, then every minute. If **no clot has formed by 7 minutes**, or a soft clot breaks down easily, she has a **coagulopathy**. Tell the anaesthetist (spinal is contraindicated) and get fresh whole blood or FFP ready. (Some units use the 20-minute whole-blood clotting test instead: no clot at 20 minutes = coagulopathy.)

### Laboratory and imaging

| Test | When | Expected / abnormal finding | How it changes management |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Hb / FBC** (HemoCue at HC IV; full FBC at hospital) | Every CS, before surgery; day 1–2 after if EBL ≥1 L or symptomatic | Hb ≥11 g/dL normal; **<7 g/dL severe**. WBC up to ~25 × 10⁹/L can be normal in labour. **Platelets <75–80 × 10⁹/L** | Transfusion planning; low platelets rule out a spinal; post-op anaemia means iron (or transfusion if symptomatic) |
| **Blood group and cross-match** (HC IV with a blood bank; always at RRH/national) | Every emergency CS | **2 units** routinely; **4+ units** for praevia, abruption, rupture or suspected accreta | Blood must be in theatre before a high-risk CS starts |
| **HIV rapid test** (all levels) | Status unknown, or no recent negative test (national retesting schedule) | Reactive → confirm per national algorithm | Same-day ART for the mother; NVP prophylaxis and EID plan for the baby; double gloving |
| **Urinalysis** (dipstick at all levels) | Every woman | Protein; blood; ketones | Suggests PE, obstructed labour or dehydration |
| **Random blood sugar** (all levels) | Diabetes, steroids, obesity | >11 mmol/L is high | Peri-op glucose control (reduces SSI) |
| **Creatinine, electrolytes, LFTs** (RRH/national; some HC IVs) | Severe PE, HELLP, abruption, sepsis, prolonged obstructed labour | Creatinine >90 µmol/L, raised ALT/AST | Avoid NSAIDs; fluid plan; HDU care |
| **Clotting: PT, APTT, fibrinogen** (RRH/national; bedside clotting test elsewhere) | Abruption, HELLP, IUFD, massive PPH | **Fibrinogen <2 g/L** = severe coagulopathy | No spinal; FFP, cryoprecipitate or fresh whole blood |
| **Sickle cell test** (where indicated) | Known or suspected SCD | HbSS | Hydration, oxygen, warmth, transfusion plan, thromboprophylaxis |
| **Malaria RDT or blood smear** (all levels) | Any fever before or after CS | Positive | Treat malaria; fever in labour is not always chorioamnionitis |
| **Blood cultures** (RRH/national; often unavailable at HC IV) | Fever with sepsis signs, before or after CS | Growth of Gram-negatives (often ESBL) or *Staph* | Switch from empirical to targeted antibiotics |
| **Pus swab / wound culture** (RRH/national) | Discharging or opened wound | *Klebsiella*, *E. coli* (often **ceftriaxone-resistant**), MRSA | Guides antibiotics; alert IPC if several cases |
| **Urine culture** (RRH/national) | Fever with urinary symptoms, or after a long catheter | Significant growth | Targeted antibiotic |
| **Obstetric ultrasound** (many HC IVs have a machine; skill varies) | Before an elective or planned CS; APH; suspected IUFD | Viability, presentation, **placental site** (praevia, accreta signs), EFW | Plans incision, blood and seniority; confirms IUFD (vaginal birth usually better) |
| **Pelvic/abdominal ultrasound after CS** (RRH/national) | Fever not settling after 48–72 h of antibiotics, a mass or wound swelling | Pelvic, subsheath or wound **collection**, retained products | Drainage or re-laparotomy |
| **Doppler ultrasound of the leg / CTPA** (national referral or private) | Suspected DVT or PE | Thrombus | Therapeutic LMWH; do not wait for imaging if PE is likely |

Three points to remember:

- **Blood is the investigation that saves lives at CS.** Send the cross-match at the time of decision, not when the bleeding starts: **2 units** for a routine emergency CS and **4 or more** for praevia, abruption, rupture or accreta.
- **Platelets and clotting decide the anaesthetic.** A spinal is safe with platelets above about **75–80 × 10⁹/L** and a normal bedside clotting test.
- **Fever is not always chorioamnionitis or a wound infection in Uganda.** Always do a malaria RDT or smear.

| Check | Normal | Abnormal: think |
|---|---|---|
| **Pulse, BP, temperature, RR** | Pulse <100, afebrile | Tachycardia: bleeding, sepsis, PE, anaemia. **Fever ≥38.0 °C**: see the fever workup (section 8) |
| **Pallor** | Pink conjunctivae | Anaemia: check Hb |
| **Chest** | Clear | Basal crackles (atelectasis, aspiration) |
| **Breasts** | Soft, milk coming | Engorgement, cracked nipples |

### Interpreting deterioration in labour in a woman with a scar (or in obstructed labour)

| Condition | Distinguishing features | Key investigation / action |
|---|---|---|
| **Uterine rupture** | Scar or obstruction, **abnormal FHR (earliest)**, constant pain, contractions stop, loss of station, easily felt fetal parts, shock out of proportion to visible bleeding, haematuria | Clinical diagnosis. **Immediate laparotomy.** Bedside US may show free fluid or a fetus outside the uterus, but don't delay |
| **Placental abruption** | Painful dark bleeding, **woody-hard tender uterus**, contractions continue, fetal distress or IUFD, shock, may have PE | Clinical; Hb, clotting (bedside clotting time) for DIC; deliver |
| **Placenta praevia** | **Painless** bright bleeding, **soft non-tender** uterus, high head or malpresentation, FHR usually normal | Ultrasound for placental site. **No digital VE** |
| **Obstructed labour without rupture** | Prolonged labour, 3+ moulding, caput, head ≥3/5, Bandl's ring, oedematous vulva, maternal exhaustion, haematuria | Clinical and partograph (past the action line). CS |
| **Chorioamnionitis** | Fever, maternal and fetal tachycardia, uterine tenderness, foul liquor | FBC, blood culture; therapeutic antibiotics; deliver |
| **Scar tenderness only / "scar pain"** | Local tenderness with normal FHR, vitals and progress | Close monitoring. A normal FHR is reassuring |

### Worked example 1: interpreting pre-op results

A woman with a **placental abruption** and a live baby at 36 weeks: Hb 8.9 g/dL, platelets 62 × 10⁹/L, bedside clotting test **no clot at 7 minutes**. *Interpretation:* anaemia plus **consumptive coagulopathy (DIC)**. *What changes:* a **spinal is contraindicated** (low platelets and coagulopathy), so she needs **GA** with an experienced anaesthetist; cross-match **4 units** and ask for **fresh whole blood or FFP**; give **tranexamic acid** if bleeding at surgery; prepare for **PPH** (oxytocin infusion, uterine compression sutures); and plan HDU care afterwards.

## 7. Clinical Acumen

### First 5 minutes: when you are called

**To a woman who may need a CS:**

1. **Look at her and at the baby's heart first.** Airway and breathing, colour, conscious level. Take **pulse, BP, RR, temperature and SpO₂**, and work out the **shock index** (HR ÷ SBP; **≥0.9 is abnormal**, **≥1.4 needs urgent intervention**). Listen to the **fetal heart for a full minute after a contraction** (normal 110–160 bpm).
2. **Look for the Category 1 situations**: cord prolapse, sustained fetal bradycardia, suspected uterine rupture, major abruption with a live baby, maternal collapse. These cannot wait for a full clerking.
3. **Call for help early**: the medical officer or obstetrician (to confirm the decision), the **anaesthetist/anaesthetic officer**, the theatre team, and the lab for blood. **Say the category out loud.**
4. **Start in parallel** (one person each, not one after another): two **16–18 G cannulae**, bloods for **Hb and cross-match**, **stop any oxytocin** if the FHR is abnormal or rupture is suspected, **left lateral position**, oxygen if she is shocked, a **Foley catheter**, and fluids (Ringer's lactate or normal saline) if she is dehydrated or bleeding.
5. **Stabilise first when the mother is the patient** (section 2).

**To a woman after a CS** (bleeding, low BP, fever, "not looking well"): pulse, BP, RR, SpO₂, temperature, **fundus (height and tone)**, **lochia and pad**, **wound dressing**, **urine output** in the catheter bag, and a quick look at the drug and fluid chart. A **pulse >100 with a rising fundus or a soaked pad** is PPH until proven otherwise. Rub up the uterus and call your senior.

### Red flags: escalate now

> [!REDFLAG]
> - **Fetal bradycardia (<110 bpm sustained) or recurrent decelerations in a woman with a scar** means suspect rupture. Category 1.
> - **Maternal tachycardia, hypotension, pallor or collapse** in labour means haemorrhage (abruption, rupture) until proven otherwise.
> - **Constant abdominal pain, shoulder-tip pain, contractions that suddenly stop, presenting part that recedes (loss of station), fetal parts felt easily under the skin**: uterine rupture.
> - **Bandl's ring, haematuria, oedematous vulva, 3+ moulding, head ≥3/5 palpable** after prolonged labour: obstructed labour. Do not augment; prepare CS.
> - **Cord felt in the vagina** (cord prolapse): Category 1 if the baby is alive and she is not fully dilated.
> - **Fever >38 °C, foul liquor, fetal tachycardia**: chorioamnionitis. Give therapeutic antibiotics, not prophylaxis only.
> - **Post-op**: pulse >100, falling BP, rising fundus, heavy lochia, a distended silent abdomen, fever after day 2, wound discharge, calf swelling, breathlessness.

### Thinking like a clinician

1. **Is vaginal birth impossible, or just harder?** Absolute indications (major praevia, transverse lie in labour, true CPD, previous classical scar) have no alternative. For relative indications, weigh the risk to the baby now against the risk to this woman now and in every future pregnancy.
2. **Is the baby alive?** A CS for a dead baby exposes the mother to all the risk for none of the benefit (section 2).
3. **"If X then think Y"**:
   - Previous CS plus a **new** abnormal FHR: **rupture**, not "fetal distress".
   - Prolonged labour plus **haematuria** plus a high head: **obstructed labour** with bladder compression. Expect a thin lower segment and a high bladder at surgery, so incise the peritoneum high.
   - Fully dilated plus a **deeply impacted head**: expect a difficult delivery of the head and extensions. Call a senior. Prepare the "push" (an assistant pushes up from the vagina) or "pull" (reverse breech extraction) technique.
   - APH plus a soft non-tender uterus plus a high or malpresenting part: **placenta praevia**. **No VE outside theatre.**
   - Fever before surgery: take cultures, then give **therapeutic** antibiotics (not a single dose).
4. **Common student pitfalls:**
   - Assuming a Pfannenstiel skin scar means a lower-segment uterine incision. Not necessarily.
   - Forgetting to check the FHR in theatre, or forgetting to re-examine a fully dilated woman (a ventouse may be possible).
   - Writing "fetal distress" without describing the FHR (rate, pattern, relation to contractions, duration).
   - Giving antibiotics "after the baby is out".
   - Keeping women "nil by mouth until bowel sounds return". This is an outdated practice.
   - Forgetting the **next-pregnancy counselling** at discharge.

### Planning the next birth: trial of labour after CS (TOLAC) / VBAC

**Who is a good candidate?**

- **One** previous **lower-segment transverse** CS with an uncomplicated recovery.
- Singleton, **cephalic**, term (37–41 weeks), **spontaneous onset** of labour.
- **Non-recurrent** previous indication (breech, fetal distress, APH); a **previous vaginal birth** helps.
- Inter-delivery interval **≥18 months**; no other indication for CS; a clinically adequate pelvis; EFW under about 4 kg.
- **Delivering in a facility that can perform an immediate CS and transfuse (HC IV/hospital) with continuous midwife presence.**

**Contraindications to TOLAC**: previous **classical, T or J incision**; previous **uterine rupture**; **placenta praevia**; any other indication for CS (e.g. transverse lie); **2 or more previous CS** (in most Ugandan units; RCOG allows a carefully counselled trial); **a facility without CS capability**.

**Counselling numbers (RCOG GTG 45)**

| | Planned VBAC | Elective repeat CS |
|---|---|---|
| Chance of vaginal birth | **72–75%** (85–90% if previous vaginal birth) | 0% |
| Uterine rupture | **About 0.5% (1 in 200)**; 2–3 times higher with induction or augmentation | Extremely rare (about 0.02%) |
| Advantages | Shorter recovery, avoids surgery, lower risk of praevia/accreta and surgical complications in future pregnancies | Planned, avoids labour and rupture |
| Disadvantages | About 1 in 4 need an emergency CS in labour (higher morbidity than an elective CS) | Surgical risks; each additional scar raises future accreta risk; neonatal TTN |

**Management of TOLAC in labour ("trial of scar")**

1. Admit early in labour. Confirm the previous incision type from the notes. Take **Hb and group & save/cross-match**, and insert an IV cannula.
2. Monitor with a **partograph**. FHR: continuous CTG where available (RCOG). In most Ugandan units, **intermittent auscultation every 15 minutes in the first stage and every 5 minutes in the second stage**, always after a contraction.
3. Watch maternal pulse, pain and scar tenderness, vaginal bleeding and haematuria, descent (fifths) and contractions.
4. **Progress**: expect normal progress. **Do not allow the partograph to cross the action line**; slow progress means reassessment by a senior and usually a CS.
5. **Oxytocin augmentation**: only with a senior decision, in a hospital, at a low dose with careful titration. Many Ugandan units avoid it.
6. **Induction**: **misoprostol is contraindicated** with a uterine scar at term. Use **Foley catheter ripening, then ARM, then cautious oxytocin** only at a referral hospital, with counselling about the higher rupture risk.
7. Second stage: avoid prolonged pushing. Consider an assisted delivery (ventouse) for delay. Some units routinely shorten the second stage.
8. After a VBAC, watch for PPH and signs of scar dehiscence (unexplained tachycardia, pain, bleeding, haematuria).

### What happens at HC IV vs referral hospital

| Setting | What to do |
|---|---|
| **HC III** | Identify women with a scar or CS indication at ANC and refer them for **delivery at HC IV/hospital**. In labour: partograph; refer early if progress is slow or the scar is tender. Before referral: IV line, fluids, catheter if obstructed, **first-dose antibiotics** if ROM is prolonged or she has a fever, and a referral note. Send with a health worker and a blood donor |
| **HC IV** (CEmONC) | Routine and emergency LSCS under spinal, transfusion, TOLAC for well-selected women, repair of simple rupture, bladder repair only if skilled |
| **General / regional referral hospital** | Complex CS: 2 or more previous scars, praevia/accreta, second-stage impacted heads, rupture needing hysterectomy, severe PE/HELLP, cardiac disease, bladder/ureteric injury, ICU/HDU care, VBAC induction with Foley |
| **National referral (Kawempe/Mulago)** | Placenta accreta spectrum, multiple scars with complications, fistula management, ICU |

## 8. Complications and Troubleshooting

| Timing | Maternal | Neonatal |
|---|---|---|
| **Intra-operative** | **Haemorrhage** (atony, angle extensions into the broad ligament and uterine vessels, praevia/accreta); **bladder injury** (repeat CS, obstructed labour); ureteric injury (lateral extensions, blind clamping); bowel injury (adhesions); anaesthetic (hypotension, high spinal, failed intubation, aspiration); amniotic fluid embolism | **Fetal laceration** (1–2%); difficult delivery of an impacted head causing skull fracture or hypoxia |
| **Early post-op (days)** | **PPH**; **endometritis and SSI** (the commonest); UTI; **wound haematoma, dehiscence or burst abdomen**; paralytic ileus; **VTE** (DVT/PE); urinary retention; post-dural puncture headache; chest infection; **sepsis and death** | **Transient tachypnoea of the newborn** (especially elective CS before 39 weeks); respiratory distress; delayed breastfeeding |
| **Late** | Adhesions and chronic pelvic pain; incisional hernia; **caesarean scar niche** (isthmocele) causing spotting, dysmenorrhoea and subfertility; **scar ectopic pregnancy**; **uterine rupture** in a later pregnancy; **placenta praevia and placenta accreta spectrum** (the risk climbs with each CS) | Possibly altered microbiome and a small increase in asthma or obesity (association) |

> [!PEARL]
> **Placenta accreta risk climbs steeply with each CS**, especially when the placenta is praevia over the scar. With a praevia, the accreta risk is about **11% after one previous CS, about 40% after two and over 60% after three** (Silver et al., 2006, a large US cohort). This is the strongest argument for avoiding the *first* unnecessary CS in a woman who wants a large family.

**Troubleshooting**

| Problem | Action |
|---|---|
| **Spinal hypotension** | Left tilt, IV fluids, phenylephrine 50–100 µg or ephedrine 6 mg bolus; treat early (hypotension harms the baby) |
| **High or total spinal** | Breathlessness, arm weakness, bradycardia, apnoea: airway support and ventilation by the anaesthetist |
| **Post-dural puncture headache** | Worse upright: fluids, analgesia, caffeine; blood patch if severe |
| **Deeply impacted head** | Call a senior; "push" from below or "pull" (reverse breech) |
| **Lateral extension / angle bleeding** | Green-Armytage clamps, secure the angles beyond the apex; identify the ureter before deep sutures |
| **Atony / PPH at surgery** | Oxytocin infusion, tranexamic acid 1 g IV, uterine compression sutures; hysterectomy if needed |
| **Bladder injury** | Repair in 2 layers; catheter for **7–14 days** |
| **Post-op PPH** (pulse >100, rising fundus, soaked pad) | Rub up the uterus, oxytocin, call the senior; return to theatre if bleeding continues |
| **Wound discharge** | Open the wound at the point of discharge, drain pus, swab for culture, daily dressing |
| **Fever not settling after 48–72 h of antibiotics** | Ultrasound for a pelvic or subsheath collection or retained products; consider a resistant organism; drain |

### Worked example 2: the post-CS fever workup

A woman is febrile (**38.6 °C**, pulse 108) on **day 4** after an emergency CS for obstructed labour. Think through the causes by timing, using the **"W" list**:

| Cause | Typical day | Clues | Test |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Womb** (endometritis) | Day 2–5 | Tender uterus, **offensive lochia**, long labour, many VEs | FBC, blood culture; high vaginal swab |
| **Wound** (SSI) | **Day 4–7** | Redness, induration, pus, pain out of proportion | Pus swab culture before antibiotics |
| **Water** (UTI) | Day 3–5 | Catheter, dysuria, loin pain | Dipstick, urine culture |
| **Wind** (chest) | Day 1–2 | Cough, crackles, GA or aspiration | SpO₂; chest X-ray if available |
| **Walking** (VTE) | Day 5 onwards | Calf swelling, breathlessness, tachycardia | Doppler; treat if likely |
| **Weaning** (breasts) | Day 3 onwards | Engorged, red, tender breast | Clinical |
| **Wonder drugs / drip** | Any | Cannula-site phlebitis, drug rash | Look at every cannula site |
| **Malaria** (Uganda) | Any | Rigors, no other focus | **RDT or smear** |

On examination she has **offensive lochia, a tender bulky uterus** and a **red, indurated wound with a small pus leak**. *Interpretation:* **endometritis plus a superficial incisional SSI**. *Plan:* sepsis screen (FBC, blood cultures, pus swab, RDT), then **IV ampicillin 2 g 6-hourly + gentamicin 5 mg/kg daily + metronidazole 500 mg 8-hourly** (or the unit's ceftriaxone + metronidazole), **open the wound at the point of discharge** (remove 1–2 skin sutures) to drain pus, daily dressing, fluids and paracetamol, and **review the culture**. If there is no improvement after **48–72 h**, look for a **pelvic or subsheath collection** (ultrasound) or **retained products**, and ask whether the organism is resistant. If several women on the ward have similar infections, **tell the IPC focal person** (see the IPC topic).

> [!EXAM]
> **SSI classification (CDC/WHO surveillance):** infection **within 30 days** of surgery. **Superficial incisional** = skin and fat; **deep incisional** = fascia and muscle; **organ/space** = uterus (endometritis), pelvic abscess. A deep or organ/space SSI with a fever that will not settle usually needs **drainage**, not just "stronger antibiotics".

### Emergency: suspected uterine rupture (algorithm)

1. **Call for help**: senior obstetrician, anaesthetist, theatre team, lab for blood. **Category 1 CS / laparotomy.**
2. **Stop oxytocin.** Position left lateral. Give oxygen 10–15 L/min by face mask.
3. **Two 16 G cannulae**; send Hb and **cross-match 4 units**; bedside clotting test.
4. **Fluids**: warm crystalloid (RL/NS) 1–2 L rapidly, then **blood** as soon as available. Aim for SBP ≥90 and urine output ≥30 mL/h. Avoid over-infusing crystalloid; permissive resuscitation until the bleeding is controlled.
5. **Catheterise** (haematuria suggests bladder involvement).
6. **Antibiotics**: ampicillin 2 g IV + metronidazole 500 mg IV + gentamicin 5 mg/kg IV (or ceftriaxone 2 g IV + metronidazole), then continue as a treatment course.
7. **Laparotomy through a midline incision**: deliver the baby and placenta, find the bleeding points, and clamp.
8. **Repair vs hysterectomy**:
   - **Repair** (two layers) if the tear is clean, not too extensive, she is stable, and she wants more children. Strongly offer **tubal ligation** or counsel that any future pregnancy needs an elective CS at 36–37 weeks.
   - **Subtotal (or total) hysterectomy** if the tear is extensive, involves the uterine vessels or cervix, the tissue is infected or necrotic, or she is unstable. Life comes before fertility.
9. **Inspect the bladder** (repair in 2 layers, catheter for 10–14 days) and the ureters.
10. Post-op: HDU-level monitoring, transfusion, antibiotics, thromboprophylaxis once bleeding has stopped, counselling and bereavement support, and **an MPDSR review** for a maternal or perinatal death or near miss.

## 9. Documentation and Presenting Findings

**Model pre-op summary line:** "Mrs B.K., 26, G2P1 at 39+4 weeks with **one previous LSCS for breech**, in labour for 14 h, now **8 cm for 4 hours with the head 3/5 palpable, moulding +3 and haematuria**, FHR 150. Impression: **obstructed labour with a scarred uterus, no signs of rupture yet**. Plan: **Category 2 emergency LSCS**."

**Problem list:** (1) obstructed labour; (2) previous CS (rupture risk); (3) haematuria (bladder at risk, long catheter afterwards); (4) dehydration and ketosis; (5) infection risk (long labour, many VEs).

**Documenting the decision**: indication in one line, absolute or relative, urgency category, **time of decision**, the FHR described (rate, pattern, relation to contractions, duration), not just "fetal distress".

**The operation note must include**: indication, category, times, anaesthesia, **incision type (skin and uterus)**, findings, complications, **number of layers of uterine closure**, EBL, counts, and the **plan for the next pregnancy**. A J or inverted-T extension must be documented because it changes all future births.

**Discharge documentation**: the uterine incision type and the next-birth plan on her card and **Mother–Child Health Passport** (a classical scar in bold: elective CS at 36–37 weeks, never labour at home).

**Model post-operative presentation**

> "Mrs A.N., 24-year-old **P2+0**, day **1** after an **emergency lower-segment CS**, **Category 2**, done yesterday at 18:00 under **spinal** for **obstructed labour** (Robson group 3). Intra-operatively the lower segment was thin, the bladder was high but intact, there was a live male baby weighing 3.6 kg with Apgars 7 and 9, the uterus was closed in 2 layers, and EBL was 700 mL. She received ceftriaxone 2 g and metronidazole 500 mg IV at induction. This morning she has mild wound pain, is tolerating oral fluids, and has not yet mobilised. Pulse 88, BP 118/72, temp 37.2 °C, not pale, uterus well contracted at the umbilicus, lochia normal, wound dry, calves soft, and urine clear at 60 mL/h (catheter in, because of prolonged labour with haematuria). **Plan**: regular paracetamol and diclofenac, mobilise today, enoxaparin 40 mg SC daily, **keep the catheter for 7 days** in view of the obstructed labour with haematuria, Hb check tomorrow, breastfeeding support, and counselling on the reason for CS and the next birth plan."

**The post-CS review checklist ("head to toe, then the 6 Ps")**: **P**ulse/BP/temp, **P**ain, **P**allor, **P**ee (urine output, catheter), **P**ad (lochia), **P**ins (calves), plus the fundus, bowel sounds and distension, the wound, breasts and baby, mobilisation, and the drug chart.

## 11. Key Points

### High-yield points

- Two questions for every CS: **is it indicated? how urgent is it?** Say the category out loud.
- **Category 1 within 30 min; Category 2 within 75 min.**
- **Robson** audits CS rates (Group 5 usually biggest); it does not decide for one woman.
- **Antibiotic before incision**, single dose; prolonged "prophylaxis" breeds resistance.
- **Check the FHR in theatre** and re-examine a fully dilated woman.
- **Lower-segment transverse** is standard; a **classical scar** means elective CS at 36–37 weeks in every future pregnancy.
- **Spinal is the anaesthetic of choice**; not with platelets <75–80 or coagulopathy or uncorrected shock.
- **Abnormal FHR is the earliest sign of rupture** in a woman with a scar.
- **ERAS**: eat, mobilise, catheter out early (but 7–14 days after bladder repair or obstruction with haematuria), thromboprophylaxis.
- **Every woman leaves with a plan for her next birth.**

### Exam traps

- **"Give the antibiotic after cord clamping to protect the baby."** Wrong. Give it **before skin incision**; it reduces endometritis and wound infection without harming the baby.
- **"A Pfannenstiel scar means she had a lower-segment CS."** Not necessarily. The skin scar does not tell you the uterine incision: **find the operation note**.
- **"A new abnormal FHR in a woman with a scar is fetal distress."** Think **uterine rupture** first; it is the earliest sign.
- **"Five days of ceftriaxone prevents wound infection."** It does not. Prophylaxis is **one dose**; prolonged courses breed resistance. Treat established infection with a proper course.
- **"Nil by mouth until bowel sounds return."** Outdated. After an uncomplicated CS she can **drink within hours and eat when hungry**.
- **"The category depends on how soon theatre is free."** No. The category describes the **clinical state** of the mother and baby.
- **"Do a CS for an IUFD with a cephalic presentation."** Usually wrong. A **vaginal birth** is safer for her unless there is praevia, transverse lie with obstruction, rupture or another absolute indication.
- **"Induce a woman with a scar with misoprostol."** Contraindicated at term. Use a **Foley catheter**, then ARM and cautious oxytocin at a referral hospital.

### Questions seniors ask

**Viva questions**

1. **Define Category 1 CS and give its decision-to-delivery interval.** An immediate threat to the life of the woman or fetus; deliver as soon as possible, within 30 minutes.
2. **Why give antibiotics before incision rather than after cord clamping?** Tissue levels must be present when contamination occurs. Pre-incision dosing reduces endometritis and wound infection without harming the baby.
3. **What are the Robson groups used for?** A standard, prospective audit of CS rates by 10 mutually exclusive groups, to find which groups drive the rate and to compare facilities.
4. **List 4 indications for a classical CS.** Very preterm with no lower segment; transverse lie back-down with ruptured membranes; large lower-segment fibroid or dense adhesions; anterior praevia/accreta; cervical cancer; perimortem CS.
5. **What are the chances of VBAC success and rupture?** Success is 72–75% (85–90% with a prior vaginal birth). Rupture is about 0.5%, higher with induction or augmentation.
6. **The earliest sign of scar rupture?** An abnormal fetal heart rate (bradycardia or decelerations).
7. **Why is the bladder at risk in CS for obstructed labour?** The bladder is drawn up with the stretched lower segment and is oedematous, so open the peritoneum high and push the bladder down carefully.
8. **When do you remove the catheter after CS?** Once mobile and ≥12 h after the spinal. Keep it 7–14 days after bladder repair or prolonged obstructed labour with haematuria.
9. **Why not close the subcutaneous fat routinely?** It is only beneficial if it is more than 2 cm thick, where it reduces wound separation.
10. **What is the commonest complication of spinal anaesthesia at CS, and how do you manage it?** Hypotension. Left tilt, IV fluids, phenylephrine or ephedrine.

**On the ward round**

1. **What must you check in theatre just before the incision?** The **fetal heart** (the baby may have died or recovered), and a **repeat VE if she was fully dilated** (the head may now be low enough for a ventouse). Confirm the antibiotic has been given at the Time out.
2. **What are the contraindications to a spinal?** Refusal, **coagulopathy** or platelets below about 75–80, **uncorrected hypovolaemia**, infection at the site, raised intracranial pressure, and some fixed-output cardiac lesions.
3. **It is day 4 after an emergency CS and she has a fever of 38.6 °C. How do you work it up?** Go through the "W" list (womb, wound, water, wind, walking, weaning/breasts, wonder drugs/drip) plus **malaria**. Examine the uterus, lochia, wound, chest, calves, breasts and cannula sites. Send FBC, **blood and pus cultures before antibiotics**, urine dipstick/culture and an RDT. Start therapeutic antibiotics and **drain any pus**.
4. **What category is obstructed labour with a normal FHR?** **Category 2** (compromise, not immediately life-threatening): deliver within 75 min. With fetal bradycardia or signs of rupture it becomes Category 1.
5. **When would you keep the catheter in longer than usual?** After a **bladder repair** or **prolonged obstructed labour with haematuria**: **7–14 days**, to prevent a vesicovaginal fistula.
6. **What should be written in the operation note?** Indication, category, times, anaesthesia, **incision type (skin and uterus)**, findings, complications, **number of layers of uterine closure**, EBL, counts, and the **plan for the next pregnancy**.
7. **What bloods do you send before an emergency CS?** **Hb and group & cross-match** for all; HIV test if unknown; add platelets, creatinine, LFTs and clotting (or a bedside clotting test) for PE, abruption, sepsis or IUFD; RDT if febrile.

### Mnemonics

- **Urgency 1-2-3-4 = "Life – Compromise – Early – Elective"**; the times to remember are **30 and 75 minutes**.
- **Robson "1-2 first-timers, 3-4 multips, 5 scar, 6-7 breech, 8 twins, 9 lie, 10 preterm."**
- **Pre-op "CABIN-FT"**: **C**onsent, **A**ntibiotics before incision, **B**lood (Hb and cross-match), **I**V lines ×2, **N**il by mouth/antacid, **F**oley catheter, **T**ilt (and **T**heatre FHR check).
- **Layers to open ("Some Fat Sheaths Rarely Protect Uteri")**: Skin, Fat, Sheath, Recti (separate), Peritoneum, Uterus (after the uterovesical fold and bladder).
- **Signs of rupture ("FHR FIRST")**: **F**etal heart abnormal, **H**ypotension/tachycardia, **R**eceding presenting part, **F**etal parts palpable, **I**ncreasing constant pain, **R**ed urine (haematuria), **S**top in contractions, **T**ip of shoulder pain.
- **TOLAC candidate "ONE-LOW-HEAD-HOSPITAL"**: **one** previous CS, **low** transverse incision, cephalic **head**, delivering in a **hospital** (or HC IV) with a theatre.
- **Post-CS fever "W" list**: Womb, Wound, Water, Wind, Walking, Weaning (breasts), Wonder drugs/drip, plus malaria.
- **Post-CS review "6 Ps"**: Pulse, Pain, Pallor, Pee, Pad, Pins.

### OSCE tips

> [!EXAM]
> OSCE stations on CS usually test **consent** (explain indication, procedure, risks and alternatives; check understanding; allow questions), **post-op review**, or **counselling for VBAC**. Always start with an introduction, confirm identity, **explain the reason** in plain language, avoid jargon, give numbers ("about 1 in 200"), and close by checking understanding and next steps.

- **VBAC counselling**: candidate criteria, 72–75% success (85–90% with a prior vaginal birth), rupture about 1 in 200 (2–3 times higher with induction or augmentation), about 1 in 4 need an emergency CS, deliver at a facility with theatre and blood, no misoprostol.
- Use the OSCE checklists in section 10.
