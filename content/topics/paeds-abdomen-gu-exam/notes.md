## 1. Purpose and Indications

The paediatric abdominal and genito-urinary (GU) examination is the **adult examination adapted to a frightened child**. You use the same sequence (inspect, palpate, percuss, auscultate), but you must:

- **Gain trust first** (play, warm hands, examine on the mother's lap if needed), because a crying child tenses the abdomen and you will feel nothing.
- **Know age-specific normals**: a protuberant abdomen, a palpable liver edge and a non-retractile foreskin are normal in young children.
- **Always examine the groins and genitalia** (hernias, undescended testes, torsion, hypospadias) and **complete** the examination with **growth, blood pressure and a urine dipstick**.
- **Protect the child**: chaperone, privacy, consent and assent, and no internal examination of a prepubertal child.

In Uganda the findings carry big diagnoses: **hepatosplenomegaly** (malaria, schistosomiasis, sickle cell disease, HIV, Burkitt lymphoma), **abdominal masses** (Burkitt, Wilms, neuroblastoma), **ascites** (nephrotic syndrome, endomyocardial fibrosis, TB) and **the acute scrotum**.

> [!PEARL]
> The one thing never to forget: **watch the child's face, not your hand.** A grimace or a pause in crying tells you where the tenderness is far better than a 3-year-old's words. And if a boy has abdominal pain, **look at the testes**: torsion often presents as "tummy ache".

**Indications**

- Abdominal pain, vomiting, diarrhoea, constipation, abdominal distension or mass.
- Jaundice, pallor, fever without a focus (spleen, liver, kidney tenderness), failure to thrive, oedema (ascites).
- GU symptoms: dysuria, haematuria, enuresis, poor stream.
- Scrotal or groin swelling or pain, ambiguous genitalia, undescended testes, pubertal concerns.
- Suspected abuse (examination by trained seniors).
- Every **routine full paediatric examination** and newborn check.

## 2. Contraindications and Precautions

There is no absolute contraindication to examining a child's abdomen, but the examination is **modified or deferred** in these situations:

- **Shock, airway compromise, convulsions**: resuscitate first (ETAT); a full abdominal examination waits. A shocked child with a distended abdomen needs **resuscitation and a surgeon**, not a long examination.
- **Suspected Wilms tumour or neuroblastoma**: **palpate gently and once** (one examiner); repeated vigorous palpation risks tumour rupture and spread.
- **Suspected sexual abuse**: inspect only what is needed for immediate care; the **forensic examination is done once by a trained clinician** with a chaperone and documentation.
- **Distressed child**: pause, play, examine on the mother's lap, or come back after analgesia or when asleep (a sleeping infant is the best abdomen to palpate).
- **Digital rectal examination**: **not routine** in children; only on a senior's indication, with the little finger in infants.
- **Prepubertal girls**: **inspection only**; never a speculum or digital vaginal examination on the ward.
- **Foreskin**: never forcibly retract.
- **Rebound tenderness**: do not elicit it deliberately; use cough, hop or percussion tenderness instead.

**Pain relief early**: analgesia does **not** mask surgical signs. Give paracetamol **15 mg/kg** orally or IV; morphine **0.1 mg/kg IV**, titrated, for severe pain, by a senior.

## 3. Relevant Anatomy and Normal Values

| Feature | Normal in children | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| **Abdominal shape** | Toddlers have a **protuberant abdomen** with lumbar lordosis ("pot belly") when standing; flattens by school age | Do not call it distension unless tense, shiny, or with dilated veins or fluid |
| **Breathing** | Infants are **abdominal (diaphragmatic) breathers** | A still abdomen in a breathing infant suggests **peritonitis** |
| **Liver** | Relatively large; **edge palpable 1–2 cm** below the right costal margin in infants and toddlers (up to about 3 cm in newborns), soft and sharp | Measure the **span by percussion**; a firm, nodular or > 2 cm liver is abnormal (hyperinflation from bronchiolitis or asthma pushes a normal liver down) |
| **Liver span** (percussion, midclavicular line) | About **4.5–5 cm** in the first week of life; about **7–8 cm (boys)** and **6–6.5 cm (girls)** by 12 years | A large span confirms hepatomegaly |
| **Spleen** | Tip palpable in some **healthy neonates and young infants**; otherwise not palpable | Palpable spleen beyond infancy = splenomegaly until proven otherwise |
| **Kidneys** | **Lower poles may be felt** in neonates; rarely felt later | Enlarged kidney: hydronephrosis, Wilms, polycystic disease |
| **Bladder** | **Abdominal organ in infants** (palpable or percussible above the pubis when full) | A persistently palpable bladder in a boy with a poor stream = **posterior urethral valves** |
| **Umbilicus** | **Umbilical hernia is common** in African infants; most close spontaneously by 4–5 years | Reassure; incarceration is rare |
| **Recti** | **Divarication of recti** is normal in infants | Not a hernia |
| **Testes** | Descend by birth in term boys; may complete descent by **3 months**; the **cremasteric reflex** makes them **retractile** from about 6 months to puberty | Examine warm and relaxed; distinguish retractile from undescended |
| **Foreskin** | Non-retractile at birth; retractable in about **10% at 1 year, 50% at 10 years, 99% by 17 years** | Never forcibly retract; ballooning on voiding is normal |
| **Penis** | Micropenis = stretched length **< 2.5 cm** at term | Ambiguous genitalia work-up |
| **Female genitalia** | Newborns may have **swollen labia, white discharge or a small withdrawal bleed** from maternal oestrogen; the prepubertal hymen is thin and sensitive | Reassure; prepubertal girls: inspection only |
| **Abdominal aorta** | Pulsation is normal in thin children | Not an aneurysm |

**Respiratory rate context** (lower-lobe pneumonia presenting as abdominal pain): fast breathing is ≥ 60 (< 2 months), ≥ 50 (2–12 months), ≥ 40 (1–5 years).

### Hackett grading of splenomegaly (malaria surveys)

| Grade | Spleen tip position (child supine, along the line toward the umbilicus) |
|---|---|
| **0** | Not palpable, even on deep inspiration |
| **1** | Palpable only on deep inspiration |
| **2** | Palpable on normal breathing, but not beyond a horizontal line **halfway between the costal margin and the umbilicus** |
| **3** | Beyond that line but **not below the umbilicus** |
| **4** | **Below the umbilicus** but not beyond a line halfway between the umbilicus and the pubic symphysis |
| **5** | Beyond that line (toward the pubis or right iliac fossa) |

On the ward, also record the size in **cm below the left costal margin**, measured along the line toward the right iliac fossa (the axis of splenic enlargement).

### Tanner (sexual maturity) stages, simplified

| Stage | Boys: genitalia | Girls: breasts | Pubic hair (both) |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1** | Prepubertal; testes < 4 mL | Prepubertal (papilla only) | None |
| **2** | **Testes ≥ 4 mL** (first sign), scrotum reddens and thins | **Breast bud** (first sign) | Sparse, long, slightly pigmented at the base of the penis or labia |
| **3** | Penis lengthens | Breast and areola enlarge, no separation of contour | Darker, coarser, curled, spreading over the pubis |
| **4** | Penis broadens, glans develops; testes 10–15 mL | Areola and papilla form a **secondary mound** | Adult type, not on the thighs |
| **5** | Adult; testes 15–25 mL | Adult contour | Adult, spreading to the medial thighs |

Puberty starts at **8–13 years in girls** (breast buds; menarche about 2–2.5 years later) and **9–14 years in boys** (testicular enlargement). **Precocious**: before 8 (girls) or 9 (boys). **Delayed**: no signs by 13 (girls) or 14 (boys).

## 4. Equipment and Preparation

### Equipment

| Item | Use |
|---|---|
| Hand hygiene (soap or alcohol rub), **warm hands** | Infection control; a relaxed abdomen |
| **Toys, a light, a pen torch** | Distraction; **transillumination** of scrotal swellings |
| **Tape measure** (non-stretch) | Abdominal girth (ascites), liver and spleen size in cm, head circumference |
| **Weighing scale, length board or stadiometer, MUAC tape** | Growth and nutrition (part of every GI examination) |
| **Paediatric BP cuff** (bladder 80–100% of arm circumference, width ≥ 40%) | Hypertension (AGN, Wilms, renal disease, coarctation) |
| **Stethoscope** (paediatric diaphragm) | Bowel sounds, bruits |
| **Gloves, lubricant** | Genital and perianal inspection; rarely rectal examination (senior) |
| **Urine dipstick and specimen pot** | Haematuria, proteinuria, glucose, nitrites and leucocytes |
| **Orchidometer** (Prader beads), Tanner charts | Testicular volume and pubertal staging (adolescents) |
| **WHO growth charts**, child health card | Plot and interpret |
| **Chaperone** (nurse or parent) | Mandatory for genital and breast examination |

### First 5 minutes: is the child sick?

1. **Quick ETAT look**: airway, breathing, circulation (capillary refill, pulse), consciousness, convulsions, severe dehydration. Emergency signs come first (section 2).
2. **Pain relief early** (section 2).
3. **Privacy and warmth**: screens, a warm room, keep the child covered where possible.

### Focused history (what to ask before you examine)

| Ask | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| **Main complaint and duration** | Acute (surgical?) versus chronic (organomegaly, mass) |
| **Pain**: site, onset, character, colicky versus constant, radiation, relation to meals, stools, urine | Colicky + pallor + red-currant jelly = intussusception; RIF + fever = appendicitis; loin + fever = pyelonephritis |
| **Vomiting** (bilious? projectile?) | Bilious = obstruction (malrotation and volvulus); projectile non-bilious at 2–8 weeks = pyloric stenosis |
| **Stools**: diarrhoea, blood, constipation, worms passed | Dysentery, intussusception, constipation (faecal mass), Ascaris obstruction |
| **Urine**: dysuria, frequency, colour (cola = haematuria; tea = conjugated bilirubin; red = haemoglobinuria or blackwater), stream, enuresis, oliguria | UTI, AGN, posterior urethral valves, malaria haemoglobinuria |
| **Swelling**: abdomen (gradual versus rapid), face and feet (oedema), groin or scrotum (comes and goes with crying?) | Ascites or nephrotic syndrome, Burkitt (rapid), hernia (intermittent) |
| **Jaundice, pallor, fever, weight loss, night sweats** | Liver disease, haemolysis (SCD), malignancy, TB |
| **Bleeding, bruising, bone pain** | Leukaemia |
| **Diet, growth, development, feeding** | Malnutrition, chronic disease |
| **Past history**: SCD, malaria episodes, HIV status, TB contact, previous surgery, neonatal course (umbilical catheter) | Causes of organomegaly; portal vein thrombosis and portal hypertension; adhesions after surgery |
| **Residence and water contact** (lake shores, rivers; Karamoja) | **Schistosomiasis**, **visceral leishmaniasis** |
| **Immunisation** (UNEPI) and **family history** (SCD, polycystic kidneys) | Hepatitis B, hereditary causes |
| **Genital concerns**: undescended testes noticed at birth, swelling, circumcision plans, ambiguous genitalia, discharge, pubertal timing, possible abuse (behaviour change, bleeding, discharge in a young girl) | Timing of surgery; hypospadias before circumcision; safeguarding |

### Preparation checklist

- **Consent and assent**: verbal consent from the parent and **assent** from the child in words the child understands; explain specifically **before** examining the genitalia.
- **Chaperone** for genital examination (always), and for any examination of an adolescent. Record the chaperone's name.
- **Positioning and exposure** by age are in section 5, step A.

## 5. Procedure Step by Step

The sequence follows Hutchison's Clinical Methods (24th edition), adapted for children: be **opportunistic** (observe first, palpate while the child is calm, auscultate while quiet, leave distressing parts to last), use play and distraction, and judge each finding against **age-specific normals** (section 3).

**A. Introduction, consent and preparation**

1. **Introduce yourself** to the parent and child, confirm the child's name and age, and explain what you will do in simple words.
2. **Consent** from the parent, **assent** from the child; **offer a chaperone** (mandatory before the genitalia).
3. **Hand hygiene**; **warm your hands** (rub them, or warm them under the armpit).
4. **Position**:
   - **Infant**: supine on the couch or on the **mother's lap** (lying across her knees), possibly while breastfeeding or with a dummy or toy.
   - **Toddler**: on the mother's lap facing you, or supine with the mother at the head end holding hands; **distract** (bubbles, toy, "count the buttons").
   - **Older child or adolescent**: supine, **one pillow**, arms by the sides, **knees slightly flexed** to relax the abdomen.
5. **Exposure**: from the **nipples to the knees** for a full inspection of abdomen, groins and genitalia, but in practice **expose the abdomen first** and the genitalia **only when you examine them** (dignity; older children keep underwear on until then).
6. **Kneel or sit** so your eyes are at the level of the abdomen.

**B. General inspection from the end of the bed**

1. **Well or unwell**; comfortable, **lying still** (peritonitis) or **writhing** (colic); alert; interaction.
2. **Colour**: pallor, jaundice, cyanosis.
3. **Hydration**: dry mouth, sunken eyes, skin pinch.
4. **Nutrition**: wasting, oedema; **dysmorphism**.
5. **Vital signs**: temperature, heart rate, respiratory rate, capillary refill, **BP with the correct cuff**.
6. **Growth**: weight, length or height, head circumference (< 2 years), **MUAC**; **plot Z-scores**.

**C. Hands**

- Clubbing (chronic liver disease, IBD, cystic fibrosis, cyanotic heart disease), koilonychia, leukonychia (hypoalbuminaemia), palmar erythema, **pallor of the palmar creases**.

**D. Face, eyes and mouth**

1. **Eyes**: **pallor** (conjunctivae), **jaundice** (sclerae, in daylight), periorbital puffiness (nephrotic syndrome, AGN), raccoon eyes (neuroblastoma).
2. **Face**: frontal bossing and maxillary overgrowth (SCD), **jaw swelling** (Burkitt).
3. **Mouth**: thrush (HIV), ulcers, angular stomatitis, dental caries, **parotid enlargement** (HIV).

**E. Neck, nodes, skin and oedema**

1. **Lymph nodes**: cervical, axillary and inguinal lymphadenopathy; **JVP** in older children.
2. **Skin**: spider naevi, bruising or petechiae (leukaemia, liver disease), scratch marks (cholestasis), rashes (HSP on buttocks and legs).
3. **Oedema**: feet, sacrum, face.

**F. Abdomen: inspection** (stand back, then at the level of the abdomen)

1. **Shape**: flat, scaphoid (diaphragmatic hernia in a newborn; dehydration), protuberant (normal toddler), **distended** (the 5 Fs: fluid, flatus, faeces, fat, fetus in adolescents; plus **organomegaly and masses**).
2. **Movement with breathing**: moves freely, or held still (peritonitis).
3. **Visible peristalsis**: waves from the **left upper quadrant to the right** in pyloric stenosis (watch after a test feed); generalised in obstruction.
4. **Skin**: scars, striae (steroids, Cushing), **dilated veins** (portal hypertension: flow away from the umbilicus, caput medusae; IVC obstruction: flow upward), bruising (trauma, abuse), cutaneous signs.
5. **Umbilicus**: hernia (size of the fascial defect), granuloma or discharge (newborn), everted (ascites).
6. **Visible masses**, asymmetry (Wilms bulge in the flank), **divarication of recti** (normal in infants).
7. **Groins**: swelling at rest and when the child cries or coughs.

**G. Abdomen: palpation**

1. **Ask "where does it hurt?"** (point with one finger); start in the **quadrant farthest from the pain**.
2. **Light palpation** of all 9 regions with the flat of the hand, **watching the face**: tenderness, guarding (voluntary versus involuntary), rigidity. In young children, **palpate with the child's own hand under yours**, or palpate with the stethoscope while "listening".
3. **Deep palpation** of all 9 regions: masses, faecal loading (left iliac fossa, indentable).
4. **Peritonism**: avoid deliberate rebound; instead ask the child to **cough, hop or jump** (pain = peritonism), or tap the heel; **percussion tenderness** is gentler.
5. **Liver**: start in the **right iliac fossa** (a large liver starts low), fingers pointing upward or the radial border of the index finger parallel to the costal margin; move up 1–2 cm with each breath until the edge meets your hand. Describe: **cm below the costal margin in the midclavicular line**, edge (sharp or rounded), surface (smooth or nodular), consistency (soft, firm, hard), tenderness, pulsatility (tricuspid regurgitation). In infants, use a **light** touch: the edge is soft and easily missed.
6. **Spleen**: start in the **right iliac fossa**, move diagonally toward the **left hypochondrium** with each breath; if not felt, roll the child **toward you onto the right side** and palpate again with your left hand supporting the left lower ribs. Describe size (cm along its axis, Hackett grade), notch, consistency, tenderness.
7. **Kidneys**: **bimanual (ballotting)**: one hand behind the loin, one in front; push up with the posterior hand to "bounce" the kidney against the anterior hand. Compare sides.
8. **Bladder**: suprapubic mass that is dull and that you cannot get below; recheck after voiding.
9. **Any mass**: **site, size (cm), shape, surface, edge, consistency, tenderness, mobility (with breathing, side to side), can you get above or below it, does it cross the midline, percussion note, pulsatility, bruit, transillumination**.
10. **Abdominal aorta**: pulsation is normal in thin children.

**H. Abdomen: percussion**

1. **Liver span**: percuss down the right midclavicular line from the chest (resonant) to the upper border of dullness, and up from the abdomen to the lower border; measure the span in cm.
2. **Spleen**: percuss **Traube's space** (left lower anterior chest) and the left flank; dullness supports splenomegaly.
3. **Masses and bladder**: dull (solid organ, full bladder) versus resonant (bowel in front, for example a kidney).
4. **Ascites**: **shifting dullness** (percuss from the midline to the flank until dull, keep a finger there, roll the child away from you, wait 30 seconds, and percuss again: the dull area becomes resonant). **Fluid thrill** only in tense, large ascites (needs an assistant to place the edge of a hand on the midline). Measure **abdominal girth** at the umbilicus daily.

**I. Abdomen: auscultation**

1. **Bowel sounds**: listen for up to 1–2 minutes beside the umbilicus. **Absent** (ileus, peritonitis), **hyperactive** (gastroenteritis), **high-pitched tinkling** (obstruction).
2. **Bruits**: renal (over the flanks or above the umbilicus) in a hypertensive child; hepatic (rare).
3. **Succussion splash** (gastric outlet obstruction): gently rock the child.

**J. Groins and femoral pulses**

1. Inspect the **inguinal regions** with the child standing (older child) or crying (infant) for a swelling.
2. **Hernia**: swelling in the groin or scrotum, **cannot get above it**, cough or cry **impulse**, usually reducible (gently, toward the internal ring).
3. **Femoral pulses**: compare with the right brachial or radial (**radio-femoral delay** or weak femorals = **coarctation of the aorta**).
4. **Inguinal lymph nodes**.

**K. Male genitalia** (chaperone, warm hands; a toddler sitting **cross-legged ("frog" position)** or squatting relaxes the cremaster)

1. **Penis**: size (micropenis: stretched length < 2.5 cm at term), position of the **urethral meatus** (ventral = **hypospadias**, dorsal = epispadias), **chordee** (ventral curvature), **hooded foreskin** (dorsal hood with hypospadias), circumcision status, inflammation (balanitis), **paraphimosis** (retracted, swollen foreskin trapped behind the glans).
2. **Foreskin**: **do not retract**; note ballooning, scarring of the tip (true phimosis = scarred white ring, balanitis xerotica obliterans), discharge.
3. **Scrotum**: size, symmetry, rugae (underdeveloped on the side of an undescended testis), colour (red or blue in torsion), swelling.
4. **Testes**:
   - Before touching the scrotum, place your fingers at the **inguinal canal** and **"milk" downward** toward the scrotum (prevents the testis retracting upward).
   - Palpate each testis between thumb and fingers: **present?**, size (orchidometer in adolescents), consistency, tenderness, **lie** (vertical normal; horizontal and high-riding in torsion), epididymis (posterolateral), a **"blue dot"** at the upper pole (torsion of the appendix testis).
   - **Retractile** testis: can be brought down to the bottom of the scrotum and **stays there** briefly: normal. **Undescended**: cannot be brought into the scrotum (or springs straight back). **Impalpable**: not felt anywhere (abdominal, absent).
   - **Cremasteric reflex** (stroke the inner thigh; the ipsilateral testis rises): **absent in torsion**.
5. **Scrotal swelling**: can you **get above it**? **Transilluminate** with a torch in a darkened room (hydrocele glows; hernia, tumour and haematoma do not, although infant bowel can transmit some light). Is the testis separate from it?

**L. Female genitalia** (inspection only; chaperone; parent present)

1. Position: **supine "frog-leg"** (soles of the feet together, knees apart) on the couch or the **mother's lap**; or knee–chest for a brief look in an older child.
2. **Gentle labial separation** (fingers on the labia majora, drawing slightly **outward and downward**) to see the introitus.
3. Look for: **labial adhesions** (fused labia minora with a thin midline line), **clitoromegaly** (CAH), discharge (vulvovaginitis: poor hygiene, threadworms, foreign body; gonorrhoea or trichomonas = abuse until proven otherwise), bleeding, erythema, **injury**, warts, an imperforate hymen (bulging bluish membrane in a pubertal girl with cyclical pain and amenorrhoea).
4. **Never** use a speculum or do a digital vaginal examination in a prepubertal girl on the ward.

**M. Anus and perineum** (inspection)

1. Gently part the buttocks: **fissure** (constipation), **rectal prolapse** (*Shigella*, *Trichuris*, cystic fibrosis), **threadworms**, perianal excoriation, warts, abscess, and **injury** (abuse); in a newborn, **patency and position** of the anus (imperforate anus, fistula).
2. **Digital rectal examination**: only if specifically indicated (for example suspected Hirschsprung disease, intussusception, pelvic abscess), by or with a senior, using the **little finger** in infants.

**N. Completing the examination**

1. **Urine dipstick** (and microscopy and culture if abnormal) on a clean-catch or bag specimen.
2. **Blood pressure** with a correctly sized cuff (if not done in step B), compared with age and height centiles.
3. **Plot growth**; **Tanner stage** in adolescents.
4. Examine **other systems** as relevant: chest (lower-lobe pneumonia), heart (murmurs, failure), skin, joints; **ears and throat last** (distressing).
5. Cover the child, **thank** the child and parent, **wash hands**.
6. **Explain the findings** to the parent and child and **summarise** (section 9).

## 6. Findings and Interpretation

### Inspection and palpation signs

| Finding | What it means |
|---|---|
| Child lying still, abdomen not moving with breathing | Peritonitis |
| Writhing | Colic (intussusception, renal colic, obstruction) |
| Scaphoid abdomen | Congenital diaphragmatic hernia (newborn); dehydration |
| Distension (5 Fs + organomegaly or masses) | Tense, shiny, with dilated veins or fluid = true distension; a soft pot belly in a toddler is normal |
| Visible peristalsis LUQ → right after a test feed | Pyloric stenosis |
| Dilated veins, flow away from the umbilicus | Portal hypertension (schistosomiasis, portal vein thrombosis) |
| Dilated veins, flow upward | IVC obstruction |
| Everted umbilicus | Ascites |
| Bruising on the abdomen | Trauma or abuse |
| Involuntary guarding, rigidity, pain on cough or hop | Peritonism (perforated appendix, typhoid perforation) |
| Indentable mass in the left iliac fossa | Faeces (constipation) |
| Suprapubic dull mass you cannot get below, disappears after voiding | Full bladder; persistent in a boy with a poor stream = posterior urethral valves |
| Liver edge > 2 cm, firm, nodular or rounded, with an increased span | True hepatomegaly |
| Liver edge low but span normal | Displaced by hyperinflation (bronchiolitis, asthma) |
| Tender smooth liver + raised JVP, gallop, oedema | Heart failure |
| Pulsatile liver | Tricuspid regurgitation |
| Palpable spleen beyond infancy | Splenomegaly until proven otherwise |
| Shifting dullness, fluid thrill (tense) | Ascites |
| Absent bowel sounds | Ileus, peritonitis |
| High-pitched, tinkling bowel sounds | Obstruction |
| Succussion splash | Gastric outlet obstruction |

## 7. Clinical Acumen

### Red flags

> [!REDFLAG]
> - **Bilious (green) vomiting** in an infant: malrotation with volvulus until proven otherwise (surgical emergency).
> - **Acute scrotal pain or swelling**: torsion until proven otherwise; theatre within 6 hours.
> - **Irreducible, tender groin swelling**, vomiting, distension: incarcerated or strangulated hernia.
> - **Abdominal mass** in a child (Burkitt, Wilms, neuroblastoma): urgent referral; palpate gently.
> - **Rigid, silent abdomen**, guarding, rebound: peritonitis (perforated appendix, typhoid perforation).
> - **Massive splenomegaly with sudden pallor and shock** in a child with SCD: **splenic sequestration** (transfuse now).
> - **Hypertension with haematuria and oedema**: AGN with risk of encephalopathy.
> - **Genital injury, bleeding or discharge in a prepubertal child**: possible sexual abuse.
> - **Ambiguous genitalia with vomiting in a newborn**: salt-wasting CAH (check Na, K, glucose).

### Thinking like a clinician

- **Pain in the abdomen may come from elsewhere**: lower-lobe pneumonia, testicular torsion, DKA, sickle cell crisis, HSP, UTI, otitis and tonsillitis (mesenteric adenitis), and constipation.
- **A big liver is not always a big liver**: hyperinflated lungs (bronchiolitis, asthma) push it down; measure the span.
- **Tender, smooth hepatomegaly with a raised JVP, gallop and oedema = heart failure** (rheumatic heart disease, cardiomyopathy, severe anaemia).
- **Massive splenomegaly** in Uganda: hyperreactive malarial splenomegaly, schistosomiasis with portal hypertension, visceral leishmaniasis (Karamoja), chronic myeloid leukaemia (rare), and **SCD** (classically the spleen auto-infarcts by school age, but in malaria-endemic Uganda **persistent splenomegaly** in older children with SCD is common).
- **Ascites with a normal liver and heavy proteinuria = nephrotic syndrome**; with a **raised JVP and a small heart sound** think **endomyocardial fibrosis** (EMF, still seen in Uganda) or constrictive pericarditis (TB); with fever and weight loss think **TB peritonitis**.
- **Abdominal mass + jaw swelling in a 6-year-old** in Uganda = **Burkitt lymphoma** (fast-growing; tumour lysis risk).
- **A "hernia" that you can get above and that transilluminates is a hydrocele**; a scrotal swelling that you cannot get above is a hernia.

### Judgement calls

- **Crying child**: stop, play, use the mother's lap, palpate between cries during inspiration, while feeding or asleep, or after analgesia. A useless examination is worse than a delayed one.
- **When to stop and escalate**: emergency signs (ETAT), a suspected tumour (one gentle examination, then ultrasound and referral), suspected torsion (surgeon now, no ultrasound first), suspected abuse (stop unnecessary examination, call the senior).

### Common student mistakes

- Starting palpation where it hurts.
- Examining the abdomen standing above a crying child.
- Forgetting the groins and testes.
- Forcibly retracting a foreskin.
- Calling a normal infant liver "hepatomegaly".
- Missing a full bladder (the "mass" that disappears after voiding).
- Not measuring BP; not dipping the urine.

### Worked examples

> [!EXAM]
> **Worked example 1: spleen or kidney?**
> A 4-year-old has a left upper quadrant mass. It **moves down and toward the right iliac fossa** with inspiration, you **cannot get above it**, there is a **notch** on its medial border, it is **not ballotable**, and it is **dull** to percussion → **spleen**. If instead the mass were **ballotable**, you **could get above it**, it moved only slightly downward with breathing and had a **resonant band** over it (colon in front) → **kidney** (Wilms tumour, hydronephrosis).

> [!EXAM]
> **Worked example 2: is this liver really big?**
> A 5-month-old with bronchiolitis has a liver edge **3 cm** below the costal margin. Percussion: upper border of dullness at the **7th intercostal space** (low because of hyperinflation), span **5 cm** (normal for age). Interpretation: **displaced, not enlarged** liver. Contrast: a 5-month-old with a **tender liver 3 cm**, gallop, and RR 70 → **heart failure** (large VSD), where the liver span is increased and the edge rounded.

## 8. Complications and Troubleshooting

### Complications of the examination

| Situation | Risk | Prevention |
|---|---|---|
| Vigorous palpation of Wilms or neuroblastoma | Tumour rupture and spread | Gentle, single examiner |
| Forcible foreskin retraction | Tears, scarring, **pathological phimosis**, paraphimosis | Never retract |
| Missed testicular torsion ("tummy ache") | Loss of the testis | Examine the scrotum in every boy with abdominal pain |
| Missed incarcerated hernia | Bowel strangulation, testicular infarction | Examine the groins in every vomiting infant |
| Circumcision of a hypospadic boy | Loss of tissue for repair | Examine the meatus first |
| Rough or unexplained genital examination | Psychological harm; retraumatisation in abuse | Consent and assent, chaperone, inspection only |
| Over-diagnosis of hepatomegaly | Unnecessary tests | Measure the span |
| Cold hands, standing over the child | Crying, guarding, a useless examination | Warm hands, child's eye level, play |

### Managing what you find: organomegaly

| Finding | Likely causes (Uganda) | First steps |
|---|---|---|
| **Splenomegaly ± hepatomegaly with fever** | **Malaria** | RDT or smear; treat (ACT or artesunate per severity) |
| **Massive splenomegaly, recurrent malaria, high IgM** | **Hyperreactive malarial splenomegaly** | Long-term antimalarial prophylaxis per specialist; exclude lymphoma |
| **Firm hepatosplenomegaly, lakeside, portal hypertension** | **Schistosomiasis (*S. mansoni*)** | Stool Kato-Katz, ultrasound (periportal fibrosis); **praziquantel 40 mg/kg single dose**; watch for variceal bleeding |
| **Splenomegaly in an infant or young child with pallor, dactylitis, jaundice** | **Sickle cell disease** | Sickling or HbS test; folic acid, penicillin V prophylaxis, pneumococcal vaccine, malaria prevention; teach parents to **palpate the spleen daily** (sequestration) |
| **Acute splenic enlargement + sudden pallor + shock** in SCD | **Splenic sequestration** | **Emergency transfusion** (10 mL/kg packed cells, cautiously), fluids, senior |
| **Massive spleen, fever, wasting, pancytopenia** (Karamoja, north-east) | **Visceral leishmaniasis (kala-azar)** | rK39, refer to a treatment centre |
| **Hepatosplenomegaly + lymphadenopathy + bruising or bone pain** | **Leukaemia**, lymphoma | FBC and film, urgent referral (Uganda Cancer Institute) |
| **Hepatosplenomegaly + thrush, parotids, failure to thrive** | **HIV** (± TB, CMV) | HIV test or PCR, ART, TB screen |
| **Tender smooth hepatomegaly, raised JVP, gallop, oedema** | **Heart failure** (RHD, cardiomyopathy, severe anaemia, large shunts) | Oxygen, furosemide **1 mg/kg**, treat the cause; echo |
| **Tender hepatomegaly with fever** | Amoebic or pyogenic liver abscess, viral hepatitis, typhoid | Ultrasound; metronidazole for amoebic abscess |

### Managing what you find: abdominal masses

1. **Do not over-palpate** (tumour rupture); **one examiner**, gently.
2. **Measure BP** (Wilms and neuroblastoma can cause hypertension), dip the urine (haematuria in Wilms), check for **jaw or orbital masses** (Burkitt) and raccoon eyes (neuroblastoma).
3. **Ultrasound** first; then refer urgently.
4. **Burkitt lymphoma**: very fast growing; before chemotherapy start **hydration and allopurinol** (tumour lysis prevention); treatment at the **Uganda Cancer Institute** or regional centres, with an excellent response to chemotherapy.
5. **Faecal masses** (indentable, left iliac fossa, constipation history): disimpaction and laxatives (lactulose; see constipation guidance).
6. **Full bladder**: re-examine after voiding; a persistently distended bladder in a boy → **posterior urethral valves** (catheterise, urology).

### Managing what you find: ascites

- Confirm (shifting dullness, girth), then look for the cause: **urine protein** (nephrotic syndrome → steroids per the nephrotic syndrome topic), **JVP, heart sounds, echo** (EMF, constrictive pericarditis, heart failure), **liver and spleen** (portal hypertension), **TB screen** (ascitic tap: exudate, lymphocytes; Xpert), **albumin** (malnutrition, protein-losing states).
- Diagnostic **ascitic tap** by a senior, with ultrasound guidance where available.

### Managing what you find: groin and scrotum

| Finding | Management |
|---|---|
| **Reducible inguinal hernia** | **Refer for elective herniotomy soon** (within weeks; sooner in infants and preterms because of the incarceration risk). Teach parents the signs of incarceration |
| **Incarcerated (irreducible) hernia, no signs of strangulation** | Analgesia, position, **gentle sustained manual reduction (taxis)** by a senior; if reduced, herniotomy within 24–48 h; if not reducible → emergency surgery |
| **Strangulated hernia** (tender, red, vomiting, distension, unwell) | **Nil by mouth, IV fluids, analgesia, emergency surgery** |
| **Hydrocele** (transilluminates, can get above, testis normal) | **Observe**: most close by **1–2 years**; refer if it persists beyond 1–2 years, fluctuates in size (communicating), or is very large |
| **Testicular torsion suspected** | **Emergency**: analgesia, **nil by mouth**, **urgent surgical review and exploration** (do not delay for ultrasound); viability 80–100% if operated within **4–6 hours**; contralateral orchidopexy |
| **Torsion of the appendix testis** (blue dot, upper-pole tenderness, cremasteric reflex present) | Analgesia, rest, scrotal support; settles in about a week (if any doubt, explore) |
| **Epididymo-orchitis** | Urinalysis and culture; antibiotics per organism; in infants look for a urinary tract anomaly; in adolescents consider STI and safeguarding; mumps orchitis 4–6 days after parotitis |
| **Undescended testis** | Re-examine at **6–8 weeks** and **3 months**; refer if not in the scrotum by **6 months** for **orchidopexy at 6–12 months (by 18 months)**; bilateral impalpable testes in a newborn → urgent senior review (exclude **CAH or DSD**: electrolytes, karyotype) |
| **Retractile testis** | Reassure; review yearly until puberty (may "ascend") |
| **Umbilical hernia** | Reassure; most close by **4–5 years**; refer if persisting beyond about 4–5 years, a very large defect, or ever incarcerated; no coins or binders |

### Managing what you find: penis and foreskin

| Finding | Management |
|---|---|
| **Physiological non-retractile foreskin** | Reassure; **never forcibly retract**; clean the outside only |
| **Ballooning** on voiding | Normal; topical **0.05% betamethasone** to the preputial ring 3 times daily for 6–12 weeks only if problematic (RCH) |
| **Balanitis** | Warm salt-water soaks, barrier cream or 1% hydrocortisone, antifungal if candida; antibiotics only if cellulitis |
| **True (pathological) phimosis** (scarred, white, fibrotic ring; BXO) | Topical steroid trial; **circumcision** by surgeons if scarred or recurrent infections |
| **Paraphimosis** | **Emergency**: analgesia (local anaesthetic gel, penile block), compress the oedematous glans, and reduce the foreskin forward; surgical help if this fails |
| **Hypospadias** | **Do not circumcise** (the foreskin is used in the repair); refer to paediatric surgery or urology (repair usually **6–18 months**); if hypospadias with an **undescended testis**, check for **DSD or CAH** |
| **Micropenis or ambiguous genitalia** | Do not assign sex in a hurry; check **electrolytes and glucose** (salt-wasting CAH); urgent specialist referral |

> [!UGANDA]
> **Circumcision is common** in Uganda (Muslim families, cultural ceremonies such as *imbalu* among the Bagisu, and voluntary medical male circumcision for HIV prevention in adolescents). Before any neonatal or infant circumcision, **examine for hypospadias and chordee** and make sure both testes are descended. Many families seek surgery for a normal non-retractile foreskin; reassurance is the right treatment. Paediatric surgical services are at **Mulago, Mbarara, Gulu, Mengo, CoRSU** and other referral centres.

### Managing what you find: female genitalia

| Finding | Management |
|---|---|
| **Neonatal discharge or withdrawal bleed** | Reassure (maternal oestrogen) |
| **Labial adhesions** | Reassure if asymptomatic (most separate at puberty); **topical oestrogen cream** for 2–4 weeks if urinary symptoms; never forcibly separate |
| **Vulvovaginitis** (poor hygiene, threadworms, irritants) | Hygiene advice, cotton underwear, avoid soaps and bubble baths, treat threadworms (**albendazole 400 mg**, or 200 mg if under 2 years); persistent bloody discharge → foreign body, refer |
| **Clitoromegaly** | Senior review the same day; electrolytes (**CAH**) |
| **Imperforate hymen** (pubertal girl, cyclical pain, bulging bluish membrane) | Gynaecology referral for hymenotomy |
| **Suspected sexual abuse** | **Safety first**: inform the senior; **child protection** (probation and social welfare officer, police; Police Form 3 completed by an authorised medical officer); **HIV PEP within 72 hours**, emergency contraception (pubertal girls) within 120 h, STI prophylaxis, hepatitis B vaccination; forensic examination by a trained clinician; document exact words and findings; psychosocial support |

### Drug doses used in this topic

| Drug | Dose | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Paracetamol | **15 mg/kg** 6-hourly (max 60 mg/kg/day; max 1 g/dose), oral or IV | Analgesia does not mask surgical signs |
| Morphine | **0.1 mg/kg IV**, titrated | Severe pain; by a senior |
| Praziquantel | **40 mg/kg** single dose | *S. mansoni* |

## 9. Documentation and Presenting Findings

**Document these positives and negatives**

| Document these positives | And these negatives |
|---|---|
| "Liver 4 cm below RCM, span 9 cm, firm, non-tender, smooth" | "No ascites (no shifting dullness)" |
| "Spleen 6 cm along its axis, Hackett grade 3, firm, notch felt" | "Kidneys not ballotable" |
| "10 × 8 cm firm, smooth mass in the left flank, does not cross the midline, ballotable" | "Both testes descended, normal lie, non-tender" |
| "Right inguinal swelling, cannot get above, reducible, cough impulse" | "Foreskin not retracted (normal)"; "No hypospadias" |
| BP and centile; urine dipstick result | "No lymphadenopathy, no jaundice" |

Record the **chaperone's name**.

**Model one-line summary**

> "A 7-year-old boy from Buliisa (Lake Albert) with 6 months of abdominal swelling and occasional blood in the stool: well-grown (WAZ −1.2), pale, **firm hepatomegaly (left lobe prominent) 5 cm** below the costal margin with a **span of 11 cm**, **firm splenomegaly 8 cm (Hackett grade 3)**, dilated periumbilical veins, no ascites, BP normal, urine dipstick negative: **hepatosplenomegaly with signs of portal hypertension, most likely hepatosplenic schistosomiasis (*S. mansoni*)**; differentials: hyperreactive malarial splenomegaly, chronic liver disease, haematological malignancy."

**Model case presentation (ward round)**

> "Opio is a **6-year-old boy from Lira**, weight **17 kg (WAZ −1.6)**, height 112 cm (HAZ −0.8), **fully immunised** on UNEPI, **HIV-negative** (tested this admission), eating family foods (posho, beans, greens; little animal protein), with a **3-week history of a rapidly enlarging abdomen**, reduced appetite, night sweats and weight loss. No jaw swelling, no haematuria.
> On examination he is **pale**, not jaundiced, with no lymphadenopathy. The abdomen is **distended and asymmetrical**. There is a **14 × 10 cm hard, irregular, non-tender mass in the right iliac fossa and periumbilical region**, which **does not move with respiration**, is **dull** to percussion, and **crosses the midline**; I can get above it. The liver and spleen are not separately palpable; kidneys not ballotable. **Shifting dullness present** (moderate ascites). Bowel sounds normal. **Both testes descended**, no hernias. **BP 104/66** (normal for age). **Urine dipstick: no blood or protein.**
> **Summary**: a rapidly growing intra-abdominal mass with ascites in a 6-year-old in northern Uganda: **abdominal Burkitt lymphoma** is most likely; differentials include neuroblastoma, abdominal TB and Wilms tumour (less likely: crosses the midline, no haematuria). **Plan**: ultrasound, FBC, LDH, uric acid, creatinine and electrolytes, ascitic fluid cytology or FNA of the mass, **IV hydration and allopurinol** (tumour lysis prevention), and urgent referral to the Uganda Cancer Institute or Lacor paediatric oncology."

**After the examination**

1. Present findings with **relevant positives and negatives**.
2. Request the **right next test** (ultrasound, FBC, urinalysis, specific tests) and the **right referral** (surgery, oncology, cardiology, nephrology).
3. **Explain** to the parent and child, and agree a plan and safety-netting (for example hernia incarceration signs, daily spleen palpation in SCD).
4. Document clearly, including the **chaperone's name**.

## 11. Key Points

### High-yield points

- **Watch the face, warm the hands, start far away** from the pain.
- General examination and growth first; finish with **BP, urine dipstick and growth plotted**.
- A liver edge up to **1–2 cm** is normal in infants; **measure the span**.
- Start liver and spleen palpation in the **right iliac fossa**.
- Spleen: notch, moves toward the RIF, cannot get above, dull. Kidney: ballotable, can get above, resonant.
- **Every boy with abdominal pain gets his testes examined**; torsion = theatre within 4–6 hours, no ultrasound first.
- Hernia: cannot get above. Hydrocele: can get above and transilluminates.
- **Never** forcibly retract a foreskin; **never** circumcise a boy with hypospadias.
- Prepubertal girls: **inspection only**, with a chaperone.
- Suspected Wilms or neuroblastoma: **one gentle examination**.

| Precocious / delayed | < **8** / > **13** (girls); < **9** / > **14** (boys) |
| Paracetamol | **15 mg/kg** 6-hourly (max 60 mg/kg/day; max 1 g/dose) |
| Praziquantel (schistosomiasis) | **40 mg/kg** single dose |
| Sexual abuse | HIV **PEP within 72 h**; emergency contraception within **120 h** |

### Exam traps

- **"Palpable liver edge in an infant = hepatomegaly."** Wrong: up to 1–2 cm is normal; measure the span.
- **"Start palpating at the costal margin."** Wrong: start in the **RIF** for liver and spleen, or you will miss a big organ.
- **"Retract the foreskin to clean it."** Wrong: never forcibly retract.
- **"Circumcise a boy with a hooded foreskin and ventral meatus."** Wrong: hypospadias; refer first.
- **"Order an ultrasound before surgery for suspected torsion."** Wrong: explore urgently.
- **"Scrotal swelling transilluminates, so it is a hernia."** Wrong: transillumination suggests hydrocele.
- **"Examine a prepubertal girl with a speculum for discharge."** Wrong: inspection only; refer.
- **"Abdominal pain, so the cause is in the abdomen."** Wrong: check the testes, the chest (lower-lobe pneumonia), glucose (DKA) and the throat; and in SCD think vaso-occlusive crisis or sequestration.

### Questions seniors ask

**Viva questions**

1. *How do you distinguish spleen from left kidney?* Spleen: notch, moves toward the RIF with inspiration, cannot get above it, dull to percussion, not ballotable. Kidney: ballotable, can get above it, resonant band over it, moves little.
2. *How do you tell a hernia from a hydrocele?* Hernia: cannot get above it, cough or cry impulse, reducible, usually not transilluminant. Hydrocele: can get above it, transilluminates, no impulse, testis may be within it.
3. *What is a retractile testis?* A descended testis pulled up by an active cremasteric reflex that can be brought down to the scrotum and stays; normal; review yearly.
4. *When should an undescended testis be operated on?* Refer if not down by 6 months; orchidopexy at 6–12 months, by 18 months at the latest (fertility and cancer risk).
5. *Why should you not circumcise a boy with hypospadias?* The foreskin is used for the reconstruction.
6. *Name causes of hepatosplenomegaly in Ugandan children.* Malaria (and hyperreactive malarial splenomegaly), schistosomiasis, SCD (early), HIV, TB, leukaemia or lymphoma, visceral leishmaniasis, heart failure (liver), typhoid.
7. *What is the Hackett grading?* A 0–5 scale of spleen size relative to the costal margin, umbilicus and pubic symphysis, used in malaria surveys.
8. *How do you assess for ascites?* Inspection (distension, flanks, everted umbilicus), shifting dullness, fluid thrill (tense ascites), girth; then look for the cause (urine protein, JVP and heart, liver, TB).

**On the ward round**

- **How would you examine the abdomen of a crying 18-month-old?** On the mother's lap, warm hands, distraction (toy, bubbles), palpate during inspiration between cries or while the child is feeding or asleep, palpate with the child's hand under mine, and watch the face for tenderness.
- **This boy has abdominal pain. What part of the examination must you never skip?** The groins and testes (torsion and incarcerated hernia), plus the chest (lower-lobe pneumonia), a urine dipstick and glucose.
- **How do you describe a mass?** Site, size, shape, surface, edge, consistency, tenderness, mobility (with respiration and side to side), whether you can get above or below it, whether it crosses the midline, percussion note, pulsatility, bruit, and transillumination; then BP and urine.
- **What features distinguish Wilms tumour from neuroblastoma?** Wilms: younger child (2–5 y), well, smooth flank mass not crossing the midline, ± haematuria and hypertension. Neuroblastoma: often under 2, unwell, irregular hard mass crossing the midline, calcification, raccoon eyes, bone pain.
- **A testis is not in the scrotum. How do you decide if it is retractile or undescended?** Examine warm and relaxed (cross-legged), milk from the inguinal canal downward; if it can be brought to the bottom of the scrotum and stays briefly, it is retractile; if not, or it springs back immediately, it is undescended; if not felt anywhere, it is impalpable (needs surgical or laparoscopic assessment).
- **What is your duty if you suspect sexual abuse during an examination?** Stop unnecessary examination, ensure the child's safety, inform the senior, document exactly, start time-sensitive care (PEP within 72 h, emergency contraception, STI prophylaxis), and involve child protection and police according to Ugandan procedures; the forensic examination is done once by a trained examiner.

### Mnemonics

- **"Watch the face, warm the hands, start far away."**
- **Distension "5 Fs + O"**: Fluid, Flatus, Faeces, Fat, Fetus, plus **O**rganomegaly or masses.
- **Spleen versus kidney "SNAP-DB"**: Spleen has a **N**otch, moves **A**cross toward the RIF, no **P**ossibility of getting above; **D**ull; kidney is **B**allotable.
- **Scrotal swelling "Above, Light, Separate"**: can you get **above** it? Does it **transilluminate**? Is the testis **separate**?
- **Torsion: "6 hours to save the testis."**
- **Foreskin "10–50–99"**: retractable in 10% at 1 year, 50% at 10 years, 99% at 17 years.
- **Wilms versus neuroblastoma**: **W**ilms is **W**ell with a **W**ithin-one-side mass; **N**euroblastoma is **N**ot well and **N**ot respecting the midline.
- **Puberty "8–9 too early, 13–14 too late"** (girls/boys).

### OSCE tips

- Start with **introduction, consent and assent, and a chaperone**.
- **Warm hands and kneel**.
- **Say what you are doing** while you do it ("I am starting away from where it hurts and watching his face").
- Do the **general examination and growth first**.
- Examine **liver and spleen from the right iliac fossa**.
- **Examine the groins and genitalia** (or state that you would, with a chaperone, if the station uses a manikin); **do not retract the foreskin**.
- Finish with **"To complete my examination I would check the blood pressure, dip the urine and plot his growth."**
